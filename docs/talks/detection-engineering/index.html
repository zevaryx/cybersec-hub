// WHY THIS TALK

The map doesn't tell you what you can detect

227 + 479techniques and sub-techniques in Enterprise ATT&CK v20.1. A map of behaviours, not a checklist.
706detection strategies in that data, with 1,773 analytics: starting points, each with settings you must tune.
3,000+rules in SigmaHQ's public repository, in one portable format.

A rule that was never tested is a hope, not a control.

  • Rules are code: they have inputs, bugs, owners and a lifecycle.
  • Indicators expire. Behaviours last, if you test them against more than one sample.
  • Two teams can both be "covered" on a heatmap and have very different detection.

"Two organizations can show the same technique as green on an ATT&CK heatmap"MITRE Center for Threat-Informed Defense, "Beyond the Heatmap", 10 Sep 2026

"Threat actors can easily modify known IOCs, such as filenames and command line arguments, or modify content to change the hash."CISA, NSA, FBI and partners, living off the land guidance, 7 Feb 2024

8 Dec 2020: detections shared as codeAfter its red-team tools were stolen, FireEye published "hundreds of countermeasures" in formats such as Yara and Snort. The repository labels each one Production (little tuning needed) or Supplemental (needs tuning for your environment, often used for hunting): maturity written next to the rule.

Sources: ATT&CK versions · ATT&CK STIX data (counted 9 Nov 2026) · SigmaHQ README · CTID · CISA LOTL guidance · FireEye post, repo

~3 min

  • SayATT&CK is a catalogue of what attackers do, and since 2025 it ships starting points for detecting each technique. That is useful, and it is also the reason for this talk: a starting point isn't a detection until it's been adapted to your data, tested, owned and measured.
  • SayThe two quotes are the thesis. The first is about measuring: a green square says very little. The second is about durability: the things that are easiest to match (a hash, a file name, a command-line detail) are the easiest for an attacker to change. We'll spend the day on what to do instead.

// FROM THREAT TO DETECTION 01

The detection lifecycle: seven steps, each with an output

IN A SIGMA RULE the lifecycle shows up as fields: status (stable, test, experimental, deprecated, unsupported), date and modified, falsepositives, level, and tags for the ATT&CK technique.

Sources: Sigma rules specification 2.1.0 (status, level, tags) · Palantir ADS framework

~3 min

  • ShowClick through the seven steps. For each: what you make, what typically goes wrong, and the question to ask. Most teams do steps 1, 3 and 5 and skip the others.
  • SayThe lifecycle isn't a waterfall: tune feeds back into rule, and retire feeds back into idea. The reason to write it as steps is that each one has an output you can review: a hypothesis, a data list, a file, a test run, a rollout plan, a filter with a reason, a retirement note.
  • AskWhich of the seven does your team do least? Answer: usually test and retire. Both are about evidence, and neither has a natural owner unless you give it one.
  • CheckThe Sigma status values and meanings are from the rules specification 2.1.0 (2 Aug 2025): stable (may be used in production), test (mostly stable, may need slight adjustments), experimental (could be noisy), deprecated (replaced or covered by another rule), unsupported (can't be used in its current state). The spec lists falsepositives and level as optional; write them anyway. The seven-step lifecycle is this talk's own framing, in line with the Palantir ADS idea of validating before production.

// FROM THREAT TO DETECTION 02

The Pyramid of Pain: what you take away from the attacker

cheap for them to changecostly

Use the lower rungs as cheap enrichment: search last month's logs, add to a blocklist. Build the detections higher up.

Sources: David Bianco, The Pyramid of Pain (1 Mar 2013) · Summiting the Pyramid, v4.0.0 levels (STP column: my loose alignment)

~5 min

  • ShowClick each rung from the bottom. The question is always the same: if I block or detect this, what does the attacker have to do? A hash: rebuild. An address: rent another. A domain: register another. Then it gets more expensive, until the top, where they have to change how they work.
  • SayBianco's point isn't that indicators are bad. His post says they're valuable if you can respond to them fast enough, but "not all indicators are created equal". The Pyramid is a way to ask what each detection costs the attacker when you take it away.
  • AskWhich rung do most of your current detections sit on? Answer: usually the bottom three, because they're easy to get from a feed and easy to write. The top is harder: it needs telemetry you may not have and rules you have to test.
  • CheckQuotes are from Bianco's post (1 Mar 2013, updated 17 Jan 2014): hash values ("It is so easy for hash values to change"), IP addresses ("they can usually recover without even breaking stride"), domain names ("slightly harder to change than just IP addresses"), tools ("they have to invest time in research"), TTPs defined as "how the adversary goes about accomplishing their mission". The "pain" rating on the left is the post's own colour and wording, not a measurement; the original diagram's difficulty labels aren't in the post text I read, so they aren't used. The Summiting the Pyramid (STP) levels are the exact names from its v4.0.0 documentation: Level 1 "Ephemeral Values", Level 2 "Core to Adversary-Brought Tools", Level 3 "Core to Pre-Existing Tools", Level 4 "Core to Some Implementations of (Sub-)Technique", Level 5 "Core to Sub-Technique or Technique". The mapping between Bianco's rungs and STP levels is my loose alignment: they measure different things (kinds of indicator versus how an analytic observes a technique).

// FROM THREAT TO DETECTION 04

Name the behaviour, find the technique

  • A technique tag is how a rule finds a place on the map, and how you find the next gap.
  • Pick the most specific one that fits: a sub-technique when the behaviour names one.
  • Check the ID against the current version. ATT&CK v19 (April 2026) replaced Defense Evasion with Stealth and Defense Impairment and revoked T1562 (Impair Defenses) in favour of T1685 (Disable or Modify Tools).
  • A behaviour can map to several techniques. SigmaHQ's certutil download rule is tagged attack.t1105 (Ingress Tool Transfer).
Choose a technique for each line, then check.

Sources: T1105 · T1059.001 · T1053.005 · T1490 · ATT&CK v19 notes · SigmaHQ certutil rule

~4 min

  • ShowPick a technique for each line and check. The distractors are real techniques that are easy to confuse: a Run key and a service are other ways to persist, and LSASS memory is credential dumping.
  • SayThe mapping step looks like paperwork and isn't. It's how you find out later that you have five rules for PowerShell and none for persistence. It also forces a specific question: what exactly is the behaviour?
  • CheckNames and IDs checked against Enterprise ATT&CK v20.1 data on 9 Nov 2026: T1566.001 Spearphishing Attachment, T1059.001 PowerShell, T1105 Ingress Tool Transfer, T1053.005 Scheduled Task, T1490 Inhibit System Recovery, T1486 Data Encrypted for Impact, T1547.001 Registry Run Keys / Startup Folder, T1543.003 Windows Service, T1003.001 LSASS Memory. // CHECKPOINT 1 · 5 MIN

Pause and discuss

Talk with a neighbour for a couple of minutes, then we share answers.

  1. Why is detecting a behaviour more durable than detecting a file hash or an IP address?

    Hint

    What does the attacker have to do to get around each one?

    Answer

    A hash changes with a rebuild and an address with a rented server: both are cheap. A behaviour, such as Office starting PowerShell with a hidden window and an encoded command, is how they get the job done, so avoiding your detection means learning and rehearsing a different way to do it.

  2. A report lists a hash, a server address and a technique. What do you do with each?

    Hint

    Which of them can you maintain for a year?

    Answer

    The hash and the address go into a search over past logs and your blocklists, as cheap, short-lived enrichment. The technique becomes a behaviour detection with tests, an owner and a review date.

  3. Your logs have no parent process and no command line. Which parts of the report can you still detect?

    Hint

    What does each statement need to be seen?

    Answer

    Mostly the indicators: addresses in network logs, domains in DNS logs. The behaviours need process telemetry with the command line. A detection you can't run isn't a detection: the missing telemetry goes on the backlog, and the gap goes on the coverage map as "no data".

5 min

  • ShowPairs talk for 2 minutes, then share. The timer starts by itself. Question 1 is the one to get right.

    Test-first: unit tests for a detection
    
  
    
    
    
      
    
      
      
    
    
    
    
    
      
    
      
    
      
    
    
    
  
    
  
    ~7 min
    • ShowStart with the narrow rule: it copies one sample and fails four of the five positive cases. Switch to the broad rule, which mentions only "urlcache": it passes four positives, wrongly fires on two negatives, and also misses the verifyctl form. So a rule can be too broad and too narrow at once. Then the good rule: all nine pass.     • SayTests make rules changeable. Without them, every tweak is a gamble, and tuning a false positive can quietly break a true positive. The negative cases matter as much as the positive ones: they are your list of benign look-alikes. The variants are cheap robustness tests: they are the ways a command can be written differently without changing what it does.
    • AskWhat other variants would you add for a PowerShell rule? Answer: abbreviated parameters (the shell accepts short forms), a different case, a copied binary, a different parent, a script block instead of a one-liner.
    • CheckThe test cases, programs and addresses are invented; the three rules are mine (the good one is the same as the playground's certutil rule). The variants are the kind SigmaHQ and the CISA guidance describe: the "windash" modifier exists because Windows tools accept "-" or "/" for flags; the joint guidance (7 Feb 2024) gives a documented example of a shortened argument ("ac i ntds" for "activate instance ntds") still working. SigmaHQ's own regression tests pair each rule with sample events and fail if the expected number of matches isn't produced (its regression_data README). PowerShell 7's documentation lists -EncodedCommand | -e | -ec, and SigmaHQ's encoded-command rule carries the comment that " -e" covers -en and -enc; I did not verify the short forms for Windows PowerShell 5.1.

    // TESTING AND COVERAGE 02
    Testing without attacking production
    
  
    
    
    
      
    What an atomic test is
    
      
    attack_technique: T1053.005
display_name: "Scheduled Task/Job: Scheduled Task"
atomic_tests:
- name: a short name
  auto_generated_guid: a UUID
  description: what it does, and what you should see
  supported_platforms:
  - windows
  executor:
    name: command_prompt
    elevation_required: true
    command: the small action that triggers the behaviour
    cleanup_command: how to undo it
    
      
      
        
    • Read every test before you run it. Authorise it. Run it on a dedicated test machine built like production, with your sensors on.
      • 
        
    • Run the cleanup. Then record the date and what you saw.
      • 
        
    • In CI, replay recorded or synthetic events. Save real execution for scheduled lab runs.
      • 
      
    
    
    
    
    
      
    A purple-team loop
    
      
    Plan→Run in the lab→Observe→Tune→Record
    
      
    Click each result to cycle it. How far did each test get?
    
      
    
      
    
      
    Sources: Atomic Red Team · Invoke-AtomicRedTeam wiki · an example test file
    
    
    
  
    
  
    ~5 min
    • SayA unit test proves the rule matches the event you wrote. An emulation test proves the whole path works: the action happened, the sensor recorded it, the pipeline parsed it, the rule fired and a person got the alert. They answer different questions and you need both.
    • ShowClick through the five results for each test: no telemetry, logged only, rule fired, alert reached an analyst, analyst knew what to do. The aim of a purple-team exercise isn't a pass: it's to find out which of the five each technique reaches, together with the red team, and to fix the weakest one. Most gaps are at "logged only" and "alert reached an analyst".
    • CheckAtomic Red Team describes itself as "a library of tests mapped to the MITRE ATT&CK framework" (MIT licence). The test structure shown matches a real file (atomics/T1053.005/T1053.005.yaml): attack_technique, display_name, then atomic_tests with name, auto_generated_guid, description, supported_platforms, executor (name, command, cleanup_command, elevation_required) and, in some, input_arguments. The Invoke-AtomicRedTeam wiki says "Executing atomic tests may leave your system in an undesirable state", that you are responsible for knowing what a test does before you run it, that you should confirm you're authorised, and recommends a dedicated test machine similar to your environment's build, with your collection or EDR in place. I did not read the wiki pages on its prerequisite and cleanup options, so the deck doesn't describe them. "Purple team" has no single standard definition; here it means red and blue working together on the same test. The five result levels and the sample results are my illustration, not a standard.

    • SayCoverage is a statement about intent: "we have something for this technique". It says nothing about whether the something works. CTID's new framing splits it in two: implementation coverage (how many distinct ways of doing the technique you can see) and detection quality (how robust and how precise the signal is). The practical version: record, per technique, the last test date and which variants it was tested against, and report that next to the heatmap.

    • CheckQuotes and terms from CTID's "Beyond the Heatmap" post (10 Sep 2026): implementation coverage ("how much of the behavior we can see"), detection quality made up of robustness ("how difficult a detection signal is for an adversary to evade or manipulate") and precision ("how well a signal distinguishes malicious behavior from benign activity"); the Detection Coverage Calculator can ingest Sigma-formatted YAML.     // CHECKPOINT 3 · 5 MIN
    Pause and discuss
    
  
    
    
    Talk with a neighbour for a couple of minutes, then we share answers.
    
    
      
      
    1. What does a green square on a coverage map actually prove?
      Hint
      Think of three things that could all be true of a square that was painted green.
      Answer
      Only that someone believes there's a rule for that technique. It doesn't show that the data exists, that the rule fires on real behaviour, which variants it catches, how often it fires on benign activity or whether anyone owns it. Record the last test date and the variants beside each square.
      2. 
      
    2. Your unit tests all pass and an emulation test still doesn't produce an alert. Where could the problem be?
      Hint
      Follow the event from the machine to the analyst.
      Answer
      Anywhere outside the rule logic: the sensor didn't record it, a field is named differently in your pipeline, the event didn't reach the SIEM, the rule isn't enabled for that data set, or the alert was suppressed or routed nowhere. That is why you need both kinds of test.
      3. 
      
    3. You're about to run adversary-emulation tests. What do you check first?
      Hint
      Three things before anything runs.
      Answer
      That you have permission, that you've read what each test does, and that it will run on a dedicated test machine built like production, with your sensors on and a cleanup step ready. Not on a production system.

    // DETECTIONS AS CODE 01
    The pipeline: break a rule and watch CI say no
    
  
    
    
    
      
    
      
      
    
    
    
    
    
      
    
      
    
      
    Sources: SigmaHQ's workflows · its regression tests · test_rules.py
    
    
    
  
    
  
    ~6 min
    • ShowThe rule starts green on all six steps. Press the break buttons one at a time and read the log: a reused id, a deleted id, a missing falsepositives list, a typo in the condition (it suggests the right name), a misspelled modifier (it suggests the right one), a misspelled field (the compile passes, and the back-test says the rule can never match), a loosened rule (the tests and the alert budget both fail), a narrowed rule (the tests fail), a technique that doesn't exist, a date in the wrong format. Then edit the rule yourself.
    • SayA detection repository is a software project. Rules go in a branch and are reviewed. CI runs them: parse, check the metadata, compile, run the unit tests, back-test on a week of recorded logs for volume, and only then ask a person to review. The back-test is the one people skip, and it's the one that stops a 3 a.m. flood.
    • AskWhich of these checks does your repository run today? Answer: most have the first two. The tests and the back-test are where the value is.
    • CheckThe pipeline runs in your browser on made-up data: a week of 52,000 invented process-creation events of which 74 mention certutil, an alert budget of 2 a day (my choice), and a rule file in the style of SigmaHQ's. SigmaHQ's real pipeline, read from its repository on 9 Nov 2026: on every push and pull request its workflows run strict yamllint, a logsource test, tests/test_rules.py (duplicate detections, file names, title in first line, and others), sigma check --fail-on-error --fail-on-issues with pySigma and the SigmaHQ validators, a duplicate-id check, and a JSON-schema validation against the Sigma 2.1.0 rule schema; separately, regression tests (a rule's info.yml points to sample events, and CI "fails if the expected number of matches is not produced"; rules with status test or stable must have them) and "goodlog" tests that run the rules against benign Windows baseline logs and show findings except known false positives. Its workflow that promotes older experimental rules is commented out, so I don't claim it runs. The team rules the demo enforces (id, status, description, author, date, level, falsepositives and an ATT&CK tag) are this talk's policy: the Sigma spec makes only title, logsource and detection mandatory.

    Metrics that matter: precision, analyst time, silence
    
  
    
    
    
      
    
        
        
        
      
    
      
    RuleAlertsRealHoursTestedVerdict
    
    
    
    
    
      
    
      
      
        
    • Precision: of the alerts, how many were real? The CTID post defines it as how well a signal separates malicious from benign.
      • 
        
    • Analyst time: alerts times triage minutes. Noisy rules cost people, not servers.
      • 
        
    • Silence is a metric: a rule that never fires is working, or broken. Test it.
      • 
        
    • Track last tested and owner too. A rule with neither is a risk.
      • 
      
    
      
    Click a column heading to sort. Every figure here is invented.
    
      
    Sources: CTID, Beyond the Heatmap (precision, robustness)
    
    
    
  
    
  
    ~5 min
    • ShowTen invented rules. Move the sliders and watch the verdicts change. "Encoded PowerShell" (about 26 analyst hours a week) and "Rare parent-child pair" (about 35) each cost a person most of a working week for almost nothing real: tune or retire. "Old VPN appliance exploit signature" has been silent for a year: is it still working, or is the threat gone? "certutil download" is fine, but not tested for 210 days. "Logon from a new country" has no owner.
    • SayPick a few numbers that drive a decision, not a dashboard. Precision tells you which rules to tune. Analyst hours tell you which noise costs most. Last-tested and owner tell you which rules are quietly rotting. And a rule's value isn't how often it fires: a rule that fires once a year on a real intrusion can be the most valuable one you have.
    • AskWhat would you do with a rule that has 100% precision and has fired twice in a year? Answer: keep it, test it on a schedule, and make sure it still can fire. Low volume is not a defect.
    • CheckAll rule names and numbers are invented. The thresholds are a teaching default, not a standard. Precision and robustness are defined in the CTID post (10 Sep 2026) as quoted on the coverage slide.

    // DETECTIONS AS CODE 03
    Rules rot: drift, canaries and retirement
    
  
    
    
    
      
    
      
    
      
    RuleAttacks caughtFalse alarmsCanary
    
      
    
    
    
    
    
      
      
        
    • Drift is the data changing under a rule: a renamed field, a policy change, a sensor upgrade, a new software version.
      • 
        
    • A canary is a synthetic event, pushed through the real pipeline on a schedule, that must fire the rule. When the canary stops firing, the rule is broken.
      • 
        
    • Retire with evidence: mark it deprecated, link what replaced it with related, and keep the history.
      • 
        
    • Your tags drift too. ATT&CK v19 (April 2026) replaced Defense Evasion with Stealth and Defense Impairment, and revoked T1562. The Sigma tags appendix (2.1.0, August 2025) still lists defense-evasion, while SigmaHQ's own certutil rule is tagged attack.stealth.
      • 
      
    
      
    Sources: ATT&CK v19 notes · Sigma tags appendix · Sigma rules specification (status, related)
    
    
    
  
    
  
    ~4 min
    • ShowFour rules over the log. Baseline: all attacks caught, one false alarm from the backup agent on the shadow-copy rule. Then: an agent upgrade renames the parent-process field, and the Office rule catches nothing. A policy switches command-line logging off: three rules go silent, and the Office rule gets noisier because its finance filter reads the command line. The sensor stops sending the original file name: renamed copies of the same tools slip past. In each case the canary row shows what a scheduled test would have told you. Nothing logged an error.
    • SayRules rot because the data around them moves. The defence is the same as for any software: tests that run on a schedule, against the real pipeline. And retirement is a decision with evidence, not a deletion: deprecate it, say what replaced it and why.
    • AskWhat would a canary for your best rule look like, and who would get the alert when it fails? Answer: a synthetic event written to the same place real events are, once a day; and an owner named in the rule.
    • CheckATT&CK v19 (28 Apr 2026) release notes: "the Defense Evasion Tactic in Enterprise ATT&CK" was split "into the Stealth and Defense Impairment Tactics"; Impair Defenses (T1562) is listed as revoked by Disable or Modify Tools (T1685); T1070 was not renumbered. The Sigma tags appendix (2.1.0, released 2 Aug 2025) lists defense-evasion among its attack tactic tags and no stealth or defense-impairment tag; SigmaHQ's certutil rule (modified Dec 2025) uses attack.stealth. The spec's deprecated status means "the rule is replaced or covered by another one. The link is established by the related field". The demo's drift events are changes I made to the made-up log; no real agent is claimed to rename these fields.
