// WHY IDENTITY

Attackers don't break in, they log in

Firewalls don't stop someone with a valid password, token or session. To your systems, they are the user.

  • Stolen passwords, phished codes, leaked tokens and forgotten accounts are the usual ways in.
  • Valid logins don't trip most alarms. They look like work.
  • So identity is where to put your effort: prove who it is, and limit what they can do.
A never-expiring admin tokenZeabur, October 2026: long-lived admin tokens in a config repository let an attacker read millions of customer secrets, unnoticed for over 16 hours.
A key nobody revokedLiteLLM, October 2026: an admin key committed in May was used five months later to steal developers' GitHub tokens.

Both from Cyber in the News: Black October, with sources.

~2 min

  • SayNeither of these needed a clever exploit. They needed a credential that still worked. That's the theme of the whole talk.
  • Next"Start with the biggest lever: one identity for everything."

// ONE IDENTITY 01

Single sign-on: one door to lock

  • An identity provider (IdP) proves who you are once. Apps trust its answer.
  • One place to enforce MFA, see sign-ins, and switch someone off.
  • Fewer passwords for people to reuse, and nothing for each app to store.
  • The flip side: the IdP is now the crown jewels. Protect its admins hardest.

~4 min

  • ShowWithout SSO, press "Sam leaves": IT disables the accounts it remembers. Some stay open for months. Tick SSO and try again: one switch, every app closed.
  • SayThe forgotten app is always the one with an export button. Apps that can't use SSO need a written offboarding step.
  • Next"How does an app trust the IdP's answer? A signed token."

// ONE IDENTITY 02

Inside an ID token


    

    

      
    
        
  • Three parts: header, payload (the claims), signature, each Base64url-encoded.
    • 
        
  • Anyone can read it: it's encoded, not encrypted. Never put secrets in a token.
    • 
        
  • Only the IdP can sign it. Apps check the signature with the IdP's public key.
    • 
        
  • Then check the claims: issuer, audience, expiry, and the nonce you sent.
    • 
      

      
CRYPTO 101 Encoding on the outside, signatures underneath. Real ES256 (P-256 ECDSA), made in your browser.

    

  

  
~5 min
  • ShowRead the payload aloud: it's just Base64. Press "role → admin": the payload changes, the signature no longer matches, the app rejects it. "alg → none" is a classic: old libraries accepted unsigned tokens. Modern ones refuse; make sure yours does.
  • SayThis is the format OpenID Connect uses for ID tokens (a JWT). SAML does the same job with signed XML.
  • EggSet alg to none.
  • Next"Two names people mix up: OAuth and OpenID Connect."






  
// ONE IDENTITY 03
OAuth vs OpenID Connect

  

    

      
OAuth 2.0: permission"This app may read my calendar."Gives an app an access token to call an API on your behalf.Like a valet key: it opens the car, not the boot.

      
OpenID Connect: login"This is Alice, she signed in at 09:14 with a passkey."Adds an ID token on top of OAuth, for the app to read.Like an ID card: says who, not what they may do.

    

    

      
Use the authorisation code flowwith PKCE, for web, mobile and single-page apps alike

      
Ask for the least scoperead-only, one calendar, not "full account access"

      
Never treat an access token as loginit proves a permission, not who's at the keyboard

      
Use a libraryyour framework's OIDC client checks the details for you

    

    
CONSENT PHISHING A malicious app can ask for real permissions on a real login page. Review which apps your users have granted access, and restrict who can approve new ones.

  

  
~4 min
  • SayOAuth answers "may this app do this?" OpenID Connect answers "who is this?" Mixing them up is how apps end up logging people in with a token that was only meant to read a calendar.
  • SayConsent phishing needs no password at all: the user really signs in, then grants a bad app access. That's why app consent belongs in your IdP's settings.
  • Next"Once you're in, you have a session. That's what gets stolen now."






  
// ONE IDENTITY 04
Sessions: the thing attackers steal now

  

    

      
MFA protects the moment you sign in. A stolen session cookie skips that moment entirely.

      
    
        
  • Malware on a laptop can copy browser cookies. The attacker arrives already signed in.
    • 
        
  • Short sessions limit the damage. NIST's guidance for most business systems (AAL2): sign in again at least every 24 hours, and after an hour of inactivity.
    • 
        
  • Sign-out must end the session on the server, not just delete the cookie.
    • 
        
  • Revoke sessions when a password changes, a device is lost, or someone leaves.
    • 
      

    

    

      
For developersCookies with Secure, HttpOnly and SameSite. Short-lived access tokens, refresh tokens that can be revoked, and a new session ID after login.

      
For adminsSession lifetimes set in the IdP, "sign out everywhere" one click away, and alerts for a session used from two countries at once.

      
SOURCE NIST SP 800-63B-4, reauthentication at AAL2 ("SHOULD" limits)

    

  

  
~4 min
  • SaySession theft is why "we have MFA" isn't the end of the story. Pair it with device protection and short sessions.
  • CheckNIST's numbers are recommendations ("SHOULD") for AAL2: no more than 24 hours overall and 1 hour of inactivity. Your industry may require shorter.
  • Next"Checkpoint."






  
// CHECKPOINT 1 · 5 MIN
Pause and discuss

  

    
Talk with a neighbour for a couple of minutes, then we share answers.

    
    
      
  1. Why does SSO make offboarding safer?
    Hint
    How many switches does IT have to find?
    Answer
    One account to disable instead of one per app, so nothing is forgotten. Apps outside SSO still need their own step.
    2. 
      
  2. Someone base64-decodes an ID token and changes their role to admin. Why doesn't it work?
    Hint
    What covers the payload?
    Answer
    The signature covers the header and payload. Change either and it no longer verifies with the IdP's public key, so the app rejects it (as long as it checks).
    3. 
      
  3. You have MFA everywhere. How can an attacker still get in as you?
    Hint
    What does your browser keep after you sign in?
    Answer
    By stealing a session cookie or token from a compromised device, or by getting a help desk to reset your MFA. Short sessions, device security and help-desk checks close those gaps.
    4. 
    

  

  
5 min
  • ShowPairs talk for 2 minutes, then share. The timer starts by itself.







  
// STRONG AUTHENTICATION 01
Not all MFA is equal

  

    

      

        
SMS or voice codesNIST calls the phone network a restricted channel: SIM swaps and relayed codes

        
Authenticator app codesBetter, but you type them, so a fake site can relay them

        
Push with number matchingStops blind approvals; a convincing fake site can still relay the session

        
Passkeys and security keysPhishing-resistant: the key only signs for the real site's address

      

    

    

      
    
        
  • NIST: anything you type in (passwords, codes) is not phishing-resistant.
    • 
        
  • Phishing-resistant means cryptographic and bound to the site's name, like WebAuthn passkeys and security keys.
    • 
        
  • Systems at NIST's middle level (AAL2) must offer a phishing-resistant option.
    • 
      

      
STILL TRUE Any MFA beats none. Move people up the ladder; don't wait for perfect.

      
The full attack-by-attack table: Passwords and Accounts.

      
SOURCE NIST SP 800-63B-4, sections 2.2, 3.1.3.3 and 3.2.5

    

  

  
~4 min
  • SayThe bottom rung still stops password reuse, which is most account takeovers. The top rung also stops the convincing fake login page, which is how targeted attacks work.
  • SayWhy passkeys resist phishing: the browser tells the authenticator which site is asking, and it's part of what gets signed. A look-alike domain gets nothing.
  • CheckNIST SP 800-63B revision 4; check your national agency's guidance too.

// STRONG AUTHENTICATION 02
Push fatigue: approve, approve, approve

  

    

      

        
        
      

      
Your phone is quiet. For now.

      

    

    

      
    
        
  • With a stolen password, an attacker can trigger sign-in prompts over and over, at 2am, until someone taps Approve to make it stop.
    • 
        
  • Number matching shows a number on the sign-in screen that you must type into the app. The attacker can't make you see it.
    • 
        
  • Also: limit how many prompts can be sent, show where the sign-in is from, and give people a "this wasn't me" button that alerts security.
    • 
      

      
TELL PEOPLE A prompt you didn't ask for means your password is known. Deny it, and report it.

    

  

  
~4 min
  • ShowSend prompts without number matching: they keep coming; each has Approve and Deny. Then tick number matching: the prompt asks for a number only the real sign-in screen shows.
  • EggApprove the 10th prompt.
  • Next"The real fix is passkeys. How do you roll them out?"






  
// STRONG AUTHENTICATION 03
Rolling out passkeys without a revolt

  

    

      
01Admins firstIdP admins, cloud admins and finance get security keys or passkeys first. They're the biggest prize.

      
02Enrol in person or on videoCheck identity properly when someone adds their first key. That moment is an attacker's best chance.

      
03Two of everythingEvery person registers two authenticators, or keeps recovery set up, so a lost phone isn't a crisis.

      
04Then switch off the weak onesOnce most people use passkeys, stop allowing SMS for those accounts.

    

    

      
Synced or device-bound?Synced passkeys live in a password manager or phone account and survive a lost device: great for most staff. NIST says synced keys suit AAL2 but not its top level, AAL3, which needs keys that can't be copied, like hardware security keys.

      
Shared and kiosk devicesShared tills and warehouse scanners can't hold everyone's passkey. Use security keys people carry, or the IdP's device-based sign-in, and short sessions.

    

  

  
~5 min
  • SayRollouts fail on recovery, not on technology. If losing a phone means a two-day lockout, people will demand SMS back.
  • SayWant to try one? Crypto 101's passkey slide creates a real passkey with your fingerprint, face or PIN.
  • Next"Some decisions can be automatic: conditional access."






  
// STRONG AUTHENTICATION 04
Conditional access: context decides

  

    

      
A sign-in arrives

      

        
        
        
        
      

      

    

    

      
    
        
  • The IdP looks at who, which device, where from and which app, then allows, asks for stronger proof, or blocks.
    • 
        
  • Start in report-only mode: see what a rule would do for a week before enforcing it.
    • 
        
  • Keep rules few and named in plain words. "Admins need a security key" beats forty overlapping exceptions.
    • 
      

    

  

  
~4 min
  • ShowA staff member on a company laptop reading email: allowed. Change to an admin on a personal phone opening the cloud console: blocked. Two countries in 20 minutes: impossible travel, blocked and flagged.
  • SayThese are example rules, not a standard. Every IdP names this differently: conditional access, access policies, context-aware access.
  • Next"Part 3: once someone's in, what can they do?"







  
// LEAST PRIVILEGE 01
Roles and groups, not one-off permissions

  

    

      
Permission by person"Give Priya access to the finance share, like Tom has."Copies whatever Tom collected over six yearsNobody can say why anyone has anything

      
Permission by rolePriya joins the group finance-analysts.The group has exactly what that job needsLeaving the job means leaving the group

    

    

      
    
        
  • Name groups after jobs, not people or projects: support-agents, not daves-team.
    • 
        
  • Every group has an owner who approves who joins and checks it later.
    • 
        
  • Never "copy access from a colleague". It copies their history, not their job.
    • 
        
  • Write down what each role is for, in one sentence. You'll thank yourself during the review.
    • 
      

    

  

  
~3 min
  • SayThis is mostly organisation, not technology: clear job-shaped groups with owners make every later step (reviews, offboarding, audits) easy.
  • Next"The most powerful roles deserve special treatment."






  
// LEAST PRIVILEGE 02
Admin access: just in time, and a glass to break

  

    

      
Separate admin accountsRead email and browse as yourself; do admin work as alice-admin, with a security key. Phishing your everyday account gets nothing.

      
Just-in-time adminNobody is admin all the time. Request it for an hour, with a reason; it switches off by itself, and it's logged.

      
Few ownersCount the people who can change the IdP or the cloud account. If you need more than two hands, it's too many.

    

    

      
Break-glass accountsTwo emergency admin accounts for when the IdP itself is broken or locked out. Long random passwords and security keys, stored offline, excluded from rules that could lock them out, never used day to day, and an alert every time one signs in.

      
TEST IT Use the break-glass process once a year, on purpose. An emergency is the worst time to find out the key is in a drawer nobody can open.

    

  

  
~4 min
  • SayStanding admin rights are an attacker's favourite find. Just-in-time access means a stolen account is usually just a normal account.
  • SayBreak-glass is the exception that makes the rules safe: you can enforce strict policies because there's a tested way back in.
  • Next"Now the slow problem: people change jobs."






  
// LEAST PRIVILEGE 03
Joiners, movers, leavers

  

    

      

        
        
        
      

      

    

    

      
    
        
  • Joiners: access comes from the role in HR's system, ready on day one.
    • 
        
  • Movers: the old role's access goes when the new one arrives. This is the step everyone forgets.
    • 
        
  • Leavers: disabled the moment HR marks them gone, then sessions revoked and devices wiped.
    • 
        
  • Automate from one source of truth, usually the HR system. Manual tickets drift.
    • 
      

    

  

  
~5 min
  • ShowWithout reviews, run a few half-years: Dana moves from support to sales to finance and keeps everything. Start over, tick reviews, and run again: access follows the job.
  • SayMovers are the quiet risk: nobody leaves, so nothing is switched off, and one account slowly becomes a skeleton key.
  • EggRun it for three years without reviews.
  • Next"Reviews only work if people actually do them."






  
// LEAST PRIVILEGE 04
Access reviews people actually finish

  

    

    

      
The review nobody finishesA spreadsheet of 4,000 rows, sent to one managerPermission names like GRP_FIN_RW_07"Approve all" by Friday, or else

    

    

      
The review that worksSmall: each group's owner reviews their own groupReadable: role names and what they're forFocused: highlight what changed and what's unused for 90 daysDefault to removing: unanswered means revoke

    

    

    
PLAIN WORDS If a reviewer can't tell what a permission does, they'll approve it. Clear names are a security control.

  

  
~3 min
  • SayMost access reviews are rubber stamps because they're designed to be impossible. Small, readable and focused reviews actually remove things.
  • Next"Not every identity is a person."






  
// LEAST PRIVILEGE 05
Non-human identities: the forgotten majority

  

    

      
Service accounts, API keys, CI tokens and bots often outnumber people, and nobody offboards them.

      
    
        
  • Inventory: every key has a name, an owner, a purpose and an expiry date.
    • 
        
  • Short-lived over long-lived: CI pipelines can swap a signed identity token for a temporary cloud credential (workload identity federation), so there's no stored key at all.
    • 
        
  • Least privilege: one key, one job, read-only where possible.
    • 
        
  • When the owner leaves, their keys get a new owner or get revoked.
    • 
      

    

    

      
Never expiring, never noticedZeabur's admin tokens were created in January 2024 and never expired. LiteLLM's key sat in a public repository for five months. Both still worked.

      
More in Git and GitHub Security (tokens) and Security Habits (least privilege).

    

  

  
~4 min
  • SayNon-human identities can't do MFA, don't leave, and don't complain when they have too much access. That's why they need a process even more than people do.
  • Next"One more way in that has nothing to do with technology."






  
// LEAST PRIVILEGE 06
The help desk is part of your login page

  

    

      

      

      

    

    

      
    
        
  • Whoever can reset a password or MFA can hand over an account. Attackers call the help desk, sounding stressed and senior.
    • 
        
  • Verify, then act: call back on the number in the directory, or check with the person's manager. Never use details the caller offers.
    • 
        
  • Make the secure way the easy way: self-service recovery with a second passkey means fewer calls, and fewer chances to be fooled.
    • 
        
  • Give the help desk a written script, and the authority to say "I'll call you back".
    • 
      

    

  

  
~3 min
  • ShowPlay the call: pick what the help desk does at each step. The safe path is always slower for one minute, and saves a breach.
  • SayThis is the general lesson of the talk: identity security is mostly process and kindness, done consistently.
  • EggChoose "Reset it: they said please".
  • Next"Checkpoint."






  
// CHECKPOINT 2 · 5 MIN
Pause and discuss

  

    
Talk with a neighbour for a couple of minutes, then we share answers.

    
    
      
  1. Which MFA methods survive a convincing phishing site, and why?
    Hint
    Which ones do you type in?
    Answer
    Passkeys and security keys. They sign for the real site's address, so a look-alike gets nothing. Anything you type, including app codes, can be relayed.
    2. 
      
  2. Dana has moved teams three times. What's the risk, and what fixes it?
    Hint
    What happens to the old access?
    Answer
    Access piles up until one account can reach everything. Moves should remove the old role, and regular reviews catch what slips through.
    3. 
      
  3. Name one non-human identity in your organisation without a clear owner.
    Hint
    CI tokens, old API keys, shared service accounts.
    Answer
    Most organisations find several. Give each an owner and an expiry date, or revoke it.
