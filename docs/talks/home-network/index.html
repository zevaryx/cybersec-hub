// WHY THIS TALK

One weak gadget can open the door for others

Your router, your TV and your doorbell camera share a network with your laptop and your phone. A weak one can become a way to the others, or a tool in someone else's attack.

  • Most trouble here is ordinary: a factory password nobody changed, a router that stopped getting updates, a gadget that's always online.
  • The fixes are settings you change once. An evening is enough.
Factory passwords, 2016Mirai, a botnet of home routers, cameras and recorders, got in with 62 common factory logins. It powered huge attacks, including one on the DNS provider Dyn on 21 October 2016.
A router past its support date, 2025The FBI warned that old routers without updates were being taken over to hide criminals' traffic, including ones with remote administration on.
A camera's account, 2023The FTC said hackers took over about 55,000 US customers' Ring accounts with stolen or guessed passwords. Ring paid $5.8 million to settle and disagreed with the allegations.

Sources: CISA: Mirai · FBI PSA, May 2025 · FTC: Ring, May 2023

~3 min

  • SayThree short stories, one per habit in this talk: a password nobody changed, a router nobody updated, and a camera account protected only by a password. None needed a genius.
  • TipCISA's alert (14 October 2016, revised 2017) says Mirai targeted "primarily home routers, network-enabled cameras, and digital video recorders" with "a short list of 62 common default usernames and passwords". The Dyn attack was on Friday 21 October 2016; Dyn said a Mirai botnet was the primary source of the attack traffic (from its own 26 October analysis, which I could only read second-hand because its blog now redirects). The FBI's PSA is I-050725-PSA, 7 May 2025: "Routers dated 2010 or earlier likely no longer receive software updates issued by the manufacturer"; a new variant of the TheMoon malware had been found on end-of-life routers with remote administration on. The FTC's Ring release is dated 31 May 2023: it said Ring faced credential-stuffing attacks in 2017 and 2018 and added multi-factor authentication only in 2019; the order needed court approval, and Ring said it disagreed with the allegations. "About 55,000" is the FTC's figure for US customers.

// THE ROUTER 01

The router is the front door

One home, one way out
The internetModemRouterWi-Fi · firewall · settingsYour devices
  • Everything in your home reaches the internet through it, so it sees and carries all of it.
  • It runs the Wi-Fi, hands out addresses, and has a built-in firewall that should be on.
  • Whoever can sign in to its settings can change how everything in your home connects.

WHOSE IS IT? A router from your internet provider may update itself: ask. One you bought is yours to update.

Two different passwords

Wi-Fi passwordLets devices join. You share it with family, and change it if it leaks.
Router admin passwordLets someone change the settings. Only you should have it, and it must differ from the Wi-Fi one.

Most routers print the factory admin login and the settings address on a label. That's handy for you and for anyone who can pick the router up, which is why you change it.

Source: FTC: How to secure your home Wi-Fi network

~3 min

  • SayThe router is the one device everything depends on, and the one most people set up once and never open again. It also has two passwords that people mix up: the Wi-Fi one that guests know, and an admin one that should be yours alone.
  • AskWho has opened their router's settings page in the last year? Most hands stay down. That's normal: tonight's list fixes it.
  • TipThe FTC's advice (December 2022): set two strong passwords, one for the Wi-Fi and one for the router's admin account; check the built-in firewall is on; log out of the admin account when you're done so no one can use an open session; and, if your internet provider supplied the router, ask whether it gets automatic updates. Many "router" boxes are really a modem and router in one; the advice is the same.

// THE ROUTER 02

Four settings that matter most

1 · Change the admin loginReplace the factory username and password. Avoid your name, address or router brand. Long and unique: let your password manager make it.
2 · Update the firmwareCheck the maker's site or app, and turn on automatic updates if offered. If your provider supplied the router, ask whether it updates itself.
3 · Turn off remote managementIt makes the router's settings page reachable from the internet. Unless you truly need it, off. The FBI found an old-router botnet on routers with it on.
4 · Turn off WPSThe 8-digit setup PIN can be guessed in about 11,000 tries, not 100 million, because the router confirms each half separately. Join devices with the Wi-Fi passphrase instead.

Sources: FTC · FBI PSA, May 2025 · CERT/CC: WPS PIN weakness

~4 min

  • SayIf you only do four things to your router, do these. Two are about the router itself (login and updates), two are about doors it ships with open (remote management and WPS).
  • TipWhy WPS: CERT/CC's note (December 2011) explains the PIN design leaks whether each half is right, so the search shrinks from 108 to 104 + 103 = 11,000 attempts; the fix it recommends is to turn WPS off. The push-button method is a different mode, but the FTC still says to switch WPS off. Remote management: the FBI (May 2025) tells people to turn it off and reboot, apply patches, replace routers at end of life where possible, and use long unique passwords (it suggests 16 to 64 characters). The FBI's PSA doesn't name models; it uses age (2010 or earlier) as an example.
  • ShowDon't dive into menus: each maker hides these in a different place. The next two slides and the "fix a router" demo practise the choices; at home, search "your router model + turn off WPS" or use the maker's support page.
  • Next"Then the Wi-Fi itself."

// THE ROUTER 03

Wi-Fi security: the mode and the passphrase

  • WPA3 if every device supports it, otherwise WPA2 with AES. Never an open network, WEP, or old WPA/TKIP.
  • Long and random beats clever. Six random words or 16 or more random characters. You type it once per device.
  • Hiding the network name and filtering by device address don't protect you. Apple's router guidance says to leave both off.
  • A neutral network name: no surname, address or router brand.

Sources: Wi-Fi Alliance: WPA3, Feb 2026 · Apple: recommended router settings · FTC · EFF word lists · Crypto 101 cracking estimator

~5 min

  • ShowStart on sunshine2020 under WPA2: a word and a year, flagged as popular. Click "5 random words": about 52 bits, roughly a year of non-stop guessing at the demo's generous rate, so the demo says to make it longer. "6 random words": over a thousand years. "16 random characters": far beyond that. Now flip to WPA3 and the third box changes: a recording is no use, every guess has to be a live attempt on your router. Last, try names in the network-name box.
  • SayTwo separate protections. The mode (WPA2 or WPA3) decides how the passphrase is used. On WPA2, someone in range who records a device joining can take the recording home and try passphrases against it as fast as their hardware allows. WPA3 changes the handshake so that isn't possible. Either way, a long random passphrase costs you nothing and ends the argument.
  • TipThe Wi-Fi Alliance (February 2026): WPA3 has been required in all new Wi-Fi CERTIFIED devices since 2020 and is mandatory in the 6 GHz band; WPA3-Personal's SAE is "resistant to offline dictionary attacks" and gives forward secrecy; and it strongly recommends WPA3 whenever possible. Transition mode lets WPA3 and WPA2 devices share a network, at the cost of the older mode's weakness for older devices. Apple recommends WPA3 Personal, or WPA2/WPA3 Transitional for older devices, and says to avoid WPA/WPA2 mixed modes, WEP, TKIP and open networks. WPA2-Personal turns the passphrase and network name into a key with PBKDF2 (4,096 rounds of HMAC-SHA1, per the 802.11i design; I confirmed it from secondary references); passphrases must be 8 to 63 characters (NETGEAR's documentation). Rate assumption: 100 million guesses a second is deliberately generous; community hashcat benchmarks show about 2.2 to 2.6 million a second for one top graphics card on WPA2, so 100 million is roughly forty of them. The word list is EFF's short list #1 (1,296 words, about 10.3 bits each, CC BY 3.0 US). Random characters come from 54 look-alike-free characters. Everything is made in this page with crypto.getRandomValues and is never sent or stored.
  • TipHidden SSID and MAC filtering: Apple says hiding the name "doesn't conceal the network from detection or secure it against unauthorized access" and that you "shouldn't rely on" MAC filtering because addresses can be copied or changed. Older guides, including a now-archived CISA tip (2021), suggested both, so someone may ask. The FTC (and CISA's archive) say a default name can reveal your router brand; "FBI Surveillance Van" is the classic joke name.

// THE ROUTER 04

UPnP and port forwarding: doors that open themselves

    • UPnP lets a device on your network ask the router to open a door to the internet, automatically, without asking you.
    • Convenient for games and video calls. But anything on your network, including a hacked gadget, can do the same, which is how malware can get around the router's firewall.
    • Turn it off. If something stops working, turn it on for a while, or open one specific door by hand.
    • Port forwarding is the same door, opened by you. Keep it to what you need, and don't forward cameras or the router's own settings page.

    Sources: FTC · CISA: home network security (archived) · CISA: Mirai

    ~3 min

    • ShowWith UPnP on, all four requests are granted and the "open doors" count shows four: one of them is a mystery device asking to open the telnet port. Switch it off: all refused. Then "Off, you decide": you choose which door, if any, to open.
    • SayA firewall's job is to stop connections from outside. UPnP lets things inside make exceptions on their own. That's fine when the thing inside is a game console you trust, and a problem when it's a camera with a flaw. If a game or app insists, open the one port it needs, by hand, for that one device.
    • TipThe FTC lists UPnP with remote management and WPS as features to disable. CISA's archived page (2021) says to "disable Universal Plug and Play (UPnP) when not needed" because malware on the network can use it to bypass the router's firewall, and its Mirai alert says the same for routers unless essential. The requests here are made-up illustrations; the port numbers are examples. Telnet (port 23) is the remote-login service Mirai-style malware looks for: CISA's alert tells defenders to watch ports 23 and 2323.

        // CHECKPOINT 1 · 5 MIN

        Pause and discuss

        Talk with a neighbour for a couple of minutes, then we share answers.

        1. Why is "remote administration" on a home router risky?

          Hint

          Who can reach the login page if it's reachable from the internet?

          Answer

          It puts the router's settings page on the internet, so anyone can try to sign in, or use a flaw in it. The FBI found an old-router botnet spreading on routers with it switched on. Turn it off unless you truly need it, and keep the router updated.

        2. Your Wi-Fi passphrase is "sunshine2020". What's wrong with it, and what would you change?

          Hint

          Would a guesser try a word plus a year early or late? And what does WPA3 change?

          Answer

          It's a word and a year, near the top of guess lists. On WPA2, someone in range who records a device joining can try guesses at home, offline. WPA3 stops that. So: WPA3 if you can, and a long random passphrase, such as six random words.

        3. Which one is real protection: hiding the Wi-Fi name, filtering by device address, or WPA3/WPA2 with a long passphrase?

          Hint

          Which one changes what a stranger needs in order to join and read your traffic?

          Answer

          The encryption with a long passphrase. Hiding the name doesn't conceal the network from detection, and device addresses can be copied or changed (Apple's router guidance says to leave both off). They add steps for you, not for a determined stranger.

        5 min

        • ShowPairs talk for 2 minutes, then share. The timer starts by itself.
        • TipIf someone asks "should I switch my router off at night?": it's a harmless habit, but not a security measure worth the trouble; updates and passwords do far more.

        // EVERYTHING ELSE 01

        Smart devices: the login, and the account behind it

        • Change the default login if it has one, and don't reuse a password from elsewhere. If a device gives you no way to set one, ask whether it needs to be online at all.
        • Cameras, doorbells and speakers have an account in the maker's cloud. Treat it like your bank: a unique password and two-step sign-in.
        • Switch off what you won't use: remote access, microphones, old devices you've stopped using.
        • Look at what it collects. Smart TVs have tracking settings; voice assistants can listen all the time and send recordings to the maker (the FTC's words).
        A TV that watched back, 2017The FTC said Vizio's smart TVs captured second-by-second information about what was on screen, on about 11 million TVs, without viewers' informed consent. Vizio paid $2.2 million to settle.
        A camera is a windowIn the Ring case the FTC said the damage came through customer accounts, not the camera: stolen or guessed passwords and too few sign-in protections. Two-step sign-in on the app is the lock that matters.
        Check who has accessRemove old shared users, and look at the access log for unfamiliar addresses or odd times, as the FTC suggests for IP cameras.

        Sources: FTC: securing your connected devices · FTC: Vizio, Feb 2017 · NCSC: smart devices at home

        ~4 min

        • SaySmart devices have two weak points: a login on the device, and an account in the cloud that shows or controls it. People protect the first and forget the second. Neither deserves more trust than your bank account.
        • AskHow many devices in your home are online that weren't ten years ago? Count TV, speaker, doorbell, bulbs, plugs, vacuum, printer, fridge.
        • TipThe FTC's device page (August 2022): change the default username and password, update regularly, disable features you won't use (including remote management), review a smart TV's tracking settings, and note that voice assistants "can also listen all the time and even send recordings to the manufacturer". The Vizio order was announced on 6 February 2017: a $2.2 million settlement with the FTC and New Jersey, with part suspended; the FTC alleged the viewing data was collected and shared "without viewers' informed consent". NCSC adds that the UK law banning default passwords on smart devices has applied since 29 April 2024 (next slides).

        // EVERYTHING ELSE 02

        A separate network for guests and smart devices

        • Guests get the guest network, with its own passphrase, so your main one stays private and a visitor's infected phone stays away from your files.
        • Smart gadgets go on a separate network too (the guest network, or a dedicated one if your router has it). If one is hacked, your laptop and phone are out of its reach.
        • The trade-off: casting, printing and some smart-home controls may need devices on the same network. Trade a little convenience for distance.

        "Can reach" means the router would let them talk, not that anything bad is happening. Routers differ: some let you allow specific connections.

        Sources: FTC · NSA home network guidance (summary)

        ~5 min

        • ShowStart on "One network", with the smart plug selected: it can reach every other device. Pick the laptop, the backup drive, the camera: same story. Switch to "Main, smart and guest": the colours split into three groups, and each device can only reach its own group and the internet. Pick the visitor's phone: nothing of yours is reachable.
        • SayThe point isn't that your TV is plotting something. It's that you can't update most gadgets reliably, so you limit the damage if one goes wrong. Think of it as putting the gadgets in a different room with a lock on the door.
        • TipThe FTC says a guest network keeps guest devices that might carry malware off your primary network and lets you keep your main password private. The NSA's February 2023 home-network sheet recommends wireless network segmentation (primary, guest and IoT networks); I could only read summaries of it, as its PDF blocks automated reading. Routers differ: look for "guest network", "IoT network", "client isolation" or "AP isolation". If yours has no guest network, a mesh system or a second router can add one. In the demo every device can reach its own network and the internet; real routers can be stricter or looser.

        // EVERYTHING ELSE 03

        Which network does each device belong on?

          • Main: the devices that hold your accounts and files, and that you keep updated.
          • Smart: gadgets you can't fully control, that rarely update, or that see and hear your home.
          • Guest: anyone else's device.
          • A few are a judgement call: a printer or games console works on either, as long as it isn't next to your files.

            ~4 min

            • ShowPut each device on a network, then check. The rule of thumb is: would I be upset if this were compromised, and can I keep it updated? Yes and yes means Main. No and no means Smart.
            • SayGuests are easy. Gadgets are the question. If the router only has one extra network, put guests and gadgets on it: it's still better than one big network.
            • TipThis is an illustration, not a rule from the sources: the logic is the FTC's and NSA's segmentation advice applied to everyday devices. Work laptops: follow your employer's advice, which may differ.

            // EVERYTHING ELSE 04

            Updates, and the "no more updates" problem

            • Turn on automatic updates: the router, each app, each device that offers it.
            • Before you buy, ask how many years of security updates it gets, and treat the end date as a "use by" date.
            • When updates stop, a device doesn't stop working. It stops being safe to leave exposed.

            An old device lost its updates. What now?

            Camera, mic or lock?
            Works offline?

            Answer both questions.

            Nest thermostats, October 2025Google ended support for first- and second-generation Nest Learning Thermostats on 25 October 2025: no more software or security updates, and no app. Schedules still run on the device.
            Wemo, January 2026Belkin ended support for most older Wemo products on 31 January 2026: no more updates, app or cloud features. Products set up with Apple HomeKit were due to keep working.
            Labels and lawsUK law since 29 April 2024 bans default passwords and requires a stated support period. EU reporting duties began 11 September 2026. The voluntary US Cyber Trust Mark wasn't in shops by late October 2026.

            Sources: Google: Nest end of support · TechCrunch: Wemo · NCSC · EU CRA · Nextgov: Cyber Trust Mark

            ~4 min

            • SayEverything stops getting updates eventually, usually long before it stops working. That's the real cost of a cheap smart gadget: the date it quietly becomes a risk. Ask for the date when you buy it.
            • ShowAnswer the two questions in the helper. A camera, microphone or lock with no updates is the one to replace first; something harmless that works offline can simply be taken off the internet.
            • TipGoogle's page: the thermostats "will no longer receive software or security updates", and "the pre-set schedules will continue to work". Wemo: announced 10 July 2025; per TechCrunch the devices lose firmware and software updates, the app and cloud features; HomeKit-set-up and Thread products keep working; I couldn't read Belkin's own page. NCSC: UK rules from 29 April 2024 ban default passwords and require stating the product support end date. EU CRA (Commission page): in force 10 December 2024, reporting from 11 September 2026, main obligations from 11 December 2027. The US Cyber Trust Mark is the FCC's voluntary label: UL Solutions withdrew as lead administrator in December 2025, ioXt Alliance was named in April 2026, and on 28 October 2026 an FCC commissioner said she hoped labelled products would reach shoppers "within the next year"; the label is meant to include the support commitment and says it can't guarantee safety. Check it hasn't moved. The FBI's May 2025 PSA says routers dated 2010 or earlier likely no longer get updates.

            // EVERYTHING ELSE 05

            What your provider can see, and private DNS

            WhatWho can see it

            • Encrypted DNS hides your lookups from the network between you and the resolver. Android's "Private DNS" is on automatic by default; routers and other devices may need setting.
            • It moves trust, it doesn't remove it. The resolver you choose still sees every lookup.
            • Some resolvers block known-bad sites: Quad9 (9.9.9.9) blocks known malware domains, Cloudflare 1.1.1.2 blocks malware and 1.1.1.3 adds adult content. A home filter like Pi-hole or AdGuard Home can cut ads and trackers for every device.
            • Blocking can break a page now and then. Keep a way to switch it off.

            Sources: RFC 9076: DNS privacy · RFC 9849: ECH · Android: Private DNS · Quad9 · Cloudflare · Pi-hole · AdGuard Home · How HTTPS Really Works

            ~3 min

            • ShowStart on "Provider's DNS": your provider carries all your traffic, and with plain DNS it sees each website name your devices ask for. Switch to encrypted DNS: only the resolver you chose sees the lookups. The pages you read are hidden from everyone on the path in both cases, because of HTTPS.
            • SayYour internet provider is the one company that carries every byte. It can't read an HTTPS page, but it can see who you talk to and when. Encrypted DNS removes one of the ways it learns site names. It doesn't make you invisible, and a VPN or Tor is a different trade, which is the Privacy tools talk.
            • TipRFC 9076 section 5.1: for unencrypted transports, "DNS traffic can be seen by an eavesdropper like any other traffic"; section 6.1.4.1: "Use of encrypted transports does not reduce the data available in the recursive resolver". The website name can also appear in the connection setup (the TLS "server name") unless the site and browser use Encrypted Client Hello, published as RFC 9849 in March 2026; many sites don't use it yet. What your provider does with data about you varies by country and company: ask what they log. Quad9's page lists 9.9.9.9 and 149.112.112.112 as "Malware Blocking, DNSSEC Validation"; Cloudflare's setup page lists 1.1.1.2 for malware blocking and 1.1.1.3 for malware and adult content. Android's help page says Private DNS is used on all networks that can by default.

            // CHECKPOINT 2 · 5 MIN

            Pause and discuss

            Talk with a neighbour for a couple of minutes, then we share answers.

            1. Your smart plug stops getting updates. What are your options?

              Hint

              What does it control? Can it work without the internet? Where is it on your network?

              Answer

              Keep it on the guest or smart network, and switch off its cloud features and remote access if it works without them. Replace it if it controls something that matters, such as a heater, a lock or a camera. Unplug it if you don't use it. Next time, check the update promise before buying.

            2. A visitor asks for the Wi-Fi password. Which network do they get, and why?

              Hint

              What can their phone reach on each network?

              Answer

              The guest network, with its own passphrase. Their phone, and anything on it, can reach the internet but not your laptop, backup drive or cameras, and you never share the main passphrase.

            3. You switch your router to encrypted DNS. Who can still see which sites your devices look up?

              Hint

              Who runs the new resolver? And what else gives a site's name away?

              Answer

              The resolver you chose sees every lookup, and your provider can often still see site names in the connection setup (unless the site uses Encrypted Client Hello) and which servers you talk to. What no one on the path can read is the content of HTTPS pages.

            5 min

            • ShowPairs talk for 2 minutes, then share. The timer starts by itself.
            • TipIf someone asks about a VPN: it moves the visibility from your provider to the VPN company, which is a trust decision, not a free pass. The Privacy tools talk covers it.

            // YOUR CHECKLIST 03

            Six habits to take home

            1 · A router you controlIts own admin login, updates on, remote management off.
            2 · Strong Wi-FiWPA3 or WPA2 with AES, a long random passphrase, a neutral name.
            3 · Doors closedWPS and UPnP off, and no camera or admin page forwarded to the internet.
            4 · Separate networksGuests and smart gadgets away from your laptop and phone.
            5 · Accounts tooUnique password and two-step sign-in on every camera and smart-home account.
            6 · A "use by" dateKnow when each gadget stops updating, then isolate or replace it.

            ~1 min

            • SaySix habits, and most of them are things you can finish before bedtime. If you only do one tonight, change the router's admin login and turn off remote management: they cost nothing and close the doors attackers use most.
