// WHY THIS TALK

To prove you know something, you usually hand it over

A password is a proof by disclosure: you show the secret and the other side keeps it. An ID shows your birthday to prove you're over 18. Zero-knowledge proofs ask: can the proof carry only the fact, and none of the secret?

1985: a new kind of proofGoldwasser, Micali and Rackoff defined interactive proofs and "knowledge complexity", and showed that a proof can leave the verifier with nothing except the fact that the statement is true.
1989: a cave, and a smart cardQuisquater, Guillou and Berson explained zero knowledge with an Ali Baba cave, in a paper called "How to explain zero-knowledge protocols to your children". The same year, Schnorr published an efficient identification scheme built for smart cards.
Today: proving without handing overAn age check that reveals "over 18" and nothing else, anonymous votes, payments whose amounts stay hidden, and proofs that a computer ran a program correctly.

Sources: Goldwasser, Micali, Rackoff (STOC 1985) · Quisquater et al. (1989), annotated copy · Schnorr (CRYPTO 1989)

~3 min

  • SayThree dates and one idea. Ordinary security hands the secret over: your password goes to a server, and you trust it to behave. In 1985 three researchers showed you can prove a statement and reveal nothing but its truth. In 1989 two things happened: a story clear enough for children, and an efficient protocol small enough for a smart card. Today the same idea sits behind some age checks, some payments and some blockchains.
  • AskWhere have you handed over far more than you needed to prove? (Showing a driving licence to prove you're over 18 is the usual answer: it also shows your name, address and exact birthday.)
  • CheckThe 1989 date for the cave paper is the CRYPTO '89 conference; the Springer chapter is dated 1990. The Schnorr paper is "Efficient Identification and Signatures for Smart Cards", CRYPTO '89 (LNCS 435). The journal paper that followed has a different title. The first slide-3 link goes to the DBLP record of the 1985 extended abstract; the full journal version is SIAM J. Computing, 1989. The cave paper is in LNCS 435 (CRYPTO '89), starting at page 628 (a 2002 mailing-list post links the Springer record). // INTUITION 01

The cave: prove you know the magic word without saying it

Prover


      

    

    

      
    
        
  • The prover walks in and takes tunnel A or B while the verifier waits at the entrance, out of sight. Deep inside, a door joins the two tunnels. It opens only with a magic word.
    • 
        
  • The verifier shouts which tunnel the prover must come out of. Someone who knows the word can always do it, crossing the door if they have to. A bluffer can only do it when they happened to pick the called side: half the time.
    • 
        
  • One round proves little. Many rounds prove a lot. Ten clean rounds in a row would fool a bluffer about 1 time in 1,024.
    • 
        
  • The verifier never hears the word, and learns nothing else about it.
    • 
      

      
A RECORDING PROVES NOTHING The verifier could agree the calls with a friend beforehand and film a fake cave. So a video convinces nobody else. That's what zero-knowledge means; slide 11 makes it exact.

      
Source: Quisquater et al., "How to explain zero-knowledge protocols to your children" (annotated copy)

    

  

  
~6 min
  • SayTell the story first: a ring-shaped cave, a door at the back that only opens with a magic word. The prover goes in by one tunnel, the verifier shouts which one to come out of. (Cryptographers traditionally call them Peggy and Victor, prover and verifier. The original paper tells it as a chase: a thief escapes into a cave whose passage forks left and right, and the verifier-and-prover version is the usual retelling. The excerpt I could read stops before the door and the magic word, which are as the story is usually told.)
  • ShowLeave "Knows the word" selected, press "Call out: A" a few times, then "Run 10 rounds": every round succeeds, because crossing the door is no problem. The log shows which tunnel the prover had actually entered; the verifier never sees that. Now switch to "Bluffing" and run 10 rounds: it fails about half the time, and a failure resets the confidence bar.
  • AskI've seen the prover come out of A five times in a row. Am I sure? (No: a bluffer does that once in 32 tries. Keep going, and the odds shrink with every round.)
  • SayThe key point: the verifier learns the prover knows the word, and learns nothing about the word itself. And a film of this convinces no one else, because the verifier could have staged it. That second point is surprising and is exactly what "zero-knowledge" means.

// INTUITION 02
Where's Waldo, with a big piece of cardboard

  

    

      
Prover

      

        
The prover's picture (secret)

        
What the verifier sees

      

      

        
        
      

      

      

    

    

      
    
        
  • The setup: the prover has a huge picture with one striped figure somewhere in it. They lay it behind a sheet of card, much bigger than the picture, with a single figure-sized hole in it. Out of the verifier's sight they slide the picture around until the figure shows through the hole.
    •

    •

    •

// INTUITION 03
Three properties: complete, sound, zero-knowledge

  

    

      
CompletenessIf the statement is true and both sides follow the rules, the verifier accepts. Cave: someone who knows the word always comes out of the right side.

      
SoundnessIf the statement is false, no cheater can make the verifier accept, except with a tiny probability. Cave: a bluffer is caught half the time, every round.

      
Zero-knowledgeThe verifier learns nothing except that the statement is true. Cave: the word stays secret, and a recording convinces nobody else.

    

    

      
WHICH PROPERTY IS BROKEN?

      

      

      

    

    
Sources: Goldwasser, Micali, Rackoff (1985) · Boneh and Shoup, A Graduate Course in Applied Cryptography, chapter 19

  

  
~5 min
  • SayThree words to carry out of the room. Completeness: honest provers get through. Soundness: cheaters don't (almost always). Zero-knowledge: the verifier gets nothing but "yes". Each one can fail on its own, and a proof that has two of the three is a different, usually less useful, thing.
  • ShowThe four scenarios below. Let the room vote before pressing. Scenario 4 is a trick: nothing is broken.
  • SayOne precision. Some explainers say a sound proof is "impossible to fool". The accurate statement is probabilistic: a cheater succeeds with probability at most some tiny number, and for many real systems that also assumes a hard maths problem stays hard. That's why you will see "negligible" in papers.

// A REAL PROTOCOL 01
Schnorr: prove you know x without revealing x

  

    

      
Curve

      
Prover

      

        
        
        
        
        
        
      

      
ProverAnyone watching

      

      

    

    

      
    
        
  • The statement: "I know a number x such that Y = x·G". G is a fixed point on an elliptic curve, Y is public, and finding x from Y is the discrete-logarithm problem.
    • 
        
  • Commit: pick a random k and send R = k·G. Challenge: the verifier sends a random c. Respond: s = k + c·x (mod n).
    • 
        
  • Check: s·G must equal R + c·Y. It does, because s·G = k·G + c·(x·G).
    • 
        
  • Why s doesn't leak x: k is random and used once, so k + c·x is just a random-looking number.
    • 
      

      
SAME CURVES AS CRYPTO 101 "Tiny numbers" is the curve y² = x³ + 2x + 2 over the numbers mod 17, so you can follow every step. NIST P-256 is the real thing, from the same shared code as Crypto 101's signatures.

      
Sources: Schnorr (CRYPTO 1989) · Boneh and Shoup, chapter 19

    

  

  
~8 min
  • SaySame shape as the cave: commit, challenge, respond. The "tunnel" is now a random number k hidden inside a point R; the "call" is a number c; and the "come out the right side" is a number s that only someone who knows x can produce for any c they're asked.
  • ShowOn "Tiny numbers", press 1 to 5 in order and read the table: Y is public, k stays with the prover, R and c and s are what the watcher sees, and the check line shows both sides of the equation matching. Press "Start over" and repeat on NIST P-256: same steps, 256-bit numbers.
  • ShowSelect "Bluffing" and run 200 bluffing provers on "Tiny numbers": about 10 of them (200/19) get lucky, because the toy challenge has only 19 possible values. Switch to P-256: none do. The bluffer's best strategy is to guess the challenge in advance, so the chance per round is one in the number of possible challenges.
  • AskWhy do we need the random k at all? (Without it, s = c·x would hand over x as soon as anyone divides by c. k acts as a one-time pad for x.)
  • CheckThis is the Schnorr identification protocol (CRYPTO '89). Soundness error per round is 1 / (number of possible challenges); here the challenge is any number below the group order n. The toy curve's group order is 19. The bluff strategy is the textbook one: pick c and s first and solve for R.

// A REAL PROTOCOL 02
Soundness: two answers to one commitment reveal the secret

  

    

      

        
        
        
        
        
      

      

      

      

    

    

      
    
        
  • A prover who can answer two different challenges for the same R must know x: subtracting the answers gives s₁ − s₂ = (c₁ − c₂)·x, so x = (s₁ − s₂) / (c₁ − c₂). The division works because the group order n is prime.
    • 
        
  • That's the proof of soundness: a prover who convinces the verifier reliably could be "rewound" and asked twice, so they must hold x. Cheaters can't, because they can answer only one challenge per commitment.
    • 
        
  • The flip side for honest provers: never answer twice. k is single-use. Reuse it and anyone can compute x.
    • 
      

      
THE SAME BUG, IN THE WILD A repeated nonce in an ECDSA signature gives away the private key by this arithmetic. In 2010 the fail0verflow group showed, at the 27C3 conference, that Sony's PS3 firmware signing reused one; their slides say "from a pair of signatures that use the same m, we can compute m and k". Crypto 101 has a slide on exactly this.

      
Sources: fail0verflow, "Console Hacking 2010" (27C3) · Boneh and Shoup, §19.4.1 (special soundness)

    

  

  
~5 min
  • ShowPress 1 to 4. The prover commits once. We ask c₁ and get s₁. Then we "rewind" and ask c₂ about the same R and get s₂. The page divides, and the number it prints equals the prover's secret x, which was hidden all along. Then press "Same arithmetic: a signer reuses k": two Schnorr signatures on different messages with the same k, and the key falls out the same way.
  • SayThis is the soundness argument in one line. A cheater can answer one question; a prover who can answer two must know the secret. Cryptographers call it special soundness, and the rewinding trick is how they prove that an honest-looking prover really knows what they claim.
  • SayDon't forget the practical lesson: for a real prover, k must be fresh every time. That's why signing code is full of warnings about nonces, and why the same arithmetic broke a game console.
  • CheckThe fail0verflow slides (27C3, 29 December 2010) show the ECDSA arithmetic for a repeated random number and a slide titled "Sony's ECDSA code". They did not publish the key they could derive. Read from the slide PDF on 9 November 2026: the ECDSA arithmetic and the quote on the slide. The slide titled "Sony's ECDSA code" is an image I could not read, so the claim that Sony's implementation used a fixed random number rests on contemporary reports of the talk (for example Exophase) and on Crypto 101's own slide.

// A REAL PROTOCOL 03
Zero-knowledge: a transcript you can fake

  

    

      
Two transcripts of the same proof, for the same public Y on P-256. One is a real run by a prover who knows x. The other was made with no secret at all. Both pass the check. Which is which?

      

      

        
        
        
      

      

      

    

    

      
    
        
  • The simulator doesn't know x. It picks c and s at random first, then works out R = s·G − c·Y. The result passes the verifier's check.
    • 
        
  • Real and fake come from the same distribution (c and s are uniformly random, and R follows from them). So a transcript carries no information about x: anyone could have made it.
    • 
        
  • That is zero-knowledge: if the verifier could have produced the whole conversation alone, the conversation can't have taught them anything.
    • 
        
  • And the film proves nothing: the cave recording is exactly such a transcript.
    • 
      

      
WHY CHEATERS CAN'T USE THIS The simulator picks c before R. A live verifier picks c after seeing R, so a cheater would have to guess it in advance. Strictly, the plain protocol is "honest-verifier" zero-knowledge: it assumes the verifier picks c at random. Full zero-knowledge against any verifier needs extra steps.

      
Source: Boneh and Shoup, §19.1.1 (honest verifier zero knowledge) and §19.4.2

    

  

  
~5 min
  • ShowMake a guess, press the button, and the page reveals which was real and tallies your score. Press "New pair" and repeat. Both transcripts always show "verifier's check: accepted". Over many guesses you should land near 50%, which is exactly what zero-knowledge predicts: the two are indistinguishable.
  • SayThis slide is the definition of zero-knowledge made concrete. If I can write a program that, without the secret, produces transcripts that look exactly like real ones, then anyone who watched the real conversation learned nothing they couldn't have made up. It also explains deniability: a transcript of an interactive proof can't convince a third party.
  • AskIf the simulator can fake it, why can't a cheater use it to fool a live verifier? (Because it picks the challenge first. The live verifier picks the challenge after seeing R. The cheater has to commit to R before the question arrives.)
  • CheckThe "same distribution" claim is for an honest verifier (Boneh and Shoup, Definition 19.1). The toy-curve version of this check was run for the build: over 38,000 samples, real and simulated transcripts matched across all 361 possible (c, s) pairs. A verifier that picks challenges adaptively needs a stronger protocol.

// A REAL PROTOCOL 04
Fiat-Shamir: let a hash ask the question

  

    

      
      
The hash covers

      

        
        
        
      

      

      

    

    

      
    
        
  • Replace the verifier's random c with a hash: c = H(G, Y, R, message). There's nobody to ask: the proof is just (R, s), and anyone can check it, any time.
    • 
        
  • It stays unpredictable because c can't be known until R is fixed, and changing R changes c completely. That's the "after the commitment" rule from slide 7.
    • 
        
  • With the message inside the hash it's a signature. Ed25519 and Bitcoin's BIP340 signatures are Schnorr-style signatures built this way.
    • 
        
  • The trap: hash everything. All public values in the statement, plus every commitment. Leave one out and the demo's two bugs appear: anyone can forge, or a signature can be moved to a related key.
    • 
      

      
REAL BUGS In April 2022 Trail of Bits disclosed "Frozen Heart": proof libraries (PlonK and Bulletproofs implementations among them) that left public values out of the hash, which let attackers forge proofs for false statements. A 2023 study found 36 such implementations across 12 proof systems.

      
Sources: Trail of Bits: Frozen Heart disclosure · Dao, Miller, Wright, Grubbs: weak Fiat-Shamir attacks · BIP340 · IETF CFRG Fiat-Shamir draft

    

  

  
~7 min
  • ShowWith "Everything" selected, press 1: one signature (R, s), and the verifier's check passes. Press 2: edit the message, and the same signature fails, because the hash changed. Press 3: the forgery attempt fails, because the attacker would have to find an R that fits a challenge that depends on R.
  • ShowNow pick "Bug A: not the commitment" and press 3: the forgery succeeds, for anyone, with no secret. The attacker picks s first and solves for R, exactly the simulator trick from the last slide, because the hash no longer depends on R. Then pick "Bug B: not the public key", press 1 then 3: a valid signature for Y becomes a valid signature for a related key Y + t·G, no secret needed. That's why Bitcoin's BIP340 puts the public key inside the hash.
  • SayThe one rule: hash the whole statement and every commitment. It sounds obvious, and it was missed in real libraries for years. The reason is that papers sometimes describe the transform loosely and implementers copy what's written.
  • AskWhat other thing could you put in the hash to be safe? (A context or domain label: "this is a login proof for example.com, 14 Nov". It stops a proof made for one purpose being replayed for another. The demo hashes a fixed label and the curve name.)
  • CheckTrail of Bits: affected projects included Dusk Network's PlonK, Iden3's SnarkJS and ConsenSys's gnark; notified 18 March 2022; published 13 April 2022. The Bulletproofs flaw traced to a recommendation in the original paper. Dao et al. (IEEE S&P 2023): 36 weak implementations, 12 proof systems; their abstract says weak Fiat-Shamir can break classic protocols such as Schnorr's. The IETF CFRG drafts on sigma protocols and Fiat-Shamir were at revision 04 (2 November 2026) and still active drafts on 11 November 2026. Ed25519 as Schnorr-style: RFC 8032 section 1 says EdDSA "is a variant of Schnorr's signature system with (possibly twisted) Edwards curves" (read 9 November 2026).

// REAL-WORLD ZK 01
Building block: the sealed envelope (a commitment)

  

    

      
      
Envelope

      

        
        
        
        
      

      

      

    

    

      
    
        
  • A commitment is a sealed envelope: you publish a digest now, and open it later. Here it's SHA-256(salt ‖ value).
    • 
        
  • Hiding: the digest reveals nothing about the value, as long as the value is unpredictable or salted.
    • 
        
  • Binding: you can't open it to a different value later. Finding two values with the same digest would break SHA-256's collision resistance.
    • 
        
  • Without a salt, small secrets fall out: if the value could only be yes/no, 0 to 999, or rock/paper/scissors, a guesser hashes every option and compares. A fresh random salt (16 bytes here) stops that.
    • 
        
  • Almost every zero-knowledge proof uses them. The next slide hides a whole colouring this way.
    • 
      

    

  

  
~2 min
  • ShowKeep "rock" with a random salt: press 1, then 2 (the opened value and salt re-hash to the same digest), then "Try to change my mind": opening to "paper" fails. Now pick "No salt", commit "rock" and press "Guess from the digest": the guesser finds it in a handful of tries, because there are only a few plausible values. With a salt, the same guesser fails.
  • SayTwo promises: hiding (nobody can see inside) and binding (you can't swap the contents). They're why games like rock-paper-scissors can be played over the internet fairly: both commit, then both open.

  • The puzzle: colour the eight circles with three colours so that no two joined circles match. The prover has a solution and wants to prove it without showing it.
    • 
        
  • Each round: the prover renames the three colours at random, seals every circle's colour in its own salted envelope, and shows the eight envelopes. The verifier picks one line (click one, or press the button). The prover opens just its two ends. The verifier checks the envelopes and that the colours differ.
    • 
        
  • The verifier learns nothing: two different colours, randomly renamed each round, are all they ever see.
    • 
        
  • A cheater with one bad line is caught only when that line is picked: 1 in 14 per round. So many rounds are needed: 188 for odds of 1 in a million.
    • 
        
  • Why bother: 3-colouring is NP-complete, so any puzzle of that kind (every NP problem) can be converted into it and proved this way (Goldreich, Micali, Wigderson, 1986), assuming one-way functions exist.
    • 
      

      
NOT HOW REAL SYSTEMS DO IT It shows that any checkable statement can be proved in zero knowledge. Practical systems use far more efficient encodings; the next slides look at them.

      
Sources: Goldreich, Micali, Wigderson (JACM 1991) · Princeton COS 433 lecture notes

    

  

  
~5 min
  • ShowWith "Valid" selected press "Challenge a random edge" a few times: each round the page seals eight envelopes (real SHA-256 with random salts), opens the two ends of one line and checks them. Run 20 rounds: all pass. Click different lines yourself. Now pick "One slip" and press "Run until caught": the cheat is caught only when line 5-7 comes up, after a handful or a few dozen rounds. Click line 5-7 directly and the cheat is caught at once.
  • SayTwo things at once. For soundness: a cheater with a wrong colouring survives a round with probability 13/14, so you need a few hundred rounds to be confident. For zero-knowledge: renaming the colours every round means the opened pair is just "two different colours in random order": watching a thousand rounds teaches you nothing about the real colouring.
  • AskWhy must the prover rename the colours every round? (Otherwise each opened edge would reveal part of the real colouring, and after enough rounds the verifier would have all of it.)
  • CheckPer-round catch probability is at least 1/|E| (Princeton COS 433 notes; the notes' text has a typo that writes 1 − 1/m for it, but the repetition bound (1 − 1/m)^(mk) ≈ 2^-k is what they use). For |E| = 14 and 2^-20, the page computes 188 rounds. GMW: FOCS 1986, JACM 38(3) 1991; assumes one-way functions. I could not read the original paper's scan; the protocol follows the lecture notes.

// REAL-WORLD ZK 03
Prove you're on the list, without saying who

  

    

      
I am

      

        
        
        
      

      

        
Plain Merkle proofreal and checked here

        
Zero-knowledge versionproof bytes are a stand-in

      

      

    

    

      
    
        
  • The list's fingerprint is a Merkle root: one hash covering every member's commitment, H(secret). Eight members, so a path of three hashes.
    • 
        
  • A plain Merkle proof shows your leaf and the path: it proves you're in the list, and also names you, because the leaf is yours.
    • 
        
  • The zero-knowledge version says "I know a secret whose hash is some leaf under this root", without saying which. Systems like Semaphore use this for anonymous votes and signals.
    • 
        
  • A nullifier = H(secret, poll) lets the verifier reject a second vote in the same poll without learning who voted. A different poll gives an unrelated nullifier.
    • 
        
  • What it doesn't hide: that someone voted, when, from which network address. And the crowd is the list: a list of eight is a small crowd.
    • 
      

      
STAND-IN The SHA-256 Merkle tree, the path checks and the nullifiers are real and run in this page. The zero-knowledge proof itself is shown as random-looking bytes: making a real one needs a proof system like those on slide 18.

      
Sources: Semaphore documentation · RFC 6962 (Merkle hashing with 0x00 and 0x01 prefixes)

    

  

  
~3 min
  • ShowPick a member and press "Vote in poll-1". The left box shows the plain Merkle proof: the leaf and the three sibling hashes, verified against the root, with the line "the verifier now knows: this is Dee". The right box shows what the zero-knowledge version reveals: the root, the poll name, a nullifier and a proof blob; the member's name and leaf don't appear. Press "Vote again in poll-1": the same nullifier appears and the second vote is rejected, still with no name. Press "Vote in poll-2": a different nullifier, unlinkable to the first.
  • SayThis is the shape of an anonymous ballot or a private payment: prove membership, reveal a one-time tag so you can't reuse it, reveal nothing else. The ingredients are the ones we've seen: hashes, a tree, and a proof system to say "I know a path" without showing the path.
  • CheckSemaphore docs: "provable group member without revealing your identity", with a mechanism to prevent double-signaling; groups are Merkle trees. How Semaphore derives its nullifier isn't described on the page I read, so the nullifier construction here (SHA-256 of poll name and secret) is a simplification, as is the whole zero-knowledge part. Merkle hashing uses the 0x00 leaf and 0x01 node prefixes from RFC 6962.

// REAL-WORLD ZK 04
Over 18, without showing your birthday

  

    

      

        
        
Show

      

      

        
        
        
      

      

      
    
      
    
      
    
    
    
    
    
      
      
        
    • Level 1, the plain ID: name, birth date, address, all shown to prove one fact.
      • 
        
    • Level 2, salted digests: an issuer signs a list of digests, one per claim. You reveal only the claim you choose, plus its salt. The shop learns one fact. This is the idea behind SD-JWT (RFC 9901) and the mobile-ID standard, which has ready-made "age over 18" claims. But the signature is the same at every shop, so shops can compare notes.
      • 
        
    • Level 3, zero knowledge: prove "a credential signed by this issuer says I'm over 18" without showing the credential, its signature or the birth date. Every proof looks different, so there's nothing to compare. Google open-sourced such a library (Longfellow ZK) in July 2025.
      • 
        
    • Soundness at work: if you aren't over 18, no valid proof exists.
      • 
      
    
      
    ILLUSTRATION Levels 1 and 2 are real: SHA-256 digests and an ECDSA signature by a made-up "issuer", verified in this page. Level 3 shows the shape of the result with stand-in bytes; a real one needs the machinery on the next slide.
    
      
    Sources: RFC 9901 (SD-JWT) · Google: opening up ZKP technology · longfellow-zk
    
    
    
  
    
  
    ~4 min
    • ShowStart on level 2 with age 34 and press "Present to shop A": the shop receives one claim, age_over_18 = true, plus a digest list and a signature, and the page verifies the signature and the digest. The "what the shop learns" list shows one fact. Press "Present to shop B": the same signature fingerprint appears, so the shops can link you. Press "Edit the claim to cheat" after setting an age under 18: the edited claim fails the digest check.
    • ShowSwitch to level 1: everything is revealed. Switch to level 3: the shop learns only the statement, and each presentation shows different proof bytes (stand-ins), so shop A and shop B can't link them. Set the age to 15: no proof can be made.
    • SayThree rungs on a ladder. Showing the ID is the worst. Selective disclosure is a big improvement and already deployed, but it leaves a fingerprint. Zero-knowledge closes that last gap, at the cost of heavier machinery. The honest label on the slide matters: levels 1 and 2 are real here; level 3 shows what you'd get, not how.
    • CheckRFC 9901 (SD-JWT, November 2025) uses salted digests so verifiers can't guess undisclosed values. The mobile-ID standard ISO/IEC 18013-5 defines "age_over_NN" elements and a signed list of salted digests: I confirmed this through secondary sources (an ETSI report and vendor documentation), not the standard's text. Google's July 2025 post doesn't say which credential format its library handles; the longfellow-zk README names ISO mdoc, JWT and W3C Verifiable Credentials, reports several independent security reviews, and lists an IETF draft. The Lean model in the repo says it does not verify the code itself.

    // REAL-WORLD ZK 05
    SNARKs, STARKs, Bulletproofs: what's the catch?
    
  
    
    
    
      
    
        
        
        
      
    
      
    SystemTrusted setup?Proof sizeQuantum?
    
      
    
    
    
    
    
      
      
        
    • Trusted setup: some systems need a one-time ceremony to create public parameters. If someone keeps the secret behind them, they can forge proofs.
      • 
        
    • Proof size and cost: checking a proof is cheap by design; making one usually takes much more work than just running the computation. The details vary by system.
      • 
        
    • Assumptions: Schnorr and Bulletproofs rest on discrete logarithms, pairing-based SNARKs on pairings (also broken by a large quantum computer), and STARKs on hash functions.
      • 
        
    • Zcash's counterfeiting flaw: found on 1 March 2018 and disclosed 5 February 2019. Extra elements in the setup parameters let a cheating prover turn one valid proof into another. The post found no evidence it was exploited, and the Sapling upgrade (28 October 2018) closed it.
      • 
      
    
      
    WHY ZCASH CHANGED Its May 2022 upgrade (NU5) moved to Halo 2, which "eliminates protocol reliance on complex setup ceremonies".
    
      
    Sources: Groth16 · PLONK · STARK · Bulletproofs (Blockstream) · Zcash disclosure · NU5
    
    
    
  
    
  
    ~3 min
    • ShowTick a box and the table highlights the systems that fit. Tick two and the list shrinks. Tick all three: none are left. That's the point: there's no free lunch, only trade-offs.
    • SayThe systems in the news are descendants of the idea you've just seen, with two big upgrades: they can prove any program, not just "I know x", and the proof is a single message you can check quickly. The trade-offs are real: some need a ceremony, some make large proofs, some would not survive a quantum computer. You don't need to remember the names; you need to know to ask which trade-offs a product made.
    • CheckSourced facts: Groth16 proofs are three group elements (the paper); PLONK is a "universal SNARK construction" with fully succinct verification (abstract); a 64-bit Bulletproofs range proof is 674 bytes, logarithmic growth, no trusted setup (Blockstream); STARKs are transparent, with "post-quantum secure" in the paper's title. Not sourced to a primary page: that PLONK's setup is also updatable, PLONK's and STARK's exact sizes (the table says "hundreds of bytes" and "tens of KB or more": treat as rough), and the claim that provers cost much more than plain computation. A Schnorr signature is about 512 bits per Boneh and Shoup. Zcash: Ariel Gabizon found the flaw on 1 March 2018; Zcash disclosed it privately to Horizen and Komodo on 13 November 2018; public on 5 February 2019 as CVE-2019-7167; Sapling activated 28 October 2018; the post says "we have found no evidence" of counterfeiting.

    // REAL-WORLD ZK 06
    Where it's used, and where it's just a label
    
  
    
    
    
      
    Private paymentsZcash launched on 28 October 2016 with shielded transactions that hide sender, receiver and amount using zk-SNARKs. Its NU5 upgrade (31 May 2022) added Halo 2, which needs no trusted setup.
    
      
    RollupsZK-rollups run transactions off-chain and post a validity proof. Ethereum checks the proof before it accepts the new state, so no one has to re-run the transactions.
    
      
    Age and credential checksGoogle open-sourced Longfellow ZK (3 July 2025). The EU's age-verification blueprint (14 July 2025) is designed to work with the EU Digital Identity Wallets due by the end of 2026. Its text says age-verification apps should use zero-knowledge proofs, which critics point out is optional.
    
      
    Anonymous signalsSemaphore lets a group member vote or endorse as a provable member without revealing which one, with a mechanism that prevents double-signalling.
    
    
    
    
    A LABEL, NOT ALWAYS A PROOF "Zero-knowledge" on a product page is sometimes used for a different idea: the provider can't read your data, because it's encrypted end to end with keys only you hold (some password managers describe themselves this way). That's a good design, but it isn't a zero-knowledge proof. Ask what exactly is being proved, and to whom.
    
    
    Sources: Zcash NU5 · Zcash basics · ethereum.org: ZK-rollups · Google · European Commission · EDRi · Semaphore · Bitwarden
    
  
    
  
    ~3 min
    • SayFour places this exists today, and one warning. Private payments were the first big deployment. Rollups use proofs to check a pile of computation cheaply. Age checks are the one most people will meet: your phone proves "over 18" to a website. Anonymous signals are the plain membership proof from two slides ago. And the warning: marketing sometimes uses the phrase for end-to-end encryption, which is a different thing.
    • AskWhich of these would you trust with a real decision, and what would you want to check first? (Who audited it, what the trusted setup was, whether the statement being proved is the one that matters. The last slide is a checklist for exactly this.)
    • CheckZcash launch on 28 October 2016: Electric Coin Co announcement and Zcash docs. NU5 activated 31 May 2022 (Electric Coin Co post, now hosted at zodl.com). Ethereum.org (updated 5 November 2026): "L2 transactions are finalized only if the L1 contract accepts the validity proof". Google post: 3 July 2025; it does not say Google Wallet uses the library. Commission page: blueprint made available 14 July 2025, "feature ready" on 15 April 2026, wallets by the end of 2026, "prove they are over 18 without sharing any other personal information"; the page doesn't mention zero-knowledge proofs by name. EDRi (7 September 2026) quotes the blueprint's "SHOULD implement the Zero-Knowledge Proof mechanism" and warns that mandatory ZKPs wouldn't by themselves guarantee privacy; it's an advocacy group's reading of the blueprint, not the Commission's. Bitwarden's post describes "zero-knowledge encryption" as end-to-end encrypted vaults whose keys only users hold. Researchers at ETH Zurich and USI Lugano have questioned whether password-manager providers truly can't see vaults (reported by Techzine); that's a separate claim I did not verify here.

    // WRAP-UP 01
    What a proof does not tell you
    
  
    
    
    
      
    DOES A VALID PROOF GUARANTEE THIS?
    
      
    
      
    
      
    
      
    
    
    
    
    
      
      
        
    • A proof is a tool for one job: convincing someone a particular statement is true, revealing nothing more.
      • 
        
    • Everything around the statement is on you: that it's the right statement, that its inputs were honest, that the program or circuit encoding it is correct, that the setup was clean, and that what leaks around the proof (network address, timing, list size) is acceptable.
      • 
        
    • The weak points in real incidents were rarely the core maths. Frozen Heart was a hashing slip around the maths, and Zcash's 2018 flaw was in the setup parameters.
      • 
      
    
      
    GARBAGE IN, PROOF OUT A proof that a computation ran correctly says nothing about whether the data fed in was true. A proof of "over 18" says nothing about whether the person presenting it is the person it describes.
    
    
    
  
    
  
    ~4 min
    • ShowLet the room answer each statement before pressing. The answers: 1 Yes (up to the proof system's tiny error), 2 No, 3 No, 4 No, 5 No, 6 Depends.
    • SayThe theme of this part: a zero-knowledge proof is strong where it applies and silent about everything else. Most of the damage in real incidents came from the things around the proof: a slip in how the hash was built, extra values in a setup, a statement that didn't say what people thought it said.
    • AskAn app proves it ran a computation correctly. What question should you ask next? (Where did the inputs come from, and who could have lied about them?)
    • CheckOn "quantum": discrete-log and pairing-based systems fall to a large quantum computer; hash-based STARKs are believed to resist (the paper's title says "post-quantum secure"). That's the state of knowledge, not a guarantee. Circuit bugs ("under-constrained" statements that accept too much) are a known class of problem in proof systems; I describe the class, and cite no specific incident beyond the two on this slide.
