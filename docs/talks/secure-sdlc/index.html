// LIFECYCLE 01

Why a process beats a hero

HOW YOU TREAT A REPORT · 2021 TO 2022A journalist, a web page and a governorA St. Louis Post-Dispatch journalist told Missouri's education department that its website exposed teachers' Social Security numbers in the page source. The governor called it hacking and promised prosecution. The county prosecutor declined to charge in February 2022. The flaw had been there since 2011. A policy decides in advance whether a report is a gift or a threat.
A LEGAL DUTY · 11 SEPTEMBER 2026EU reporting now appliesUnder the Cyber Resilience Act, manufacturers of products with digital elements sold in the EU must report actively exploited vulnerabilities: an early warning within 24 hours, a full notification within 72 hours, through ENISA's single reporting platform. You can't improvise that on the day.
A MOVING STANDARD · 2025 TO 2026NIST SSDF is being revisedThe Secure Software Development Framework (SP 800-218) is at version 1.1, final since February 2022. A Rev. 1 (version 1.2) draft went out for comment on 17 December 2025, with comments closed on 30 January 2026. The practices keep evolving. The habit of reviewing your process is the part that lasts.
The idea: add the smallest step that works at each stage, make the safe way the easy way, decide in advance how you'll treat a report, and watch numbers that show whether it's working. Nothing in that list depends on one brilliant person staying.

~3 min

  • SayThree stories, one theme: what happens depends on what the organisation decided beforehand. In Missouri, the page source held the numbers; the reporter did what a person with a browser can do, and told the state. How the state reacted shows what happens with no policy that says "thank you". The Cyber Resilience Act made one piece of process mandatory in the EU from 11 September 2026: if you ship a product that contains software and someone is actively exploiting a vulnerability in it, the clock starts the moment you become aware. And the standards you'll be asked about keep moving, which is why the point is a loop, not a snapshot. Security that depends on a hero fails when the hero is on holiday, leaves or burns out.
  • AskIf your best security person left next month, what would stop working first? Answer: usually reviews, triage of reports and knowing which of the old exceptions are still valid.
  • TipSources: Missouri (Post-Dispatch reporting; Cole County prosecutor's decision on 11 February 2022; the flaw's age from a later state police report as reported). CRA: the European Commission's CRA reporting page says reporting duties apply from 11 September 2026, early warning within 24 hours of becoming aware, full notification within 72 hours, final report no later than 14 days after a corrective measure is available (actively exploited vulnerabilities), through the ENISA single reporting platform. SSDF: csrc.nist.gov page for SP 800-218 Rev. 1 (initial public draft, 17 December 2025, comments due 30 January 2026).
  • CheckThe Missouri account rests on press coverage (Malwarebytes, NBC News, Post-Dispatch, Krebs on Security and others) and not the court record; keep to "declined to charge". The "since 2011" detail is from the Missouri State Highway Patrol's report as covered by KSHB and KBIA on 22 February 2022, which also found the reporter did nothing wrong; reported counts of exposed numbers range from 100,000 to 576,000, so the slide gives none. Whether the CRA applies to your product depends on what it is and where it's sold: this talk isn't legal advice.

// LIFECYCLE 02

One light step at every stage

~7 min

  • SayClick through the six stages. Each has one lightweight step, not a gate. Requirements: acceptance criteria. Design: a short threat-modelling session, but only for changes that add a boundary, a data store or a new way in. Code: secure defaults and a checklist. Build and test: automation that runs on every change. Release: signed, pinned, least-privilege. Operate: a policy, logs, rehearsed response. Every step links to the talk in this track that teaches the detail. The "if you find it later" line is deliberately qualitative: the famous "N times more expensive" figure has weak sources, so I don't quote one.
  • AskWhich stage in your process has no step at all? Answer: for most teams it's design, and then operate.
  • TipStart with the stage where bugs reach customers. If incidents come from forgotten authorization checks, start at requirements and code review. If they come from leaked keys, start at code and CI. Add one step per quarter, not six at once.

// LIFECYCLE 03

Requirements as acceptance criteria

Pick a feature from the backlog

    ~5 min

    • SayThe cheapest security control is a sentence in the story. "Users can reset their password" becomes five testable statements: the link works once, it expires, the response doesn't reveal whether an account exists, the requests are rate limited, and sessions end on change. Each of those is something a developer can build and a test can check. Tick the box to see a test name for each. If a criterion can't be turned into a test, it's probably too vague, and a reviewer can't hold anyone to it either. Pick the feature that is closest to what your team ships next week.
    • AskWhich of these would your current definition of done have missed? Answer: usually the "same response for unknown emails" and "sessions end" items, because they're about what must not happen.
    • TipKeep a short list of reusable criteria per feature type (sign-in, upload, export, admin, webhook) in the team wiki, and paste from it into stories. OWASP's Application Security Verification Standard is the longer source when you need more. All the criteria here are my own examples.

    // LIFECYCLE 04

    Frameworks to borrow from

    Where each speaks to the lifecycle

    ~5 min

    • SayBorrow, don't adopt wholesale. OWASP SAMM is the one I'd start with: five business functions with three practices each, scored on three maturity levels, built for a "score, pick two, improve, score again" loop. The NIST SSDF is a catalogue of outcomes in four groups, useful when a customer or government buyer asks for it. BSIMM is a benchmark: it tells you what firms like yours actually do, not what they should. CISA's Secure by Design pledge is a ready-made set of seven promises to make about your product. The ISO standards describe the shape of a disclosure and handling process. The table on the right shows where each speaks to our six stages.
    • AskHas anyone been asked "do you follow the SSDF?" by a customer? Answer: if yes, a one-page mapping from your own steps to its four groups answers it.
    • TipSources read for this talk: SAMM's model page (the five functions and fifteen practices); NIST's CSRC pages for SP 800-218 and its Rev. 1 draft; CISA's pledge page (the seven goals, voluntary); Black Duck's BSIMM page ("descriptive", 100+ organisations); ISO/IEC 29147:2018 and 30111:2019 via secondary summaries.
    • CheckThe current SAMM release: the owaspsamm/core GitHub releases page lists v2.2.0 as latest, released on 6 July (the page shows no year; the previous release was v2.1.0, September 2024); owaspsamm.org's model page still calls it "version 2". Confirm the year on owaspsamm.org before quoting it. The SSDF practice groups' names come from my own knowledge, and the NIST page I read didn't list them, so check them against the PDF. I did not read the ISO documents themselves (they're paid). Whether SSDF 1.2 has been finalised after May 2026: not confirmed.

    // CHECKPOINT 1 · 5 MIN

    Through the lifecycle

    Talk with a neighbour for a couple of minutes, then we share answers.

    1. Where in your current process would a design flaw be caught today, if at all?

      Hint

      Walk a recent feature from idea to release and note who looked at its design.

      Answer

      For most teams: nowhere, until it's built. The fix is a short design review (threat modelling) for changes that add a boundary, a data store or a new way in, and an owner who checks it happened.

    2. Which of your last three stories would have been safer with security acceptance criteria, and what would one have said?

      Hint

      Think about what must not happen: reuse, enumeration, another tenant's data.

      Answer

      There's no single answer. A good one is testable: "a reset link works once" or "an export only contains the caller's organisation's rows".

    3. You can add one step this quarter. Which stage, and what makes you sure it's the one?

      Hint

      Where did your last real incident or near miss come from?

      Answer

      The stage where the last bug reached customers. Pick the cheapest step that would have caught it, and measure whether it does.

    // PEOPLE AND PROCESS 01

    Security champions, planned honestly

    What you give them

      ~6 min

      • SayA champion is a developer on a product team who becomes the first stop for security questions there: they join design reviews, help triage findings and carry lessons back to the team. The programme works when the time is protected and the person is supported, and it fails when it's a title with extra unpaid work. Slide the numbers: with a champion for every ten developers and ten teams, you get eight champions and two teams with none. Drop the hours below one a week and the verdict turns red, because nothing will change. Push it above eight and it's a second job. Then tick the five support items: training, a direct line to security, a say in the paved road, recognition and protected time. Fewer than three and burnout follows.
      • AskWho would you pick, and what would their manager need to agree to? Answer: someone respected by peers, not just the most security-keen; and a manager who has agreed in writing to the hours.
      • TipThe ratios and hours here are teaching defaults of my own, not published standards: there's no authoritative number. Start with one volunteer per team for the teams with the most risk, and expand. Rotate the role yearly so it isn't a trap.
      • CheckAll numbers are my own heuristics. I didn't read a published source for champions-programme sizing (OWASP's and BSIMM's material on champions describe the practice, not a ratio).
      • Next"Champions help people choose well. A paved road makes the safe choice the easy one."

      // PEOPLE AND PROCESS 02

      Paved roads: make the safe way the easy way

      Pick a task teams repeat

      ~6 min

      • SayA paved road is a template, library or default that does the secure thing without asking the developer to know why: a shared HTTP client with timeouts and TLS, one helper for reading a secret, a template engine that escapes by default, a policy decorator for authorization, a service template with CI, scanning and a hardened image. People take the easier path. The model is crude on purpose: adoption is the share of real cases the road covers, weighted by how much easier it is than going alone. If the paved way takes as long as the DIY way, you get half. If the road is harder than going alone, you get little, and no mandate fixes that, because a mandate adds some adoption on paper and a pile of workarounds in practice. Adjust the sliders to see what you'd need.
      • AskWhat's the one task your teams repeat that would pay back a paved road fastest? Answer: usually "start a new service" or "read a secret".
      • TipTreat the road like a product: it has users, a roadmap and a feedback channel (champions are good for that). Measure how many services use it and which exceptions keep appearing. The model's formula is mine, for teaching: it is not an empirical result.
      • Next"Next: how you handle the people who find what you missed."

      // PEOPLE AND PROCESS 03

      A disclosure policy and security.txt

      Serve at /.well-known/security.txt as text/plain, over https

      
      
      
      
        
      
        
    
        
  
        
  
        ~7 min
        • SayA disclosure policy is a short public page that says how to report a vulnerability, what's in scope, whether testing is allowed, when you'll reply and what the reporter can expect. The security.txt file, defined by RFC 9116, points to it. Two fields are required: Contact, and Expires, so a stale file can't pass for a current one. The contact is a URI, so an email needs mailto:. Web links must be https. Expires should be less than a year ahead. Everything else is optional: Policy, Acknowledgments, Encryption (a link to your key, never the key pasted in), Preferred-Languages, Hiring and Canonical. Fill the form on the left; the file on the right passes the same checker as the title slide. Put it at /.well-known/security.txt, served as text/plain over https.
        • AskWho reads the security@ mailbox today, and what happens when they're away? Answer: if the answer is "one person", that's the first thing to fix.
        • TipPolicy contents, from OWASP's Vulnerability Disclosure Cheat Sheet and CISA's VDP template: scope (which systems, which are excluded), safe-harbour wording for good-faith testing reviewed by a lawyer, a secure way to report (an https form if you can), a promised reply time (CISA's template says acknowledge within three business days), credit for reporters and a promise not to threaten them. The US Department of Justice's May 2022 charging policy tells prosecutors to decline to charge good-faith security research under the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, but a policy is how you say "we agree" in advance. Add a reminder to renew Expires.
        • CheckRFC 9116 read in full for fields, requirements and HTTPS and location rules. On stale files the RFC says data past its Expires date "is considered stale and should not be used", and that no file may be better than a stale one; the checker only says "stale", which is enough. Whether your web host serves dot-directories: GitHub Pages needs a .nojekyll file or similar. The DOJ policy (announced 19 May 2022; prosecutors should decline to charge conduct that is good-faith security research, with extortion excluded) was read through law-firm summaries and the press release as mirrored elsewhere, not justice.gov itself, whose page didn't load on 11 November 2026.

        // PEOPLE AND PROCESS 04
        A report arrives: what's due, and when?
        
  
        
    
        
      
        
        
        
        
        
      
        
      
        
    
        
    
        
      
          
    
          
  
          
  
          ~6 min
          • SayPick a date and a severity. The list shows what's due and who owns each deadline. Your own: acknowledge the report (CISA's template says three business days; sooner is kinder), confirm or explain, and ship a fix. The fix targets here are invented examples: critical in seven days, high in thirty, medium in ninety, low in a hundred and eighty. The researcher's clock is separate: Google Project Zero publishes after 90 days, or after 7 if the bug is being actively exploited, and other researchers set their own. Now tick "actively exploited" and "we sell a product in the EU": three more lines appear, because under the Cyber Resilience Act, from 11 September 2026, manufacturers must send an early warning within 24 hours of becoming aware, a full notification within 72 hours and a final report no later than 14 days after a fix is available. That is why the first 24 hours matter.
          • Ask(Checkpoint question, previewed.) A researcher emails you about a vulnerability. What should happen in the first 24 hours? Answer: someone acknowledges it, one person owns it, you reproduce it, you decide severity and whether it's exploited, and you start the clocks.
          • TipAgree your own targets with the team, publish the ones you can keep, and test the first response with a drill. Business-day arithmetic here ignores public holidays. Project Zero's policy (90 days to fix, details 30 days after the patch ships, a 14-day extension on request, 7 days when a bug is actively exploited) was read on its policy page (now projectzero.google); the CRA deadlines on the Commission's CRA reporting page (digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/cra-reporting, read 11 November 2026).
          • CheckThis applies to manufacturers placing products with digital elements on the EU market, and only to actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents: I didn't check scope exemptions or the exact trigger for "becoming aware"; the Commission's page says open-source software stewards' reporting duties start later, on 11 December 2027, and that a severe incident's final report is due within a month of the 72-hour notification. It is not legal advice. The platform was reported to be at version 1.2.1 on 20 October 2026 (secondary): check the current process on ENISA's site.

          // PEOPLE AND PROCESS 05 · SKIPPABLE
          Bug bounties: when they help, and when they're premature
          
  
          
    
          
      
          Tick what's true today
          
      
          
    
          
    
          
      
          
      
          
      
          The ideaA bounty pays for volume. If your policy, replies, triage and fixing can't yet cope with the reports you get for free, paying for more makes things worse, and unanswered reporters talk.
          
    
          
  
          
  
          ~3 min
          • SayA bounty is paying researchers for valid reports. It works when you can already handle reports well. OWASP's cheat sheet says bounty programmes should only be run by organisations that already have a mature disclosure process. The checklist is my own heuristic built from that advice: the four basics (a live policy, replies within your promise, a named triager, capacity to fix) are the gate. Without them the verdict is "not yet" however many other boxes you tick. With all eight, start with a private invite-only pilot.
          • AskWhat did your last unpaid report cost you in time, and would you cope with ten a week? Answer: that is the real readiness test.
          • TipAlternatives: a plain disclosure policy with public thanks (a hall of fame), swag, or a managed triage service. Check the legal and tax side before paying anyone.
          • CheckThe "mature disclosure process first" advice is from OWASP's Vulnerability Disclosure Cheat Sheet, read for this talk. The eight items and the verdict thresholds are mine.

          // PEOPLE AND PROCESS 06 · SKIPPABLE
          Blameless reviews for security bugs
          
  
          
    
          Click a sentence to rewrite it as a question about the system
          
    
          
    
          
  
          
  
          ~4 min
          • SayAfter a security bug or an incident, the review asks "what let this happen?", not "who did it?". Each sentence starts as the blameful version. Click it to see the same fact written about the system, and the change it points to. If the review ends with a person's name, you've learned nothing that stops the next one: the same pipeline will let the next person do it. Blameless doesn't mean nobody is accountable. It means the first question is about conditions.
          • AskWhat was the last security bug you reviewed, and what changed in the system because of it? Answer: if nothing changed, the review was a ceremony.
          • TipRun the review within a week, include the people involved, write down contributing factors and give each action an owner and a date. Share it more widely than the team. The sample sentences are invented.

          // MEASURING 01
          Numbers that drive improvement
          
  
          
    
          
      
          
      
          Median days to fix, by severity (invented data)
          
      
          
    
          
    
          
      
          
      
          Repeat bug classes
          
      
          
    
          
  
          
  
          ~5 min
          • SayThree numbers that tell you whether the process is working. First, time to fix by severity: how long the important things stay open. In quarter one the median critical took about two weeks and high ones about six; in quarter two criticals took four days. Second, paved-road coverage: three of ten services in quarter one, six of ten in quarter two. That's a cause, not a symptom. Third, repeat bug classes: when the same class shows up quarter after quarter (here, authorization), the fix at the instance level isn't working, and the answer is a paved road or a test, not another reminder. Switch quarters and read the notes under the numbers.
          • AskWhich of these could you compute today from your tracker? Answer: time to fix, if tickets have severity and dates; the other two usually need a tag.
          • TipReport medians and how many are past their target, not averages. Keep the numbers with the team that owns the fix, not on a league table. All the findings, services and quarters are invented.
          • Next"The numbers that look good and teach nothing."
          

          


          
  
          // MEASURING 02
          Metrics to avoid (or to guard)
          
  
          
    
          
      
          
      
          
    
          
    
          
      
          Reward "findings closed" and see what a team does with capacity for  closures
          
      
      
          
      
          
    
          
  
          
  
          ~5 min
          • SayWhen a number becomes a target, people optimise the number. Pick a metric to see what it rewards and how it gets gamed. "Findings reported" rewards splitting bugs into many tickets. "Lines of code scanned" rewards scanning generated code. "Zero open criticals" is fine if re-rating needs a second pair of eyes and every acceptance expires. On the right, reward "findings closed" and a team with room for eight closures can close the four lows and the three mediums first, plus one high: eight closed, the same count as closing the worst first, but two criticals and two highs still open. The dashboard turns green while the risk stays. Time to fix by severity, paved-road coverage and repeat classes are harder to game, and they measure causes.
          • AskWhich number do you report upwards today, and how would a clever team improve it without making anything safer? Answer: that question finds the weak metric.
          • TipThis is Goodhart's law: a measure that becomes a target stops being a good measure. Pair every number with a guard (a re-test, a second reviewer, an expiry) and review the set once a year. The findings are the same invented set as the previous slide.
