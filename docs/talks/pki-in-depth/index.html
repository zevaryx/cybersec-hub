// WHY THIS TALK

Everything on the web leans on a few hundred promises

A browser trusts a site because a certificate authority said so, and it trusts that authority because its own makers said so. Two kinds of failure follow: the wrong party is trusted, or the right one is forgotten.

  • Any trusted CA can vouch for any name. One failure can touch every site.
  • The system is built to notice and recover: public logs, blocklists, removal from trust stores.
  • Most outages aren't attacks. They're a certificate nobody remembered to renew.
DigiNotar, 2011A Dutch CA was breached. Its rogue certificates included one for *.google.com, used against roughly 300,000 users, almost all in Iran. Browsers removed it.
eMudhra, October 2026Keys for eight intermediate CA certificates were generated with insufficient randomness. Chrome and Firefox blocked them within hours of a researcher's report.
Equifax, 2017An expired certificate on a traffic-inspection device left it blind for 19 months, according to the House Oversight report. Renewing it exposed the breach.

Sources: Fox-IT report · Google · House Oversight

~3 min

  • SayThree very different failures: a CA that was hacked, a CA that made weak keys, and a company that forgot a renewal. The first two are about who we trust; the third is about running what we have. We'll look at all of them.
  • TipThe title says "a few hundred promises" as a figure of speech about the number of roots in trust stores, not a measured count. Don't quote it as a statistic.

// THE TRUST MODEL 01

Trust stores: who decides which CAs you trust

  • Your device ships with a trust store: a list of root certificates it will accept as the end of a chain.
  • Browsers and operating systems each run a root program. Mozilla, Chrome, Apple and Microsoft decide who gets in, and who is removed.
  • Chrome now keeps its own root store on most platforms, rather than using the operating system's.
  • Employers can add their own root. That root can vouch for any site on that device: scope it carefully.

Sources: Chrome Root Program policy

~5 min

  • ShowStart on "Up-to-date laptop": every site checks out. Switch to "Old phone": it doesn't have Borealis, but news.example still works because Borealis's certificate was also signed by Atlas (a cross-sign: the next slide). Switch to "Work laptop": the company root is trusted, so the intranet works. Then click a root off and watch the sites it vouched for fail.
  • SayThe sites and CAs here are invented. The point: trust isn't global, it's whatever list this particular device carries. Old devices fall behind on that list, which is why cross-signing exists.
  • TipCheck: "most platforms" is from search coverage of Chrome's move to its own root store (it began platform by platform in 2022). Confirm the current list on the Chrome Root Program page before naming platforms.

// THE TRUST MODEL 02

Why chains exist: keep the root offline

Root CAKey kept offline. Signs only a handful of certificates, rarely.
signs ↓
Intermediate CAOnline, issues certificates every day. If it's compromised, replace it. The root stays safe.
signs ↓
Your site's certificateShort-lived, one name, sent by your server with the intermediate.
  • A root's key is valuable because every device trusts it for years. So it signs intermediates, then goes into a safe.
  • Your server sends the leaf and its intermediates, not the root. The device already has the root.
  • A missing intermediate is a common misconfiguration. Some clients fetch or cache it for you; others fail. Always serve the full chain.
  • Cross-signing: the same CA key certified by a second, older root, so devices that don't yet trust the new root still find a path.
Let's Encrypt, 2021Its ISRG Root X1 was cross-signed by IdenTrust's DST Root CA X3. When that older root expired on 30 September 2021, devices that lacked ISRG Root X1 (an iPhone 4 was the example) began showing warnings.

Source: Let's Encrypt: DST Root CA X3 expiration

~4 min

  • SayThree levels so that the most precious key almost never touches a network. If an intermediate is stolen you revoke or replace it and issue a new one; if a root is stolen, every device on earth has to change. Cross-signing is a bridge: the same key, vouched for by two different parents, so old and new devices can both find a way up.
  • AskWhy is it a problem if the root is online and signs leaf certificates directly? Answer: any breach of that server loses the key every device trusts, with no way to replace it quickly.
  • TipLet's Encrypt's page says older Android devices kept working past the expiry because Android doesn't enforce the expiry of a trust anchor. Don't over-explain it; the lesson is that old devices fall behind on their trust stores.

// THE TRUST MODEL 04
DV, OV and EV: what the CA checked, and what you see

  

    

      
DV · Domain ValidatedThe CA checked that the requester controls the name, usually by an automated challenge.Most certificates today. Free and automated with ACME.

      
OV · Organization ValidatedThe CA also checked that the organisation exists and is who it says.The details are inside the certificate, but browsers don't show them in the address bar.

      
EV · Extended ValidationStricter, standardised legal and identity checks on the organisation.Chrome 77 and Firefox 70 (2019) moved the company name out of the address bar into a click-through panel.

    

    
WHAT THE PADLOCK SAYS The connection is private, and the other end holds a certificate for this name. It does not say the business is honest: criminals get padlocks too. (See the phishing talk.)

    
Sources: Decipher (Duo) on the EV changes · Phishing and Scams

// THE TRUST MODEL 05
The CA/Browser Forum and the root programs

  

    

      
    
        
  1. A CA appliesto a root program, through the shared Common CA Database (CCADB).
    2. 
        
  2. It's auditedagainst the Baseline Requirements and the program's own policy.
    3. 
        
  3. It's includedand browsers and operating systems ship its roots.
    4. 
        
  4. It's watchedincident reports, CT logs, and researchers keep checking.
    5. 
        
  5. It can be removedif it breaks the rules or the risk outweighs the value.
    6. 
      

    

    

      
    
        
  • The CA/Browser Forum brings CAs and browser makers together to write the Baseline Requirements: the minimum rules for publicly trusted TLS certificates.
    • 
        
  • Each root program adds its own policy on top, and can be stricter.
    • 
        
  • Rules change by ballot, voted on by CAs and by browsers. Example: SC-081v3, which sets today's shrinking certificate lifetimes. Apple proposed it; Sectigo, Google and Mozilla endorsed it; it passed in April 2025 (CAs 25 yes, 0 no, 5 abstain; browsers 4 yes).
    • 
        
  • The Baseline Requirements are versioned documents. The copy on GitHub is version 2.3.1, effective 4 October 2026.
    • 
      

      
Sources: Ballot SC-081v3 · Baseline Requirements

    

  

  
~3 min
  • SayThere's no government in charge of web trust. It's a loop: browsers and OS vendors decide who to include, CAs agree to rules with the browsers, and everyone watches each other. When the rules need to change, like shortening lifetimes, it happens by a ballot in the Forum.
  • TipCheck before presenting: how many issuer and consumer votes the ballot needed is in the Forum's bylaws, which we haven't quoted here. Only the SC-081v3 vote counts and the BR version number are read from the sources.

// CHECKPOINT 1 · 5 MIN
Pause and discuss

  

    
Talk with a neighbour for a couple of minutes, then we share answers.

    
    
      
  1. Why can a single misbehaving CA affect every website?
    Hint
    Which names is a trusted CA allowed to vouch for?
    Answer
    Any CA in the trust store can issue a certificate for any name, and devices accept it. So one CA's mistake or breach puts every site at risk, unless the CA's certificate is limited by name constraints.
    2. 
      
  2. What do intermediate CAs buy us?
    Hint
    Think about which key is hardest to replace.
    Answer
    The root key can stay offline. The intermediates do the daily signing, so if one is compromised it can be revoked or replaced without every device having to change its trust store.
    3. 
      
  3. A site works in one browser but fails in a command-line tool. It sends only its own certificate. What's wrong, and what's the fix?
    Hint
    What does the server normally send besides the leaf?
    Answer
    The intermediate is missing. Some clients fetch or cache missing intermediates; others don't. Configure the server to send the full chain.

// WHEN CAS GET IT WRONG 01
Certificate Transparency: make mistakes visible

  

    

      
    
        
  1. The CA logs itThe certificate goes into public, append-only logs.
    2. 
        
  2. The log promisesIt returns a signed receipt, an SCT, that goes inside the certificate.
    3. 
        
  3. The browser checksNo receipts from enough different logs, no connection.
    4. 
        
  4. Monitors watchDomain owners read the logs for certificates they never asked for.
    5. 
      

    

    

      
    
        
  • CT doesn't prevent a bad certificate. It makes sure a bad certificate can't be secret.
    • 
        
  • Each log is a Merkle tree: add an entry and the root hash changes; edit an old one and every hash above it changes. Logs publish signed tree heads, so anyone can compare.
    • 
        
  • Chrome's policy needs 2 receipts for certificates of up to 180 days (3 for longer), from different logs, with at least two different log operators.
    • 
        
  • CT is specified in RFC 6962 (2013, by Google engineers) and RFC 9162 (version 2.0).
    • 
      

      
Sources: Chrome CT policy · RFC 6962 · RFC 9162 · certificate.transparency.dev

    

  

  
~4 min
  • SayBefore CT, a CA could issue a certificate for your domain and nobody would know until someone got attacked with it. Now every publicly trusted certificate a browser accepts has to be in the open. It doesn't stop the mistake; it makes sure the person who can fix it hears about it.
  • AskIf the log is public, who actually reads it? Answer: monitors run by domain owners, security companies and researchers, who watch for names they care about.
  • TipCheck: the CT policy page doesn't say when Chrome began enforcing CT (secondary sources say April 2018), so don't give a date from this slide. It also doesn't say RFC 6962 was written because of DigiNotar; DigiNotar is the case that shows why it matters, but the RFC doesn't cite it.

    // WHEN CAS GET IT WRONG 05
    Distrust: a cutoff date, not a cliff
    
  
    
    
    
      
    
        
        
        
      
    
      
    
    
    
    
    
      
    
      
      
        
    • Browsers often distrust by the date a certificate first appeared in CT logs (its earliest SCT). Older ones work until they expire; newer ones are refused.
      • 
        
    • Log timestamps are signed by independent logs, so a CA can't simply backdate a certificate.
      • 
        
    • Chrome did this for Entrust in 2024 and for emSign Root CA - G1 in 2026: first logged after 16 November 2026 means not trusted by default from Chrome 153.
      • 
      
    
      
    
      
    Sources: Google, eMudhra (4 Nov update) · Google, Entrust (2024)
    
    
    
  
    
  
    ~4 min
    • ShowThe default cutoff is 16 November 2026. Drag "today" forward: certificates logged before the cutoff keep working until they expire, then you can't renew from that CA. Move the cutoff earlier and more certificates stop working. Click "Cutoff = today" to see that nothing breaks today, but every renewal after it will.
    • SayThe practical message for a site owner: when your CA is distrusted, the pain arrives at your next renewal, not today. The fix is being able to get a certificate from a different CA quickly: that's an automation and inventory problem, not a PKI one.

    // REVOCATION AND LIFETIMES 01
    Revocation: a good idea that never worked well
    
  
    
    
    
      
    CRLA signed list of revoked serial numbers. Complete, but large, and browsers rarely fetch it live.
    
      
    OCSPAsk the CA about one certificate. Tells the CA which site you're visiting, and Mozilla measured about 100 ms median added to the handshake.
    
      
    StaplingThe server attaches a fresh OCSP answer. Private and fast, but only works if the server does it. "Must-Staple" made it mandatory, and browser support never spread.
    
      
    Pushed listsBrowsers ship their own: Chrome's CRLSets, Firefox's OneCRL for intermediates, and CRLite (a compact filter of all revoked certificates, on by default for Firefox desktop since version 137, refreshed every 12 hours).
    
      
    Short lifetimesIf a certificate expires in days, you need to revoke far less. The current direction.
    
    
    
    
    LET'S ENCRYPT turned off OCSP: Must-Staple requests began failing on 30 January 2025, OCSP URLs left certificates on 7 May 2025, and the responders shut down on 6 August 2025. Its reasons: privacy, and the cost of running it. CRLs replaced it.
    
    
    Sources: Let's Encrypt: ending OCSP · Mozilla: CRLite
    
  
    
  
    ~4 min
    • SayRevocation has to work for everyone, quickly, privately, and even when the CA's server is down. Real revocation checks struggled on all of those. Where a check can't complete, browsers have usually let the connection continue, which means an attacker who can block the check wins. So the industry is turning to two answers: browsers pushing their own lists, and certificates that die young.
    • TipCheck: the "browsers let the connection continue when a check fails" point (soft-fail) is widely documented but not in the sources linked here, so say it as background, not as a quoted fact. Mozilla's post says it planned to disable OCSP for domain-validated certificates in Firefox 142; confirm the current state in Firefox's release notes before saying it has happened. The 100 ms figure is Mozilla's median.

    // REVOCATION AND LIFETIMES 04
    ACME: proving you control a name
    
  
    
    
    
      
    
        
        
        
      
    
      
    
      
    
        
        
        
        
      
    
    
    
    
    
      
    
      
      
        
    • Account key: your client makes a key pair that identifies it to the CA.
      • 
        
    • The CA sends a random token. You answer with token + "." + your key's thumbprint.
      • 
        
    • That binds the token to your account: someone who sees the token can't answer for you.
      • 
        
    • Renew when the CA's renewal information (ARI) says, or about two-thirds of the way through the lifetime.
      • 
      
    
      
    Sources: RFC 8555 (March 2019) · RFC 9773 (ARI, June 2025)
    
    
    
  
    
  
    ~5 min
    • ShowStart an order: the page makes a real P-256 account key, a random token, and the real key authorization (token, a dot, and the base64url SHA-256 of the key's JWK). Publish it correctly and validate: the CA's check passes. Publish another account's answer or nothing: it fails. Choose a wildcard name like *.shop.example with HTTP-01: that can't work, because a file on one web server can't prove control of a whole zone, so wildcards need DNS-01.
    • SayACME, RFC 8555, is what lets a machine do the proof-of-control that a person used to do by email. For DNS-01 the TXT value is the SHA-256 of the key authorization, so the secret itself never appears in DNS. The CA, the client and the challenge are simulated here; no request goes out.
    • TipCheck: the key-authorization and DNS-01 construction follows RFC 8555 §8; the RFC's text was not read in full here. The "two-thirds of the lifetime" rule is Let's Encrypt's advice for clients without ARI.
    • Next"Checkpoint three."
    

    


    
  
    // CHECKPOINT 3 · 5 MIN
    Pause and discuss
    
  
    
    
    Talk with a neighbour for a couple of minutes, then we share answers.
    
    
      
      
    1. Why are shorter certificate lifetimes considered safer than better revocation?
      Hint
      What does revocation depend on, and what does expiry depend on?
      Answer
      Revocation needs a check that reaches every client, quickly and privately, and clients have often let connections continue when it fails. Expiry works by itself: a short lifetime caps the damage with no one needing to check anything.
      2. 
      
    2. Why does ACME make you answer with the token plus a hash of your account key, not just the token?
      Hint
      Who else can see the token?
      Answer
      A token alone could be copied by anyone who sees it. Combined with your account key's thumbprint, only the account that asked can produce the right answer.
      3. 
      
    3. You hand-renew 200 certificates a year and the limit is about to halve. What's your first step?
      Hint
      You can't automate what you can't list.
      Answer
      Make an inventory: every certificate, its owner and expiry. Then automate the easy ones with ACME, and put alerts on the rest.
      4. 
    
    
  
    
  
    5 min
    • ShowPairs talk for 2 minutes, then share. The timer starts by itself.
    

    



    
  
    // RUNNING YOUR OWN 01
    Internal CAs: when, and how to keep one safe
    
  
    
    
    
      
    Do you need your own CA?
    
      
      
        
    • Probably yes for devices and services only you reach: private names, service-to-service (mutual TLS) and device identity.
      • 
        
    • Probably no for anything the public visits. Use a public CA and ACME. Even internal hostnames you own can use public certificates with DNS-01.
      • 
        
    • Either way, a root you add to devices can vouch for any site on them. Treat its key like the keys to the building.
      • 
      
    
      
    
    
    
    
    
      
    
        
        
        
        
        
        
        
        
      
    
    
    
  
    
  
    ~4 min
    • SayAn internal CA is the same machine you've seen all talk, with you in every role. So all the lessons apply to you: keep the root offline, use intermediates, ship logs elsewhere, constrain by name if your clients honour it, and practise replacing an intermediate before you have to. The DigiNotar report's lessons are your checklist too.
    • ShowTick what your organisation already does. The boxes are saved in this browser only.
    • TipCheck: name-constraint support varies by client and platform; test your own clients before relying on it. Tools people use include Active Directory Certificate Services, Vault's PKI engine, step-ca and EJBCA, but pick on your needs: none is endorsed here.

    // RUNNING YOUR OWN 02
    Inventory and expiry alerts: boring on purpose
    
  
    
    
    
      
    
        
      
    
      
    
      
    
    
    
    
    
      
    Equifax, 2017A traffic-inspection device's certificate expired on 31 January 2016, so it inspected nothing for 19 months (the GAO counted ten). Renewing it on 29 July 2017 revealed the attack.
    
      
    O2 and Ericsson, 2018On 6 December 2018 an expired certificate in Ericsson's core-network software knocked out O2's data service and networks that rely on it. Press estimates: about 32 million people; Ericsson said "a limited number".
    
      
      
        
    • List every certificate with an owner, appliances and vendor software too. Find strays by watching CT and scanning your network.
      • 
        
    • Alert early, to a group, and test the alert.
      • 
      
    
      
    Sources: House Oversight, Equifax report · The Register (Ericsson's statement) · crt.sh
    
    
    
  
    
  
    ~4 min
    • ShowThe table is a made-up inventory with dates relative to today. Move the slider: more certificates light up as "soon", but notice the two with no owner: nobody will act on an alert about them. Then slide to zero: an alert on the day it expires is just the outage report.
    • SayBoth stories are ordinary: a certificate on something nobody thought of as a certificate holder. In Equifax's case the House report says renewing it let the monitoring see the attack, which had been running for weeks. An inventory finds those before they find you.
    • TipCheck: the GAO counted ten months and the House report 19 (the House report says its documents show 31 January 2016). The O2 figure of 32 million is a press estimate (The Register states it without a source; Ericsson called it "a limited number of customers"); say "millions", not a precise number.

    // RUNNING YOUR OWN 03
    Habits that keep PKI boring
    
  
    
    
    
      
    1 · InventoryEvery certificate, its owner, its expiry, and where it lives.
    
      
    2 · AutomateIssue and renew with ACME. Monitor the automation too.
    
      
    3 · Alert earlyTo a team, not a person. Test the alert at least once.
    
      
    4 · Watch CTKnow every certificate issued for your names, including ones you didn't ask for.
    
      
    5 · Drill the swapBe able to get certificates from a different CA in a day. Distrust won't wait for your roadmap.
    
      
    6 · Guard rootsOffline roots, constrained intermediates, logs kept elsewhere.
    
    
    
  
    
  
    ~2 min
    • SayNone of these are clever. They're what turns a CA incident into an inconvenience instead of an emergency: you know what you have, you can replace it, and you hear about problems early.
