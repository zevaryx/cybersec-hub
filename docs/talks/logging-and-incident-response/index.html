// WHY THIS TALK

Finding out sooner, and knowing what happened

52%of intrusions Mandiant investigated in 2025 were first noticed by the victim (43% in 2024).
34%were reported by an outside party, and in 14% the attacker announced themselves.
14 daysglobal median dwell time, up from 11. Espionage and North Korean IT-worker cases had a median of 122 days.

Logs are how you notice, and the only way to learn afterwards what happened.

  • You can only investigate what was recorded, and kept long enough.
  • Attackers delete or switch off logs. Mandiant's own advice: forward them somewhere else, immediately.
  • An alert nobody looks at is not detection.
Summer 2023: the log State could seeThe US State Department was first to spot an intrusion into Exchange Online mailboxes, using a custom alert on a log called MailItemsAccessed. That log needed a premium licence, which few victims had, and those who bought it afterwards couldn't look back at earlier activity. The Cyber Safety Review Board recommended that security logging stop being a paid add-on, with customer access logs available at no extra charge and kept for at least six months by default (recommendations 4 and 10).

The point isn't blame. It's that the log you need is decided long before the incident.

Sources: M-Trends 2026, executive edition (investigations 1 Jan to 31 Dec 2025) · CSRB report (20 Mar 2024), §1.3.1, §1.3.2.2, recs 4 and 10

~3 min

  • SayThree numbers from M-Trends. About half the time, the victim noticed first. A third of the time someone outside told them. And the median attacker had been there two weeks. Then one story, told carefully: in 2023 the only reason one agency caught a major intrusion was a log most victims didn't have.
  • SayCareful framing: logging was sold as a premium add-on, and the Board's recommendation was that cloud providers make security logging a core part of the service. It's about where the decision gets made, not about customers doing something wrong.
  • TipRansomware cases look different: the attacker notified the victim 44% of the time, internal detection 41%, and an outside party 15%. Mandiant's guidance for the long-dwell cases: standard 90-day retention is insufficient, forward logs to central long-term storage, and alert on unexpected log termination.
  • CheckAll M-Trends figures are from the executive edition (PDF, read in full for these passages): 52% internal vs 43%; 34% external vs 43%; 14% adversary; ransomware 44/41/15; median dwell 14 days vs 11; espionage and DPRK IT-worker median 122 days; BRICKSTORM average 393 days. The CSRB details are from the report text: State detected on 15 and 16 June 2023; 22 organisations and over 500 individuals affected; Microsoft couldn't give historical logs to customers who didn't already hold the premium licence.

// LOGGING THAT HELPS 01

Every log line should answer five questions

WhoThe account or service, not only an address
WhatThe action, in words a tired person can read
WhenA full date and time, with a zone, from a clock that's right
Where fromThe source address or device, and which system wrote the line
OutcomeDid it work? A denied request matters as much as an allowed one

Based on the event-log baseline in ASD's ACSC and CISA, Best practices for event logging and threat detection (21 Aug 2024), which cites OMB M-21-31's list: timestamp, event type, device, session, source and destination, user ID, command run, a unique event ID.


        

        

      

      

      

    

  

  
~3 min
  • ShowThree versions of the same event. "Failed login" tells you something happened. The syslog line is far better, and still has no year or time zone. The structured line answers everything, and a machine can search it. Type your own line in the box and watch which question you've left unanswered.
  • SayThe linter is a toy: it matches shapes. A line can pass it and still be wrong. What matters is the habit: when you add a log message, ask whether a stranger could use it at 3 a.m.
  • TipStructured logs (key=value or JSON) beat free text: you can filter on user= without writing a parser. ACSC's note: "Where possible, all data should be formatted as key-value-pairs to allow for easier extraction", and millisecond timestamps are ideal.

// LOGGING THAT HELPS 03
One clock, one format, one place

  

    

      

        

      

      
    
      
    
    
    
    
    
      
      
        
    • Record in UTC, in one format: 2026-11-09T03:12:44.649Z. No zones to convert, no daylight saving.
      • 
        
    • Sync every clock to a trusted source (more than one, in case one fails), and check that they stay in sync.
      • 
        
    • Classic syslog (RFC 3164) timestamps have no year and no zone. RFC 5424 uses RFC 3339 timestamps, with a full date and zone.
      • 
        
    • Send logs to one central place, so a deleted local log isn't the only copy.
      • 
      
    
      
    Sources: ACSC/CISA logging guide (timestamp consistency) · RFC 5424 · RFC 3164 · OWASP Logging Cheat Sheet
    
    
    
  
    
  
    ~2 min
    • ShowSix events from one intrusion, sorted by whatever their logs say. "As written": a file is copied before anyone logs in, and the VPN session appears five hours early. "Fix the time zones" repairs one source. "Also fix the slow clock" (the file server's clock is seven minutes slow) finally gives the true order. Same events, three stories.
    • SayA timeline is the first thing an incident needs, and clock errors make it lie. The fix is boring: one time standard, one format, synchronised sources, one central store. And while you're at it, note which hosts are known to drift.
    • TipOWASP's cheat sheet suggests, where you can't fix a clock, to measure the offset or record a confidence level in the event timestamp. RFC 5424 has a timeQuality field for exactly that.
    • CheckThe events, hosts and offsets are invented. ACSC: "Using Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) has the advantage of no time zones as well as no daylight savings, and is the preferred time standard", ISO 8601 with milliseconds, and multiple accurate time sources. RFC 3164 timestamps are local time, "Mmm dd hh:mm:ss"; RFC 5424 (which obsoletes 3164) derives its TIMESTAMP from RFC 3339.

    // LOGGING THAT HELPS 05
    Tamper-evident logs: a hash chain
    
  
    
    
    
      
    
        
    
        
      
    
      
    #entryprevioushash
    
      
    
        
        
        
        
        
        
      
    
      
    
      
    
    
    
    
    
      
      
        
    • Each entry's hash covers its text and the hash before it. Change anything and every later link breaks.
      • 
        
    • An attacker who can write the file can recompute the whole chain. Only a copy of the latest hash kept elsewhere (an anchor), or a secret key they don't have, exposes that.
      • 
        
    • A chain shows what was changed or removed from the middle. It can't prove the end is complete without an anchor.
      • 
        
    • The simplest defence is still forwarding logs off the machine as they're written.
      • 
      
    
      
    Same idea, bigger scaleCertificate Transparency logs are tamper-evident for the same reason, with signed heads that anyone can check. See PKI in Depth.
    
    
    
  
    
  
    Sources: Schneier & Kelsey, Secure Logs on Untrusted Machines (USENIX Security 1998) · ACSC/CISA logging guide · OWASP Logging Cheat Sheet
    
  
    ~3 min
    • ShowWith the anchor ticked, try each attack. "Edit entry 3": the entry itself fails. "Edit and fix its hash": entry 4's link breaks. "Rewrite the whole chain": on a plain chain it is internally perfect, and only the anchor exposes it. Switch to Keyed (HMAC) and try again: without the key, the attacker's recomputed hashes don't verify. "Cut off the last two": still a valid chain; only the anchor notices. Untick the anchor to see what disappears.
    • SayThis is the real SHA-256 and HMAC from the Crypto 101 code, applied to a six-line log. Three lessons: a chain makes edits visible; it needs something the attacker can't rewrite (a stored head, a key); and none of it replaces shipping logs off the box.
    • TipIn real systems the anchor might be a periodically signed hash sent to a separate service, or written to append-only (write-once) storage. The ACSC guide recommends cryptographic verification of integrity in transit and at rest, and keeping logs in a separate, segmented network with tight delete and modify permissions.
    • CheckTeaching code: the demo chains by hashing "previous hash | text", and the HMAC variant keys each link. It is not a production audit-log design. Schneier and Kelsey's 1998 paper describes a more careful scheme (forward-secure keys and hash chains) so that entries written before a compromise can't be altered undetected.
    

    


    
  
    // LOGGING THAT HELPS 06
    Keep secrets and personal data out of logs
    
  
    
    
    
      
    
      
    
      
    
    
    
    
    
      
      
        
    • Don't log: passwords, access tokens, session IDs, encryption keys, connection strings, payment card data, and sensitive personal data. Remove, mask, hash or encrypt them.
      • 
        
    • Encode what users type before logging it, so a newline can't forge a second line. Prefer structured logs.
      • 
        
    • Log events, not contents: "bob exported 1,200 rows", not the rows.
      • 
        
    • Protect the logs like the data: restrict who can read them, and review that access. Logs often outlive and out-spread the systems they describe.
      • 
      
    
      
    Source: OWASP Logging Cheat Sheet ("Data to exclude", "Log Injection") · ACSC/CISA logging guide
    
    
    
  
    
  
    ~2 min
    • ShowSix lines: four to flag, two that are fine. Make the room call each before you reveal it. The failed login with a password is the one that surprises people: a failed attempt very often holds the real password with a single typo.
    • SayLogs are a second copy of your data with weaker protection and a much wider audience. The cheapest fix is at the point of writing: don't put the secret in the line. The log-injection example matters for the earlier slide too: a forged line is a way to put false evidence in your own logs.
    • TipOWASP's list of data to exclude: session IDs (a hash can track a session's events), access tokens, authentication passwords, encryption keys and other primary secrets, sensitive personal data, bank account or card data, database connection strings, application source code. And "Never log data unless it is legally sanctioned."
    • CheckThe lines are invented; the card number is a standard test number. Your country's rules on personal data in logs vary: ask your privacy owner.

    // DETECTION 01
    Alerts, dashboards and hunts do different jobs
    
  
    
    
    
      
    AlertA person must look now. It needs an owner, a first step, and a rate people can sustain. An alert nobody acts on is noise with a pager.
    
      
    DashboardTrends and context, read when someone chooses to. Good for "is this normal?", poor for waking anyone up.
    
      
    HuntA person asks the data a question nobody has written a rule for. ACSC and Mandiant both recommend routine hunting, especially for attackers using normal admin tools.
    
    
    
    
    
      
      
        
    • Before an alert goes live, answer: Who gets it? What do they do first? How often does it fire, and how many are real? What does it miss?
      • 
        
    • Write the first look next to the rule, so the person on call at 3 a.m. isn't guessing.
      • 
        
    • Review each alert on a schedule. Retire or retune the ones that never lead to action.
      • 
      
    
      
    "Big Yellow Taxi"State's custom alert on mailbox access had fired false positives before. The CSRB notes that analysts investigated anyway, triaged it as a moderate-level event, and contacted Microsoft that same day. A noisy rule that people still trust and follow up is worth more than a quiet one nobody reads. (It shares a name with a Joni Mitchell song about not knowing what you've got till it's gone, which is also true of logs.)
    
    
    
    
    Sources: CSRB report, §1.3.1 · ACSC/CISA logging guide (detection strategy, threat hunting) · M-Trends 2026
    
  
    
  
    ~2 min
    • SayThree tools for three jobs. People build dashboards and call them detection, and nobody looks at the dashboard at 3 a.m. An alert is a promise that a human will look, now, and that promise has to be one you can keep.
    • SayThe State Department example is worth a second look: their alert on a log that most victims couldn't see had produced false alarms before, they investigated anyway, and that's how a major intrusion was found. The CSRB also notes that few organisations analysed that voluminous log in depth: buying the log wasn't enough, the custom alerting was the difference.
    • CheckThe CSRB text: State's SOC "observed multiple alerts from the 'Big Yellow Taxi' custom alert rule" on 16 June 2023; "State had seen false positive Big Yellow Taxi detections in the past—State investigated these incidents and ultimately determined that the alert indicated malicious activity"; triaged "as a moderate-level event". The CSRB doesn't say why the rule was named that, so the song reference is only about the name.
    

    


    
  
    // DETECTION 02
    Good first alerts
    
  
    
    
    
    
    
  
    
  
    Sources: ACSC/CISA logging guide (examples to alert on) · Microsoft, Events to Monitor · AWS CloudTrail StopLogging · ATT&CK T1078, T1136, T1110.003, T1685.002, T1685.005, T1486
    
  
    ~4 min
    • ShowClick each alert. For each: what it catches, the log it needs (tie back to "what to log first"), when it's usually a false alarm, and the first look. The same five show up in the explorer's attack: spray, sign-in from somewhere new, new admin, logging off, and bulk file reads.
    • SayStart with a handful you understand. Mass file changes depends on how fast it grows. Impossible travel and sprays are often daytime queue unless an account also succeeded.

    // DETECTION 03
    Tuning an alert, and alert fatigue
    
  
    
    
    
      
    
        
    
      
    
      
    
        
        
      
    
      
    
      
    
      
    
        
    1 of 3real attacks caught
    
        
    0false alerts this week
    
        
    0 hanalyst time on false alerts
    
      
    
      
    
      
    
      
    
    
    
    
    
      
      
        
    • The week has three real attacks buried in ordinary failures: a spray, a brute force on one account, and a spray slowed to six hours.
      • 
        
    • Per-account rules see brute force. A spray tries each account once, so only a per-address rule sees it. In practice you need both.
      • 
        
    • A long window catches the slow spray, and a shared kiosk can look like one too. Add context (known scanners, kiosk addresses) instead of raising thresholds until it's blind.
      • 
        
    • Count what matters: how many alerts led to action, and what the rule misses.
      • 
      
    
      
    Sources: ATT&CK T1110.003, Password Spraying · ACSC/CISA logging guide ("a single IP address attempting to authenticate as multiple different users") · simulated week
    
    
    
  
    
  
    ~5 min
    • ShowStart with "One account", 15 failures in 10 minutes: quiet, and it catches only the brute force. Lower it to 3: dozens of false alerts, from people forgetting their password and a broken service account, and still no spray. Switch to "One address": 10 different accounts in 15 minutes catches the fast spray quietly. Widen to three hours to catch the slow one, and a kiosk starts to look like an attacker.
    • SayThere are two separate lessons. One: the rule has to match the behaviour (a spray is about the address, not the account). Two: thresholds trade false alarms for blind spots, and every false alarm costs a person's attention. Alert fatigue isn't weakness: it's what happens when the cost of looking exceeds the value.
    • AskWhat would you add to the rule so the kiosk stops alerting? Answer: exclude or label known shared addresses, or require a successful sign-in after the failures. The aim is context that separates the cases, not a higher number.
    • CheckThe week is simulated: 300 accounts, roughly 1,100 failed sign-ins, fixed so everyone sees the same numbers. The 10 minutes per false alert and the quiet/heavy/fatigue labels are invented, only to give the numbers a scale. Defaults: per-account 15 in 10 minutes catches the brute force with zero false alerts; per-address 10 accounts in 15 minutes catches the spray; 10 accounts in 3 hours catches both sprays with one false alert (the kiosk). No single rule catches all three: "Both rules" (the per-address rule you set, plus 15 in 10 minutes per account) with a 3-hour window catches all three with one false alert.
    

    


    
  
    // DETECTION 04
    ATT&CK as a map of what you can't see
    
  
    
    
    
      
    
      
    
      
    
    
    
    
    
      
      
        
    • ATT&CK is a catalogue of attacker behaviour. Use it to ask "which of these would I see?", not as a score to maximise.
      • 
        
    • Coverage isn't detection. A technique is covered when the data exists, a rule uses it, the rule was tested, and someone knows what to do.
      • 
        
    • Log tampering lives under Defense Impairment, a tactic added in v19: T1685.002 (cloud logs) and T1685.005 (Windows event logs).
      • 
        
    • Reconnaissance and resource development mostly happen outside your systems. Logs can't see them.
      • 
      
    
      
    Sources: ATT&CK v19 (28 Apr 2026) release notes · Enterprise tactics (v20.1, 13 Oct 2026). The coverage map is a simplification.
    
    
    
  
    
  
    ~4 min
    • ShowStart with the three sources from earlier: most of the map has something, and Command and Control, Exfiltration and Discovery are thin. Add network and DNS. Then tick everything: only Reconnaissance and Resource Development stay dark, because most of that happens before the attacker touches your systems.
    • SayThe map doesn't tell you what you've detected: it tells you where you can't see anything. Use it to choose the next log source, then pick the techniques that matter for your own threats and write and test a detection for each.
    • CheckThe tactic list is Enterprise ATT&CK as of v20.1 (13 October 2026): 15 tactics, including Stealth (TA0005) and Defense Impairment (TA0112), which replaced Defense Evasion in v19 (28 April 2026). The mapping of sources to tactics is an illustrative judgement, not MITRE data: ATT&CK has its own data-source model. Old technique IDs (T1562.x, T1070.x for log tampering) were revoked and replaced by T1685.x in v19; use the current IDs if you cite them.

    Revision 3 (April 2025) treats Govern, Identify and Protect as preparation that supports response, and learning as continuous: lessons are shared as they appear, not saved for the end.
    
      
    
    
    
    
    Sources: NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3 (April 2025), §2.1 and Table 1 · CISA incident and vulnerability response playbooks · NCSC incident management
    
  
    
  
    ~3 min
    • ShowClick through the six steps. Prepare, detect, contain, eradicate, recover, learn is the teaching version of the classic model. The table on the right shows how NIST's 2025 revision recasts those phases as the six functions of the Cybersecurity Framework 2.0.
    • SayTwo points. First, the lifecycle isn't a straight line: new evidence sends you back to analysis, and the CISA playbook says so ("if new signs of compromise are found, return to the technical analysis step to re-scope"). Second, NIST's newer model says preparation and learning aren't phases at the edges: Govern, Identify and Protect are everyday risk management, and lessons feed back continuously.
    • CheckNIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3 (final, April 2025) supersedes Rev. 2 (August 2012). Its Table 1 maps Preparation to Govern, Identify (all categories) and Protect; Detection & Analysis to Detect and Identify (Improvement); Containment, Eradication & Recovery to Respond, Recover and Improvement; Post-Incident Activity to Improvement. The CISA playbooks still use the Rev. 2 phase names. Splitting Containment and Eradication on this slide is a teaching choice. The NCSC collection is dated 2019 (v1.0) and is still a good practical read.
    

    


    
  
    // RESPONDING 02
    Roles: someone has to be the one not typing
    
  
    
    
    
      
    
        
    Incident leadDecides, keeps the big picture, assigns work. Doesn't run the commands.
    
        
    Technical leadDirects the hands-on investigation and containment. The only people changing systems.
    
        
    Communications leadUpdates staff, leadership and, with counsel, outsiders. One voice.
    
        
    ScribeKeeps the timeline: UTC times, decisions, who did what. Makes handovers possible.
    
      
    
      
    Also consulted: legal counsel, your insurer, HR, and an executive sponsor. In a small team, one person wears several hats, but write down who is wearing which.
    
      
    Sources: Google SRE book, Managing Incidents (command, operations, communications, planning) · NCSC incident management (response team roles)
    
    
    
    
    
      
    Whose job is it?
    
      
    
      
    
      
    
    
    
  
    
  
    ~3 min
    • SayThe classic incident failure isn't lack of skill, it's everyone doing everything: the best engineer is also the one explaining things to the CEO and nobody wrote down what happened at 03:41. Four hats, even if you only have three people. The lead's job is to not be the one with their hands on the keyboard.
    • ShowSix tasks: pick the role whose job each is. Reveal shows the usual owner and why. Some have more than one reasonable answer: that's a good discussion.
    • CheckGoogle's SRE chapter, for reliability incidents, names incident command, operations (only they change the system), communications and planning. The scribe role is our addition from common practice: Google's "keep the incident document accurate and up to date" is part of communications. Role names vary by organisation.
    

    


    
  
    // RESPONDING 03
    Containing, eradicating, and keeping the evidence
    
  
    
    
    
      
    ActionStops themKeeps evidenceTips them offHurts the business
    
      
    
    
    
    
    
      
    Contain ≠ eradicateContain: stop it getting worse (isolate, disable, revoke). Often temporary. Eradicate: remove their way in and every foothold (patch, rebuild, rotate every secret they could have seen). If new signs appear, you re-scope.
    
      
    Collect the volatile firstRFC 3227's example order: registers and cache; routing and ARP tables, processes, memory; temporary files; disk; remote logs; configuration and topology; archives. Its warning: "Don't shutdown until you've completed evidence collection."
    
      
    That RFC dates from 2002 and was written for one machine. With ransomware spreading, isolating from the network usually beats powering off: it stops the damage and keeps memory. CISA's playbook lists capturing forensic images among key containment activities.
    
      
    Sources: RFC 3227 (Feb 2002, BCP 55) · CISA playbooks, Containment
    
    
    
  
    
  
    ~4 min
    • ShowClick each action. There's no free lunch: the options that stop the attacker fastest tend to cost evidence or business time. The table's dots are judgement, not measurement. Revoking sessions and isolating a host are usually the best first moves; pulling the plug costs the most evidence.
    • SayContainment is about the smallest action that stops the harm. Eradication is a bigger job: if you remove one foothold and miss another, they're back. CISA's playbook warns about "tipping off" the adversary and says to scope containment to all related activity. And save the evidence before you change things: logs, memory if you can, a disk image if it matters.
    • CheckRFC 3227 section 2.1 gives the order of volatility (read in full) and section 2.2 the line "Don't shutdown until you've completed evidence collection. Much evidence may be lost and the attacker may have altered the startup/shutdown scripts/services to destroy evidence." The CISA playbook's key containment activities include isolating systems, capturing forensic images, blocking and logging unauthorised access, changing admin passwords and rotating keys and service secrets. It describes watching an actor in a sandbox as limited to advanced SOCs with mature capabilities.
    

    


    
  
    // RESPONDING 04
    Telling people: inside, customers, regulators
    
  
    
    
    
      
      
        
    • Inside: short, regular updates with a time for the next one. Say what you know, what you don't, what you're doing, and what you need from people.
      • 
        
    • Outside: one voice, with counsel. Facts only. "We don't know yet" is fine when it comes with a plan.
      • 
        
    • Out of band: if your email or chat may be compromised, coordinate somewhere else (a phone bridge, a separate tenant). CISA's playbook includes out-of-band coordination in preparation.
      • 
        
    • Write down the time you became aware. Several clocks start there.
      • 
      
    
      
    Internal update templateStatus, as of [UTC time]. Known: … Unknown: … Doing: … Need from you: … Next update: [time].
    
    
    
    
    
      
    ExampleThe clock
    
        
    EU GDPR, Art. 33Tell the supervisory authority "without undue delay and, where feasible, not later than 72 hours" after becoming aware of a personal data breach (unless unlikely to risk people's rights). Information may follow in stages.
    
        
    US SEC, Form 8-K Item 1.05Public companies: generally within four business days of determining that an incident is material.
    
        
    US CIRCIAProposed: 72 hours for covered incidents, 24 hours for ransom payments. Not final: CISA says reporting isn't required until the final rule takes effect.
    
      
    
      
    YOURS Your country, sector, contracts and insurance policy may all set clocks. Find them before an incident, and ask counsel.
    
      
    Sources: GDPR (EUR-Lex), Art. 33 · SEC fact sheet · CISA CIRCIA (checked 9 Nov 2026) · EDPB guidelines 9/2022
    
    
    
  
    
  
    ~3 min
    • SayThese are examples, not legal advice and not a list. The lesson is that notification clocks exist, they start at different moments (awareness, materiality, payment), and you don't want to discover them mid-incident. Find your contracts, your insurance policy's notice requirement, and your regulator's own page now. A good internal update is the same every time: known, unknown, doing, need, next update.
    • CheckGDPR Art. 33(1), (2) and (4) read from the regulation's text: 72 hours "where feasible", reasons required for delay, processors tell controllers "without undue delay", phased information allowed. EDPB: awareness depends on the circumstances, and an initial investigation should start as soon as possible. SEC fact sheet: "generally within four business days" of the materiality determination, which must itself be made "without unreasonable delay following discovery"; disclosure may be delayed in limited cases. CIRCIA: CISA's page (9 Nov 2026) says the rule is still proposed, citing the April 2024 proposal, with 72 hours for covered incidents and 24 hours for ransom payments, and that reports aren't required yet. Other secondary sources describe a final rule going to review; I didn't confirm that from a primary source, so check the page on the day.
    

    


    
  
    // RESPONDING 05
    Blameless reviews, and the numbers that matter
    
  
    
    
    
      
      
        
    • Hold a review within days, while it's fresh. Walk the timeline, in UTC, from the scribe's notes.
      • 
        
    • Ask what we knew when, and why each decision made sense at the time. "Human error" is where an investigation stops, not where it ends.
      • 
        
    • Leave with changes that have an owner and a date: a log source added, an alert retuned, a runbook written.
      • 
        
    • Share lessons as you find them, not only at the end.
      • 
      
    
      
    Why blameless?"You can't 'fix' people, but you can fix systems and processes." And where blame prevails, "people will not bring issues to light for fear of punishment", so the next incident starts with less information. Google's SRE book, Postmortem Culture.
    
    
    
    
    
      
    
        
    time to detectfrom first attacker action to someone noticing
    
        
    time to containfrom noticing to the harm stopping
    
        
    time to recoverfrom containment to normal
    
      
    
      
      
        
    • Record all three for every incident, and look at the median and the worst case.
      • 
        
    • For scale: Mandiant's 2025 median dwell time was 14 days, and 52% of the time the victim noticed first.
      • 
        
    • Track who found it: you, a vendor, a customer, the attacker. The trend is the real score.
      • 
      
    
      
    Sources: Google SRE book, Postmortem Culture · NIST SP 800-61 r3 (continuous improvement) · M-Trends 2026
    
    
    
  
    
  
    ~2 min
    • SayBlameless doesn't mean nobody is accountable. It means the review asks what made the mistake easy and the detection slow, because the next person will be just as human. If people fear punishment, they'll hide the near-miss that would have saved you next time.
    • TipA useful review question: "what would have made this easier to spot or stop?" The answers are almost always logs, alerts, access limits or documentation.
    • CheckThe Google SRE quotes are from the "Postmortem Culture" chapter ("Best Practice: Avoid Blame and Keep It Constructive" and "Google's Postmortem Philosophy"). The measures are common practice; names vary (MTTD, MTTC, MTTR). Dwell time and the share found internally are M-Trends 2026 for 2025 investigations.
    

    


    
  
    // CHECKPOINT 3 · 5 MIN
    Pause and discuss
    
  
    
    
    Talk with a neighbour for a couple of minutes, then we share answers.
    
    
      
      
    1. What's the difference between containing an incident and eradicating it?
      Hint
      One stops it getting worse. The other makes sure it can't come back.
      Answer
      Containing stops the harm now (isolate, revoke). Eradicating removes their access and root cause. Contain without eradicating and they return.
      2. 
      
    2. Why should post-incident reviews be blameless?
      Hint
      What happens to information when people fear punishment?
      Answer
      Blame makes people hide near-misses, so you learn less. You can fix systems; the next person repeats the mistake if the system allows it.
      3. 
      
    3. The CEO asks "was customer data taken?" and you don't know. What do you say?
      Hint
      Truthful, useful, and not a guess.
      Answer
      What you know, what you don't, what you're checking, and when you'll update them. Involve counsel and your insurer early: clocks may be running.
      4. 
    
    
  
    
  
    5 min
    • ShowPairs talk for 2 minutes, then share. The timer starts by itself.
    

    



    
  
    // TABLETOP EXERCISE 01
    How the exercise works
    
  
    
    
    
      
      
        
    • Work in small groups, with roles: lead, technical, comms, scribe.
      • 
        
    • Each round shows a situation. Discuss for about three minutes, agree what you'd do, then choose.
      • 
        
    • Two surprises are drawn at random, like real life.
      • 
        
    • There's no winning. The meters on the side are a prompt for discussion, and invented. What matters is what you talked about.
      • 
        
    • A scribe notes each decision with its time, because the debrief starts there.
      • 
      
    
      
    Run your own: NCSC Exercise in a Box (free, UK) and CISA Tabletop Exercise Packages (scenarios, slides, an after-action report template).
    
    
    
    
    
      
    Pick a scenario
    
      
    
      
    
      
    
    
  
    
  
    ~2 min
    • SayPick a scenario for the room. Form groups of three or four and hand out the four hats. Be explicit that the numbers are a conversation starter: a high score isn't the goal, and an exercise where nothing goes wrong teaches nothing. The scribe's notes feed the debrief.
    • ShowChoose "3 a.m., a new country" for a group that knows identity and cloud, or "The Monday-morning note" for a general IT audience. "Deal the surprises" draws two at random from six, using the browser's secure random generator, and moves to the exercise.
    • CheckBoth scenarios and all consequences are invented. The meters (reach, evidence, disruption, clarity) are qualitative and not from a standard. The NCSC resource is described by the NCSC as free and for organisations of all sizes. CISA's packages (undated page) include scenarios, discussion questions, a slide deck, a feedback form and an after-action report template.
    

    


    
  
    // TABLETOP EXERCISE 02
    The exercise
    
  
    
    
    
      
    Round 1 of 3
    
      
    
      
    
      
    
      
    
    
    
    
    
      
    
      
    
    
    
  
    
  
    ~15 min
    • ShowRead the situation aloud and start the three-minute timer. Groups discuss, then call out their choice: click it and read the consequence. Each option teaches something, and none is a trap with one right answer. Rounds two and three each bring a random surprise. At the end, the summary lists every decision with its reasoning.
    • AskAfter each choice: "who would you have called first, and what would you have written in the log?" and "what did you need to know that you didn't?" The second question usually points to a missing log source or a missing contact.
    • TipIf you're short of time, skip the timers and have the room vote. If you've got longer, make groups defend their choices against each other. Keep a note of every "we'd need to find out": that's the exercise's real output.
    • CheckFacts used in the consequences: RFC 3227 (collect volatile evidence before shutting down); CISA's playbook (containment, "tipping off", watching an actor is for advanced SOCs, and re-scoping when new signs appear); the GDPR and SEC clock starts (awareness; materiality); NIST on continuous improvement. The scenarios, injects, and effects are invented.
