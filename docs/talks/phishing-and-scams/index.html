// WHY THIS TALK

Scams stopped being clumsy

The old advice was "look for spelling mistakes". AI writes clean text in any language, clones a voice from a short clip, and can fake a face on a video call.

  • Scammers aim at people, because people are easier to talk round than software.
  • Anyone can be fooled, especially when tired, busy or worried. It's a matter of timing, not intelligence.
  • The good news: the tells are about what is being asked and how, and that hasn't changed.
The scaleUS consumers reported losing more than $12.5 billion to fraud in 2024, up 25% on 2023. Reported losses; the real total is higher.
Arup, 2024A finance worker in Hong Kong joined a video call with the "CFO" and colleagues. Everyone else on it was a deepfake. After the call, 15 transfers totalling about HK$200 million (about US$25 million) went out.
Family emergency callsThe US FTC warns that a short audio clip of someone's voice can be enough to clone it for a fake "I'm in trouble" call to their family.

Sources: FTC, March 2025 · SCMP · FTC family emergency scams

~3 min

  • SayThree numbers' worth of context. The FTC figure is what people reported; plenty of victims never report. Arup is the one to remember: even a trained finance worker on a live video call with faces they recognised. Ask yourself how you'd have checked.
  • TipArup confirmed it was the victim in May 2024; the US dollar figure varies by outlet ($25-26 million), so say "about".

// HOW SCAMS WORK ON PEOPLE 01

Five pressure tricks

UrgencyYour account will be closed in 2 hours.Takes away time to think. Counter: slow down. Urgent plus unexpected is the warning.
AuthorityThis is your bank's fraud team / the police / your CEO.We obey titles. Counter: titles are free to claim. Contact them yourself.
FearSuspicious activity. A warrant has been issued.Fear shrinks our thinking. Counter: real organisations don't threaten you into paying.
SecrecyDon't tell anyone. Don't call the branch.Cuts you off from checking. Counter: "keep this secret" is a reason to tell someone.
RewardYou've won. A refund is waiting.Greed and hope also switch off caution. Counter: you can't win a contest you didn't enter.

THE ASK Look past the story at what you're asked to do: pay in gift cards or crypto, read out a code, install something, sign in, keep it secret. That's the part to verify.

~4 min

  • SayEvery scam uses some mix of these, in any channel and any language. They work by making you feel instead of think. Notice the effect on you: if your heart rate just went up, that's the signal.
  • AskWhich of the five is hardest to resist for you, and when? No wrong answers: it's often "authority" at work and "fear" at home.
  • TipFrame it kindly: these tricks work on everyone. We're not here to feel clever, but to have a plan for the moment we feel rushed.
  • Next"Let's see how many of them one message can carry."

// HOW SCAMS WORK ON PEOPLE 03

Where scams arrive

EmailphishingFake invoices, "account problem", shared-document links.
Text messagessmishingParcel fees, toll or fine notices, "wrong number" chats that turn friendly.
Phone callsvishing"Your bank", "tech support", "the tax office", a relative in trouble. Caller ID can be faked.
Chat and socialDMsA friend's hacked account, fake jobs, romance and investment "opportunities".
QR codesquishingA sticker over a real code on a poster or meter, or one inside an email. Your phone hides the address by default.
Search and adslook-alike sitesA paid ad above the real result, leading to a copy of the site.

DELIVERY CHANGES, SCRIPTS DON'T The channel is new every year. The five tricks and the "ask" aren't.

Source for QR tampering: FBI IC3 alert, January 2022

~4 min

  • SayThe odd names are worth knowing because they appear in news and bank warnings: smishing is text, vishing is voice, quishing is QR codes. The thing they share: someone you didn't expect to hear from asks you to do something. Even "wrong number" texts are a script: start friendly, move you to another app, then an investment or romance pitch.
  • TipPhones show a QR code's destination in a preview before you open it: read it with the same link rules from part 2. For payments, type the address or use the app.

// CHECKPOINT 1 · 5 MIN

Pause and discuss

Talk with a neighbour for a couple of minutes, then we share answers.

  1. Which pressure tricks are in "Your account will be closed in 2 hours unless you verify now"?

    Hint

    Think about the clock, and about what happens if you don't act.

    Answer

    Urgency (2 hours) and fear (closure). The ask is "verify now", which usually means "sign in on my page". Authority is implied if it claims to be from your bank.

  2. Your "boss" texts from an unknown number: "Quick favour, buy 5 gift cards, I'm in a meeting." Which of the five are in it, and what do you do?

    Hint

    Who is the "ask" for, and is gift cards a normal way for a company to pay?

    Answer

    Authority, urgency and (often) secrecy, with an unusual payment as the ask. Don't reply to that thread. Contact your manager on a number or app you already use, and tell your security team.

  3. Name a time you felt rushed by a message, even a real one. What made it feel urgent?

    Hint

    Real messages can be urgent too. What's different about how a real organisation behaves?

    Answer

    Real organisations give you ways to check (log in yourself, call the number on your card), don't demand odd payment methods, and don't forbid you from telling anyone.

5 min

  • ShowPairs talk for 2 minutes, then share. The timer starts by itself.
  • TipThe third question invites stories. Thank people for them: it sets up the "no shame" slide.

// SPOTTING THE FAKE 01

Reading a link without clicking it

    1. The site is the host: the text between :// and the next /, ? or #.
    2. If there's an @, everything before it is just a username. The host is what follows.
    3. Read the host from the right. The last two parts (acmebank.com) are the site. Everything to the left is a label the owner chose.
    4. So acmebank.com.account-review.net belongs to account-review.net.

    LOOK-ALIKES rn for m, 0 for o, 1 for l, and letters from other alphabets. Short links hide the destination. When unsure: don't click, and go to the site yourself.

    ~5 min

    • ShowClick "Real": the site is acmebank.com (login. is just a subdomain). "Prefix trick": same start, but the real site flips to account-review.net. "The @ trick": the bank name sits before the @, where it's only a username. "rn": the tool warns about the swapped letters. "Short link": nothing to read, so the advice is to avoid it.
    • SayThe padlock and "https" only mean the connection is private, not that the site is honest: criminals can get certificates too. The skill is reading the host, and the habit is not needing to: bookmark the sites you use for money and go through the bookmark.
    • TipThis tool knows a short list of multi-part endings like .co.uk; it's a teaching toy, not a browser. The browser's own address bar is the truth.

    // SPOTTING THE FAKE 02

    Real or fake? Judge the address

    ~4 min

    • ShowTake a show of hands for each link before revealing. Nine links: five fake, four real. Two real ones look odd on purpose (login.microsoftonline.com is genuine). The point is that real can look strange, so the rule is "read the host", not "does it look nice".
    • SayWe're judging addresses, not pages. A genuine address can still host a page that has been compromised, but that's rarer than a look-alike.
    • TipThe Cyrillic "a" in the apple link is invisible to the eye. Your browser shows it as xn--… in the real host, which is the clue.

    // SPOTTING THE FAKE 03

    Who is it really from?

    FromAcme Bank Security
    Addressalerts@acmebank-notices.net
    Reply-Tohelp@acme-bank-recovery.com
    SubjectAction required: unusual sign-in

    • The display name is whatever the sender typed. Anyone can call themselves "Acme Bank Security".
    • The address after it is what counts. Read its domain the same way as a link.
    • Reply-To decides where your answer goes. If it differs from the sender, ask why.
    • Mail checks (SPF, DKIM, DMARC) prove which domain sent a message, not that the domain is honest. Criminals can set up checks for their own look-alike domains.
    • On a phone, tap the sender's name to see the address. Many mail apps hide it.

    ~3 min

    • ShowPress the button: the display name looks official, but the real address is a look-alike domain and replies go to a third one.
    • SaySame rule as links: find the real domain. Then ask the better question: did I expect this? If your bank really wants you, it will still be there when you open the app yourself.

    // SPOTTING THE FAKE 05

    AI writes perfect scams. Judge the request, not the writing

    No longer reliableSpelling and grammar mistakes · a generic greeting · a clumsy translation · an ugly logo. AI fixes all of these in seconds, in any language.
    Still reliableYou weren't expecting it · it creates pressure · it asks for money, a code, a sign-in or secrecy · it comes by a channel the sender doesn't normally use.

    PERSONALISED NOW Scammers can use what's public about you (your job, your boss, a holiday post) to make a message fit. Tidier privacy settings mean less raw material.

    Three questions before you act

    1. Did I expect this? A surprise isn't proof of a scam, but it earns a closer look.
    2. What am I being asked to do? Pay, sign in, share a code, install, keep quiet?
    3. Can I check another way? Use a number, app or website I already have, not the one in the message.

    Perfect grammar proves nothing. It only means the writer had a good tool.

    ~3 min

    • SayPeople were taught "spot the typos". That worked when scammers wrote by hand in a second language. Today an AI can write in your colleague's tone using their public posts. So we move the defence from the writing to the situation: three questions that work no matter who or what wrote the message.
    • TipThe privacy point is general advice: set social accounts to friends-only where you can, and think before posting travel dates, workplace details or family names.
    • Next"Same idea, but with voices and faces."

    // SPOTTING THE FAKE 06

    Voice clones and deepfake calls

    "Family emergency"A call or voice note in a relative's voice: arrested, in an accident, stranded. They need money fast, and "please don't tell Mum".
    "The boss wants a transfer"A call or video meeting with a familiar face: a confidential deal, pay this invoice today. Arup lost about US$25 million this way.
    "Your bank's fraud team"Caller ID shows the real bank. They ask you to "move your money to a safe account". No real bank does that.

    Defend with a process, not a sharp eye

    • Don't rely on spotting glitches. The fakes keep getting better, and a good one is fine on a bad line.
    • Hang up and call back on a number you already have.
    • Agree a code phrase with family, and use it for any money request.
    • At work: two people approve payments and changes of bank details, and the second one calls the requester on a known number.

    Sources: FTC family emergency scams · The Guardian on Arup

    ~4 min

    • SayEach of these arrives with a voice or face you trust. That's why the defence can't be "does it sound right?" It has to be a rule you follow even when it sounds exactly right. Caller ID can be faked, so "the number matched" isn't proof.
    • TipNo real bank will ask you to move money to a "safe account". That sentence on its own is the whole warning.
    • Next"The rule, then."

    // SPOTTING THE FAKE 07

    The call-back rule, and a family code phrase

    If someone contacts you and asks for money, a code or a sign-in: stop, hang up, and reach them yourself, using a number or app you already had.

    1. Don't use the phone number, link or "reply" button in the message.
    2. Use the number on the back of your card, your contacts, or the company's own site.
    3. If the real person answers, you've lost two minutes. If not, you've dodged a scam.
    4. For an urgent "family" call, ring the person, or someone who's with them, directly.
    Family code phrase press the button

    Agree it in person. Say it out loud; don't text it. Avoid pet names, birthdays or anything on social media.

    ~3 min

    • SayOne rule covers nearly everything: you start the contact, not them. The phrase is for the moment the voice sounds exactly right: a real relative can answer it, a clone can't.
    • ShowPick a phrase: three random words chosen in your browser with the browser's own secure random numbers. It isn't sent or stored anywhere. In real life pick your own and agree it face to face.
    • TipSet a rule for the phrase too: if the caller can't say it, hang up, even if it feels rude. A real relative will understand.

    // IF IT HAPPENS 02

    Where to report

    First: closest to youWork or school: your IT or security team, before anything else. The service it came through: use the "report phishing / spam / impersonation" button. Your bank or card company: if money or card details are involved.
    Then: the national siteUS: reportfraud.ftc.gov, and ic3.gov for internet crime or money lost. UK: forward emails to report@phishing.gov.uk and texts to 7726. Canada: the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre. Australia: Scamwatch.
    • Reports help get fake sites taken down and warn others, even if you didn't lose anything.
    • Report even if you only nearly fell for it. Near misses are the cheapest lessons.
    • Keep evidence: take a screenshot, keep the message, note the number or address. Don't forward a scam to friends "as a warning" without saying so.
    • Your country not listed? Search for "report fraud" and the country name, and check the site is the government's own.

    reportfraud.ftc.gov · ic3.gov · NCSC · CAFC · Scamwatch

    ~3 min

    • SayStart at the closest layer and work outwards. At work, the security team wants to hear about it fast: one report about a phishing email protects everyone else who received it. Say the list is not exhaustive and swap in your own country's service.
    • TipCheck these before presenting to a new audience: reporting sites and numbers change, and anything country-specific should be swapped for where the audience is.

    // IF IT HAPPENS 03

    No shame: fast reports beat flawless people

    Nobody avoids every scam forever. The people who come out best aren't the ones who never get fooled: they're the ones who tell someone quickly.

    • Scammers count on embarrassment, and secrecy is one of their five tricks. Silence helps them.
    • Fast reporting limits the damage, for you and for everyone else who got the same message.
    • Telling a friend or relative first is fine. Then tell the bank or the security team.

    If you run a team or a family

    • Say out loud: "If you're ever fooled, tell me straight away. You won't be in trouble."
    • Make reporting one click: a report-phishing button, or one address everyone knows.
    • Thank people for reports, including false alarms. A 'false alarm' report is a success.
    • Practise: run a tabletop of the scenarios from earlier.

    ~2 min

    • SayThis is the most important slide for the people who lead. If reporting feels risky, people hide mistakes, and the attacker gets days of head start instead of minutes. Make the report button the easiest thing to do.
    • AskWhat's the easiest way to report something suspicious where you work, and would you know it without looking it up?

    // IF IT HAPPENS 04

    Six habits to take home

    1 · PauseUrgent and unexpected is the warning. Take a breath before you click, pay or reply.
    2 · Check the askMoney, a code, a sign-in, an install or secrecy? That's what to verify.
    3 · Go yourselfBookmark the sites you use for money, and use the app. Don't use the link in the message.
    4 · Call backReach people on a number you already have. Agree a family code phrase.
    5 · Use the toolsA password manager and passkeys refuse fake sites. Turn on MFA, and keep software updated.
    6 · Report fastTell the right people within the hour. No shame, no waiting.

    ~2 min

    • SaySix things, none of which need technical skill. If you remember only one, make it number one: scammers need you to act before you think, so the best defence is to wait ten minutes.
