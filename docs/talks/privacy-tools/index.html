// WHY THIS TALK

Privacy depends on who holds what

"Encrypted" and "private" are adverts as much as facts. The useful question is always the same: who can see it, and who holds the key?

  • What a company can hand over depends on what it keeps.
  • A tool that hides you from one watcher usually hands your view to another one.
  • You don't need to be a target to use this: the same questions apply to a café, a phone backup and a laptop.

Sources: Signal: government requests · CISA: mobile communications guidance, Dec 2024 · TechCrunch, 23 Jan 2026 · Microsoft: finding your BitLocker recovery key

A messenger that holds almost nothing, 2016A US grand jury subpoena asked Signal for information about two users. Signal could provide only when each account was created and when it last connected.
When the network itself was the target, 2024After hackers affiliated with the Chinese government targeted phone carriers' networks, CISA told highly targeted people to use only end-to-end encrypted messaging ("such as Signal or similar apps"), and not text-message codes to sign in. It says it applies to all audiences.
A key the company held, reported January 2026Reports said that, under a search warrant, Microsoft gave the FBI BitLocker recovery keys for three laptops in a Guam fraud investigation, because the keys had been saved to a Microsoft account. Microsoft's help page says where the key goes depends on how encryption was turned on.

~3 min

  • SayThree stories, one idea each, and none of them is about a clever attack. The first is a company that kept so little there was little to hand over. The second is about a network that attackers got into: end-to-end encryption still protected what people wrote. The third is the opposite: a company that held a key, and so could be asked for it. Keep the tone even: none of this is a story about someone doing something wrong. It's about what each design makes possible.
  • TipSignal: its government-requests page lists the 2016 grand jury subpoena (Eastern District of Virginia) and says the only information it could produce was the date and time a user registered and the last date of a user's connectivity. CISA: guidance of 18 December 2024, written for "highly targeted" people in senior government or political roles after PRC-affiliated actors targeted commercial telecom networks; it says it is "applicable to all audiences" and recommends "Signal or similar apps". Keep the wording as "reports said".

// A WAY TO THINK 01

Start with the worry, not the tool

Six questions, from the EFF

  1. What do I want to protect?
  2. Who do I want to protect it from?
  3. How bad are the consequences if I fail?
  4. How likely is it that I will need to protect it?
  5. How much trouble am I willing to go through?
  6. Who are my allies?

SAY IT AS A SENTENCE "I don't want someone to see something." That sentence picks your tool. The EFF's own words: "There is no perfect option for security."

Who might be watching

The café or hotel Wi-FiWhoever runs the network you join.
Your internet providerCarries everything you send from home. A 2021 FTC study found many providers collect and use more data than people expect.
The website or appKnows what you ask it for, and who you sign in as.
Advertisers and data brokersFollow you across sites with cookies and fingerprints.
The service's companyHolds your messages, files or backups, and answers legal orders.
Someone with your deviceA thief, or someone you share a computer with.
A government or courtCan order companies to hand over what they hold.

Sources: EFF Surveillance Self-Defense: your security plan · FTC staff report on ISPs, Oct 2021

~7 min

  • SayTools are answers; you need the question first. The EFF's Surveillance Self-Defense guide asks six questions, and the second one, who you want to protect it from, does most of the work. Most people's real answer is a short list: the network I'm on, the companies I use, and the person who finds my phone. Nobody needs to defend against everything, and "how much trouble am I willing to go through" is a fair question to ask yourself.
  • AskFinish the sentence for yourself: "I don't want ___ to see ___." Share one with a neighbour. Typical answers: my employer, my provider, advertisers, an ex, a thief; and my messages, my browsing, my photos, my location.
  • TipEFF: the page lists six questions today (older write-ups list five; the sixth is "Who are my allies?"). Its quoted lines: "Security is a process", "There is no perfect option for security", "Not everyone has the same priorities, concerns, or access to resources". FTC: the staff report was published 21 October 2021 on six large US providers and found that many collect, combine and use personal data more than consumers expect, with opt-outs that are hard to use. The watchers' list is mine, not the EFF's.

// HIDING WHERE YOU GO 02

VPNs: a tunnel to one company

What a VPN does: your provider sees only the tunnel; the VPN company sees what your provider used to
YouEncrypted tunnelVPN companyWebsite
  • Good uses: hiding which sites you visit from the network you're on; getting around blocking on a network that filters sites; reaching your own home or work network while away.
  • Not for: anonymity, stopping trackers, or protecting you from malware and phishing.
  • Do not use a VPN you don't trust. A good-looking privacy policy proves little.

Sources: EFF: choosing a VPN · FTC: tips on VPN apps, 2018 · Mullvad: search warrant, April 2023 · Google: HTTPS by default

~5 min

  • ShowRead each claim aloud and let the room vote with a show of hands (true, partly, misleading) before pressing a button, then read the explanation. Six claims, about forty seconds each.
  • SayA VPN is a tunnel to one company. It really does hide your sites from the network you're on, and it really does give that view to the VPN company. That can be a good trade on a hotel network or a provider you don't trust, and a poor one if the company is careless with it. The adverts promise far more than that: anonymity, protection from hackers, and "no logs" with nothing to back it up. I'm naming no products here, and I'm not recommending any.
  • AskWhy might "we keep no logs" be true and still not be enough? It's a promise; you can't check it yourself. Look for independent audits and for what the company actually did when it was asked for data.
  • TipEFF (its VPN guide): "while a VPN hides your browsing data from the ISP, it's all visible to the VPN provider"; VPNs are "best suited for one thing: routing your network connection through a different network"; two real uses (getting round censorship on a network that blocks sites, and connecting to a corporate or home network while away); "a claim is not a guarantee" about no-logs claims; "A VPN is not a tool for anonymity" (companies can still track you with cookies, tracking pixels or fingerprinting); "If you are interested in increased anonymity, Tor is a better solution than a VPN." FTC (22 February 2018): VPN apps can help shield information on public Wi-Fi, but check that the app encrypts and what it shares. Mullvad, in its own post of 20 April 2023, says police with a search warrant visited its office in Gothenburg and left without taking anything or any customer data, because it keeps none; a follow-up of 2 May says the warrant came from a German investigation that was not aimed at the service. That is the company's account. Check: "military-grade encryption" is called partly true from general knowledge (the tunnel uses ordinary strong encryption like HTTPS does), not from a source.

// HIDING WHERE YOU GO 03

Tor: three relays, three layers

WhoReceivesOpens it with its own key and learns

  • Three relays by default: a guard, a middle and an exit, from volunteer-run servers.
  • No single relay knows both who you are and what you're doing. The guard knows your address, the exit knows the site, the middle knows neither.
  • Where it stops: an observer who can see both you and the destination (or your exit) can match timings. Tor says it does not defend against that.
  • The exit can read plain HTTP, so use HTTPS. Your provider can still see that you use Tor.
  • Signing in gives you away, however you got there.

A TOY Real AES-GCM from this deck's teaching code. Real Tor negotiates a key with each relay as it builds the circuit; the layering idea is the same.

Sources: Tor Project: about Tor Browser · protections · remaining attacks · entry guards · circuit length

~5 min

  • ShowPress the button to wrap the message: the sender puts three layers on it, innermost for the exit. Keep pressing: each relay opens only its own layer, learns only the next hop, and passes the rest along. At the end the exit sees the site and, if you left HTTPS off, your message. Tick HTTPS and start over: the exit now sees only a scrambled request. Press "Try the wrong key" to see that a layer made for another relay just fails to open.
  • SayEach relay holds a different key and peels one layer. The guard knows who you are but not where you're going; the exit knows where you're going but not who you are; the middle knows neither. That is why it is hard for any one relay to tell, and why Tor is slow: three hops, each run by someone else.
  • AskWhy can't the middle relay tell who you are or where you're going? It only ever sees the relay before it and the relay after it, and the contents are still wrapped in the layer meant for the exit.
  • TipTor Project pages: "Tor works by sending your traffic through three random servers (also known as relays)"; circuits "normally use three relays by design" (more for onion services); "all your local ISP can observe now is that you are communicating with Tor nodes"; Tor "aims to improve your privacy by sending your traffic through a series of proxies"; and, on remaining attacks, "It is possible for an observer who can view both you and either the destination website or your Tor exit node to correlate timings of your traffic as it enters the Tor network and also as it exits. Tor does not defend against such a threat model." Entry guards exist so that a client keeps a small set of first relays rather than picking new ones each time. Check: torproject.org blocked my tools, so I read these pages through the Internet Archive's copies dated 2025; and the "ntor handshake", "fixed-size cells" and "different ciphers" in the toy note are general knowledge about the design, not quoted from a page.

Private DNS and Encrypted ClientHello

DNS
ECH
StepWhat a watcher on your network sees

AT HOME The Home Network talk shows where to switch on private DNS on your router and phone.

Sources: RFC 9849: Encrypted ClientHello · Mozilla: ECH FAQ · Cloudflare: ECH

  • Even with HTTPS, the site name leaks twice: in the DNS lookup, and in the first message of the handshake.
  • Encrypted DNS hides the lookup from the network between you and the resolver you chose. That resolver still sees every lookup.
  • Encrypted ClientHello (ECH) encrypts the site name in the handshake. The standard was published in March 2026.
  • They work best together, and ECH only helps when the site or its host supports it.
  • The address is still visible, though a big host serves many sites from one.

~2 min

  • ShowStart with both off: the name shows up twice. Turn on encrypted DNS: only the handshake still announces it. Turn on ECH too: a watcher sees an address and a volume, not the name. Then turn encrypted DNS off again with ECH on, to show why they belong together.
  • SayThis is the quiet fix for "my provider can still see which sites I visit, even with HTTPS". It needs your browser, the resolver and the site to cooperate. It moves trust once more: to the resolver you picked.
  • TipRFC 9849 (March 2026) says ECH "protects the SNI and other potentially sensitive fields"; section 10.2 warns that "SNI encryption is less useful without encryption of DNS queries in transit"; the ECH configuration is normally published in DNS, though "other delivery mechanisms are also possible". Mozilla's FAQ: ECH is enabled in Firefox by default since version 119, has "the most privacy benefit if used in conjunction with DNS over HTTPS", and "won't be used if family safety software is used or Firefox has been configured as part of an enterprise". Cloudflare's docs: ECH is enabled by default on Free zones and masks the real name behind a shared name, so a watcher can see that a Cloudflare site is being used but not which one. Check: browser version numbers for Chrome were reported inconsistently (117 or 118), so the slide says only that ECH is supported; and whether a particular site supports ECH is a per-site question.

// HIDING WHERE YOU GO 05

Private windows and trackers

  • A private window forgets on your device. It hides things from the next person who uses it, not from the network or the sites.
  • Trackers follow cookies and fingerprints, not routes. Choose a browser, and settings, that block them.
  • Firefox and Safari block cross-site cookies by default. Chrome has kept third-party cookies, and left the choice to you.
  • Global Privacy Control is a setting that tells sites not to sell or share your data.

Sources: Mozilla: private browsing myths · Google: Incognito · WebKit: full third-party cookie blocking · Mozilla: Total Cookie Protection · Google: Chrome's cookie approach · California: opt-out preference signals

~3 min

  • ShowSame format as the VPN slide: read each claim, vote by hand, then press and read the explanation.
  • Say"Private" on a browser window means private on this computer. Everything on the network, and every website, sees the same as in a normal window. What does the work against tracking is the browser's tracker blocking, and a setting called Global Privacy Control, not the window.
  • TipMozilla's private browsing page: it "doesn't make you anonymous to websites, your internet provider, your employer, or search engines"; in its table, private browsing does not "Hide your activity from your internet service provider", "Mask your IP address or location" or "Stop websites from identifying or tracking you", and it does delete cookies and site data when you close the window. Downloads and bookmarks you save stay. Google's Incognito page says that after a session "Chrome doesn't retain site data or a record of the sites you visited", while websites, "organizations that manage your network", and your employer, school or provider may still observe your activity. WebKit (24 March 2020): "Cookies for cross-site resources are now blocked by default across the board" in Safari. Mozilla (June 2022, updated 2024): Total Cookie Protection by default confines cookies to the site where they were created. Google (April 2025): Chrome keeps its current approach and leaves third-party cookie choice to users, with Incognito blocking them by default; and on 17 October 2025 it said it had decided to retire a list of Privacy Sandbox technologies, including Topics and Protected Audience, with a phase-out to follow. Global Privacy Control: California says businesses are required to honour opt-out preference signals, and that by 1 January 2027 all browsers must include one. Check: which browsers send it today, and which US states honour it, vary; I confirmed California only.

// CHECKPOINT 1 · 5 MIN

Pause and discuss

Talk with a neighbour for a couple of minutes, then we share answers.

  1. You use a VPN on café Wi-Fi, on a site that uses HTTPS. Who can now see which sites you visit?

    Hint

    Who is at the other end of the tunnel?

    Answer

    The VPN company, and the websites themselves. The café and your provider see only one encrypted tunnel. You've moved trust from them to the VPN company.

  2. Why can't the middle Tor relay tell who you are or where you're going?

    Hint

    What does each relay get to open?

    Answer

    It only sees the relay before it and the relay after it. The guard knows your address, the exit knows the destination, and the middle's layer wraps contents it can't read.

  3. You browse in a private window at work. Who can still see what you visited?

    Hint

    What does a private window forget, and where?

    Answer

    Your employer, your internet provider, and the websites themselves, as both Mozilla and Google say. A private window only forgets history and cookies on that device.

// HIDING WHAT YOU SAY 01

"Encrypted" versus "end-to-end encrypted"

Key

      

    

    

      

        
Encrypted in transitScrambled on the wire (HTTPS). The company at the other end gets it in the clear.

        
Encrypted at restScrambled on the company's disks. The company holds the key, so it, or anyone who can make it, can open the data.

        
End-to-end encryptedOnly the people at the ends hold the keys. The company stores scrambled data it can't open.

      

      
THREE QUESTIONS TO ASK A SERVICE Who holds the key? What can you recover if I forget my password? What do you still know about me besides the content?

      
Sources: Apple: iCloud data security overview (standard protection and Advanced Data Protection side by side). The demo is a toy: AES-128-GCM from this deck's teaching code.

    

  

  
~6 min
  • ShowStart with "The provider holds it". Press each scenario: a stolen disk can't be read (encryption at rest does its job); a legal order or someone inside the key system can read it, because the key is there; forgetting your password is fixed by the provider. Switch to "I hold it": the same four scenarios now fail for everyone but you, and forgetting your password is only fixed if you ticked "I saved my recovery key". Untick it and press the last scenario to feel the price.
  • Say"Encrypted" tells you the data is scrambled somewhere. It doesn't tell you who can unscramble it. At rest, the provider holds the key; end-to-end, you do. Neither is wrong: a provider-held key is how you get a password reset, search inside your files and a working account after you lose your phone. End-to-end is how you get a company that can't be made to hand your data over, because it can't read it. The cost is that you carry the risk.
  • AskA service says your files are "encrypted". What question should you ask next? Who holds the key?
  • TipApple's iCloud data security page puts the two designs side by side: under standard data protection, many categories are encrypted in transit and on the server, with the keys held by Apple, and Advanced Data Protection "is an optional setting" that makes most of them end-to-end; with it on, "Apple will not have the encryption keys to help you recover it". Even with Advanced Data Protection, iCloud Mail, Contacts and Calendars stay under standard protection, because mail has to interoperate with the global email system. The demo is not a security product: a real service uses careful key management. Don't attribute the scenario outcomes to any one company.

  • AskIf you turn on an end-to-end backup, where would you keep the key? Somewhere that survives losing the phone: a password manager that you can still open from another device, or a paper copy in a safe place.
  • TipApple's iCloud data security page: under standard protection the keys to iCloud Backup "are secured in Apple data centers"; with Advanced Data Protection "iCloud Backup and everything inside it is end-to-end encrypted"; "Apple will not have the encryption keys to help you recover it". Apple's UK page: users who had not already enabled Advanced Data Protection "will no longer have the option to do so"; Apple also says iMessage and FaceTime stay end-to-end encrypted in the UK. Check: Apple's page is dated to the February 2025 change; press reports in 2026 say it is still not offered to new UK users and that Apple's legal challenge is ongoing, but the UK order has never been officially confirmed. Look at Apple's page the week you present. Google's backup page: "Some of your data is end to end encrypted with your device's screen lock PIN, pattern, or password", and MMS media and Google Photos are excluded. WhatsApp (2021): end-to-end encrypted backups are optional; (30 October 2025): passkeys can replace the password or 64-digit key. Signal (2025): "Signal's secure backups are opt-in", the key is 64 characters, "never shared with Signal's servers", and "Losing it means losing access to your backup permanently, and Signal cannot help you recover it." Apple (encrypted backups): "Your backup isn't encrypted by default" for a backup made to a computer.
  • Next"One more place where a copy of the key can hide: your laptop."






  
// HIDING WHAT YOU SAY 04
Disk encryption: lost-laptop insurance

  

    

      
It protects you when
  • a laptop is lost or stolen while it's off or locked
  • the drive is taken out and read somewhere else
  • nobody has the key, or the lock

      
It doesn't protect you when
  • you're signed in and the screen is unlocked
  • malware is running as you
  • someone has the recovery key, or can make the company that holds it hand it over
  • the recovery key sits next to the laptop

      
The key question: where's the recovery key?
  • Windows (BitLocker): where it was saved depends on how encryption was turned on: your Microsoft account, a work or school account, a printout or a file.
  • Mac (FileVault): you choose to unlock with your iCloud account or with a recovery key you keep, ideally not on the encrypted disk.
  • Phones: see the Your Phone, Safely talk.

    

    
A COPY WITH A COMPANY IS A COPY THEY CAN BE ASKED FOR. That's not a reason to avoid it: for most people the best recovery option is the one they'll actually be able to use. It's a reason to choose it on purpose, and to know which you picked.

    
Sources: Microsoft: finding your BitLocker recovery key · Apple: protect data on your Mac with FileVault · TechCrunch, 23 Jan 2026

  

  
~3 min
  • SayDisk encryption turns a lost laptop into a locked box. It's the most useful of all the tools in this talk for the commonest bad day: leaving a laptop on a train. The one question to ask is the same as before: who holds a copy of the key to unlock it? Often it's you, sometimes a company, and the right answer depends on how you'd recover after a disaster. Know which you've chosen.
  • AskDo you know where the recovery key for your laptop is? Most people don't. It takes two minutes to find out, and it's on the checklist at the end.
  • TipMicrosoft's help page: the key "might be" in your Microsoft account, in a work or school account, on a printout or on a USB flash drive, "depending on the choice that was made when activating BitLocker"; I could not find a sentence saying device encryption always backs it up on sign-in, so the slide doesn't say that. Apple's FileVault guide: choose "Allow my iCloud account to unlock my disk" or a recovery key, and "keep a copy of the key somewhere other than your encrypted startup disk"; if you lose both the password and the key "your files and settings will be lost forever". The Guam reports (23 January 2026): see slide 3's check note; the keys had been stored in a Microsoft account. Phone encryption is covered in General 4, so it isn't repeated.

// CHECKPOINT 2 · 5 MIN
Pause and discuss

  

    
Talk with a neighbour for a couple of minutes, then we share answers.

    
    
      
  1. A service says your files are "encrypted". What question should you ask next?
    Hint
    Encrypted by whom, and who can unlock it?
    Answer
    Who holds the key? If the provider does, it (or anyone it's made to help) can open the data and can help you recover it. If you do, nobody else can, and nobody can help you if you lose the key.
    2. 
      
  2. Your chat app is end-to-end encrypted, and your phone backs it up to the cloud. Can the backup undo that?
    Hint
    Who holds the backup's key?
    Answer
    Yes, if the backup isn't end-to-end encrypted: the company can open it. Check the backup setting, and if you turn on the end-to-end option, keep the key safe.
    3. 
      
  3. An app can't read your messages. What might it still know about you?
    Hint
    Think of an envelope.
    Answer
    Metadata: who you talk to, when, how often, how big the messages are, and your network address. Apps keep very different amounts of it.
    4.
