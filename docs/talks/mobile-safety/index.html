// WHY THIS TALK

Your phone is the key to everything else

It's your bank card, your post box and your front-door key. It also receives the codes that prove you're you, so whoever holds it, and its number, can often reset everything else.

  • Most phone trouble is ordinary: a lost phone, an old one without updates, an app that asks for too much.
  • The defences are mostly settings you switch on once. You can do most of them during this talk.
Watched, then stolen, 2023The Wall Street Journal reported thieves watching people type their passcode in bars, then stealing the phone and changing the owner's account password within minutes. Apple later added Stolen Device Protection, which targets exactly this.
A stolen phone number, 2024Someone took over the US SEC's X account by getting control of the phone number tied to it in an apparent "SIM swap". The account's extra sign-in step had been turned off.
Fake apps, every yearGoogle says Play Protect found more than 27 million new malicious apps from outside Google Play in 2025.

Sources: Apple: Stolen Device Protection · SEC statement, Jan 2024 · Google, Feb 2026

~2 min

  • SayThree short stories, three parts of this talk: a lock that someone watched you type, a phone number that moved to someone else's SIM, and apps that aren't what they say. None of these needed a genius attacker.
  • TipThe WSJ reports (Joanna Stern and Nicole Nguyen, February and April 2023) are paywalled; Apple's own page describes the feature, not the reporting. The SEC statement is dated 22 January 2024: access to the number "occurred via the telecom carrier, not via SEC systems", and multi-factor authentication had been disabled by X Support at staff request in July 2023.

// THE LOCK 01

Passcode or face? Both, for different jobs

The passcodeThe real key. It unlocks the encryption, and it's what the phone falls back to.Its risk: someone watching you type it.
Face or fingerprintA convenient stand-in, so you can use a long passcode without typing it fifty times a day.It never leaves the phone's secure chip, and it doesn't replace the passcode.

THE TRADE A face or fingerprint lets you choose a longer passcode, because you'll rarely type it. That's the real security win.

When an iPhone insists on the passcode

  • After it's switched on or restarted.
  • When it hasn't been unlocked for more than 48 hours.
  • After five failed face or fingerprint attempts.
  • After a remote lock, or when Emergency SOS or Medical ID is started.
  • If the passcode hasn't been used for 6.5 days and Face ID or Touch ID not for 4 hours.

Android: phones with the strongest class of fingerprint or face unlock must ask for the PIN, pattern or password at least every 72 hours, and always after a restart.

Sources: Apple: set a passcode · Android: biometric classes

~3 min

  • SayPeople argue "face or code?" as if you pick one. You don't: the face is a shortcut to the code. The phone asks for the real code at the moments when it matters most, like after a restart. That's also why you sometimes get asked for it out of the blue.
  • AskWho has been asked for their passcode this week even though Face ID or fingerprint is on? Now you know why.
  • TipApple's list is from its iPhone User Guide (iOS 27). Android's rule is the Android compatibility requirement for "Class 3" biometrics: up to 72 hours before falling back to the PIN, pattern or password. Weaker "Class 2" unlock has stricter limits (24 hours, or 4 hours idle).

// THE LOCK 02

How guessable is a passcode?

  • The phone limits guesses. Its secure chip adds growing delays after wrong tries: on an iPhone, about ten before it stops accepting more; on newer Android, twenty.
  • So the danger is a popular code. In a 2012 study of 3.4 million leaked four-digit codes, 1234 alone was almost 11%, and the top 20 covered 27%.
  • Random beats long. A random six-digit code is a good minimum. Letters and digits are better still, and your face or finger does the typing.

Sources: Apple Platform Security · Android rate limiting · DataGenetics PIN study · Crypto 101 cracking estimator

~4 min

  • ShowStart on 1234: on the popular list, so a guesser probably gets it in the first few tries. Try "A birth year": years are a classic pattern. Then "6 random digits": one in a million, and with ten guesses the odds are tiny. Switch phone types to show the guess limits. The last line shows the time to try every code if someone could skip the delays and still run at the phone's own speed (Apple tunes each try to take about 80 ms).
  • SayOn a laptop's password, length is everything because attackers can guess billions of times a second offline. A phone is different: the passcode is mixed with a secret built into the chip, so guesses have to happen on that phone, at its pace, with its delays. That's why the popular-list check matters more than the maths.
  • TipApple's table (iOS Lock Screen): no delay for the first few tries, then 1 min, 5 min, 15 min, 1 h, 3 h, 8 h, and after 10 the device locks and needs a computer; Erase Data (optional) wipes after 10. Android 16 QPR2 and later default: 1 min after the 5th wrong guess, rising to years, and no guesses after 20; the bare Android 16 minimum is more lenient. Older or other Android phones vary by maker. A 2020 study (Markert et al., IEEE S&P) found six digits add little against a throttled guesser if people pick patterns, which is why the advice is "random".
  • TipThe DataGenetics data is leaked four-digit passwords, used as a stand-in for PINs, so treat the percentages as "people are predictable", not as phone statistics. The Crypto 101 link goes to the general cracking estimator.

// THE LOCK 03

Encryption, and why "before first unlock" matters

Switched off or just restartedBefore first unlock. The keys for your data aren't in memory. Almost everything is unreadable until the passcode is typed.
Locked, after first unlockThe screen is locked, but many keys stay in memory so messages and apps keep working in the background.
UnlockedIn use. Whoever holds it can see what you can.

  • Setting a passcode switches on encryption. Modern iPhones and Android phones encrypt by default, and the key comes partly from your passcode.
  • A restart puts the phone in its strongest state. If you lose it, or it's about to leave your hands, a restart costs nothing.
  • Researchers found that iPhones (iOS 18.1 and later) restart themselves after about three days locked, for the same reason.
  • Encryption protects a phone that's locked. Nothing protects an unlocked phone in someone else's hand, apart from the theft features coming up next.

Sources: Apple: data protection classes · Android file-based encryption · TechCrunch, Nov 2024

~2 min

  • ShowClick the three states. The grid of "files" shows how much is readable in each: almost nothing after a restart, more once it has been unlocked once, everything when it's open in someone's hand.
  • SayEncryption is why a locked phone found in a taxi is a paperweight to most thieves. "After first unlock" is the everyday state: locked screen, but the phone has its keys ready so your messages arrive. A restart drops them.
  • TipApple: third-party app data defaults to "Protected Until First User Authentication", which protects against attacks that involve a reboot. Android: all devices launching with Android 10 or later must use file-based encryption, with "credential encrypted" storage available only after the user first unlocks. The iPhone inactivity restart was found and confirmed by researchers (Jiska Classen; Magnet Forensics); Apple hasn't documented it. Reports say Android has been rolling out something similar through Google Play services, but its status is unclear, so don't promise it.

// CHECKPOINT 1 · 5 MIN

Pause and discuss

Talk with a neighbour for a couple of minutes, then we share answers.

  1. Why does a phone ask for your passcode after a restart, even if you use your face or fingerprint?

    Hint

    Where do the keys for your data come from, and where are they kept after a restart?

    Answer

    After a restart the keys for most of your data aren't in memory: they're rebuilt from the passcode (mixed with a secret in the phone's chip). The face or fingerprint is only a shortcut that the phone accepts once the passcode has been used. That's also why a restarted phone is the hardest to get into.

  2. Your four-digit passcode is your birth year. Why is that weaker than the "10,000 possible codes" suggests?

    Hint

    Does a guesser try codes in random order?

    Answer

    Guessers start with popular codes and patterns: 1234, repeated digits, and years. The phone only allows a handful of tries, so being near the top of that list is what matters. A random code, ideally six digits or more, fixes it.

  3. Name one thing on your own lock screen that a stranger could read right now.

    Hint

    Messages, codes, calendar, widgets…

    Answer

    Common ones: message previews, sign-in codes, calendar entries, and smart-home or payment widgets. Set previews to "when unlocked" and trim the widgets. Keep emergency medical info: that one is meant to be seen.

5 min

  • ShowPairs talk for 2 minutes, then share. The timer starts by itself.
  • TipIf someone asks "should I turn on Erase Data after 10 tries?": it's a real protection, but a toddler with the phone can trigger it, so only with good backups.

// APPS 01

Permissions: what apps can see

Location"While using the app", not "always". Turn off Precise location where a rough area is enough.
Photos and contactsShare selected photos or contacts, not the whole library.
Microphone and cameraAndroid: "Only this time". Both systems show a coloured dot while an app is using them.
Unused appsAndroid removes permissions from apps you haven't used for a few months. On iPhone, review now and then.

THE TEST Does this app need it to do the thing I installed it for? If not, say no. You can always say yes later.

  • Apps must ask before using sensitive data, and you can change your answer at any time.
  • Saying no rarely breaks an app. It just loses that one feature.
  • Reviews: iPhone Settings › Privacy & Security (each category, plus App Privacy Report). Android Settings › Security & privacy › Privacy, or Settings › Apps › an app › Permissions.
  • For a deeper check of who and what you share with, iPhone has Safety Check (Settings › Privacy & Security).

Sources: Apple: location · Apple: camera and microphone · Android: app permissions · Android: one-time and auto-reset

~3 min

  • SayBoth phones moved the same way over the last few years: smaller, shorter permissions. "While using" instead of "always", rough location instead of exact, a few photos instead of all of them. The default answer to a permission you don't understand is no.
  • TipDetails: Android's "Only this time" (Android 11+) covers location, microphone and camera; indicators: iOS shows green for camera and orange for microphone; Android 12+ shows a green privacy indicator. Android resets the permissions of apps unused "for a few months" (apps targeting Android 11+); the setting is "Pause app activity if unused". iPhone offers "Limited Access" for contacts and selected photos. Menu names vary between versions and makers.

      // APPS 03

      App stores, sideloading and fake apps

      Official stores aren't perfect, but they helpApps are reviewed, and bad ones get removed. Google Play Protect also checks apps from other sources on Android.
      Sideloading is installing from elsewhereAndroid asks you to allow a specific app to "install unknown apps". From 30 September 2026, Android starts requiring verified developers for apps installed from participating stores in Brazil, Indonesia, Singapore and Thailand, and Google plans to expand this worldwide in 2027. In the EU, iPhones can use alternative app marketplaces.
      Fake apps copy names and logosIn February 2024 a "LassPass Password Manager" copied LastPass's branding on Apple's App Store until it was removed.

      Before you install

      1. Get it from the official store, from the link on the company's own website.
      2. Check the developer name, not just the app name and icon.
      3. Be suspicious of few reviews, very new listings, or a store page full of typos.
      4. Skip "cleaner", "booster" and "virus scanner" apps: phones manage their own memory, and pop-ups saying your phone has a virus are a scam.
      5. Never install an app because someone on the phone told you to. That's how remote-access scams start.

      Sources: Google Play Protect · Android developer verification · Apple: alternative distribution · Malwarebytes on LassPass

      ~3 min

      • SayMost people never sideload, and that's the right default. The bigger everyday risk is a fake app inside the real store: same logo, almost the same name, a different developer. The LassPass copy had a single rating against tens of thousands for the real one.
      • TipAndroid developer verification (Google's timeline page): August 2026, developer APIs and an "advanced flow" for power users; 30 September 2026, protections begin for users installing apps from participating stores in Brazil, Indonesia, Singapore and Thailand on certified devices running Android 7+; 2027, expansion worldwide. Details are still changing, so check the page before you present. Apple's EU alternative distribution started with iOS 17.4 (March 2024) and requires an EU account and being in the EU; Apple's page now also lists Japan.
      • TipOn "cleaners": this is general advice, not a claim that every such app is malicious. The thing to teach is that a web page or pop-up can't scan your phone, so "your phone has a virus" in a browser is always a sales pitch or a scam.

      // APPS 04

      Updates, and how long your phone gets them

      Software Update A security update is ready. It fixes issues that may have been actively exploited.

      • Turn on automatic updates for the system and for apps. iPhone: Settings › General › Software Update › Automatic Updates. Android: Settings › System › Software updates (names vary).
      • Some updates fix holes that attackers are already using. Sooner is safer.

      When does support end?

      PhonePromise
      Google Pixel 8 and later7 years from first US availability
      Google Pixel 6 and 75 years
      Samsung Galaxy S247 years of OS and security updates
      iPhoneNo fixed number: iOS 27 (2026) still supports the iPhone 11 from 2019

      An old phone that still works isn't "fine" once updates stop: new holes stay open for good. Check your maker's support page when you buy, and plan the replacement.

      Sources: Google Pixel update policy · 9to5Google on Samsung S24 · Apple: update iOS

      ~3 min

      • ShowThe fake prompt: press "Remind me tomorrow" a few times, then "Install tonight". Updates install overnight while charging, so "tonight" costs you nothing.
      • SayUpdates are the most boring advice in this talk and among the most effective. Apple's own security notes often say an issue "may have been exploited", meaning someone was using it before the fix. Then the second question: how long will my phone get updates at all?
      • TipSupport windows change and depend on model and country, so check the maker's page for the exact phone. Samsung's seven-year promise covers the S24 series; Samsung's A series and other makers differ. Apple publishes compatibility per iOS version, not a promise in years; the iOS 27 compatible list on Apple's iPhone User Guide starts at iPhone 11 and iPhone SE (2nd generation). Newer iPhones also get "Background Security Improvements" between updates (Settings › Privacy & Security). The EU's ecodesign rules (from June 2025) push makers selling there toward at least five years of updates after a model stops being sold, but how strictly that binds is debated.

      // APPS 05

      Public Wi-Fi and chargers: real risk or myth?

      What to actually do

      • Wi-Fi: fine for most things, because sites and apps encrypt. Be wary of a Wi-Fi sign-in page that asks for your email or social password.
      • Charging: carry your own plug or power bank. Public USB attacks have been shown in research, but documented real-world cases are hard to find.
      • If a phone asks "Trust this computer?" when you only wanted to charge it, say no.

      Sources: FTC: public Wi-Fi · ChoiceJacking, USENIX 2025 · Malwarebytes on juice jacking

      ~2 min

      • ShowFour claims. Vote, then reveal. The point: the scary stories are mostly yesterday's risks, and today's everyday risks are fake sign-in pages and fake alerts.
      • SayThe FTC now says public Wi-Fi is "usually safe" because most sites encrypt. "Juice jacking" is a real research topic: in 2025 Graz University of Technology showed malicious chargers that could fake your "allow" tap on phones from eight vendors, and Apple and Google added stronger checks. But warnings outnumber documented cases, so the realistic advice is simply: your own plug.
      • TipChoiceJacking: Draschbacher, Maar, Oberhuber and Mangard, USENIX Security 2025. Heise reported Apple closed the holes in iOS 18.4; Android fixes depend on the maker. The FCC's juice-jacking page and the FBI Denver post (2023) are the warnings people remember. The FTC Wi-Fi article is dated February 2023.

      // CHECKPOINT 2 · 5 MIN

      Pause and discuss

      Talk with a neighbour for a couple of minutes, then we share answers.

      1. A free photo-editing app asks for your location "always" and all your contacts. What do you do?

        Hint

        Does editing a photo need either of those?

        Answer

        Deny both. A photo editor needs the photos you choose, nothing more. If it refuses to work without them, find a different app.

      2. Someone on the phone says they're from your bank and asks you to install an app "so we can secure your account". What's going on?

        Hint

        What can an app installed on someone's say-so do?

        Answer

        A remote-access or fake-banking-app scam. Hang up, and call the bank on the number on your card. Treat any request to install an app during a call as a scam.

      3. Your phone's maker stopped updates last year, but it works fine. Is it fine?

        Hint

        What happens to security holes found after support ends?

        Answer

        It works, but new holes won't be fixed, so it gets less safe each month. Keep banking and email off it if you can, and plan a replacement.

      // WHEN IT GOES WRONG 02

      It's gone: the first hour

      1. Find itFrom another device or the web: see where it is, play a sound.
      2. Mark it as lostLocks it, shows your contact message, and on iPhone suspends Apple Pay. Android: "Mark as lost", or Remote Lock at android.com/lock with just your number.
      3. Call your carrierSuspend the line, so your number and your texted codes can't be used.
      4. Check your accountsFrom a trusted device, check your Apple or Google account. If someone may have seen your passcode, change passwords now.
      5. Report itTo the police, with the serial number or IMEI. Insurers usually need the report.
      6. Erase it, when you've given upThen leave it on your account: removing it lets a thief reset and sell it.
      "We found your iPhone!"Thieves send texts and emails like this to trick you into typing your Apple password on a fake page. Apple says it will never contact you to say your device has been found.
      No code needed for Find My on the webSigning in to icloud.com/find doesn't ask for a two-factor code, because your trusted device may be the lost one.

      Sources: Apple: if your iPhone is lost · Google: find, lock or erase · Google: Theft protection

      ~3 min

      • SaySpeed matters in the first hour, and the order matters too: lock it before you chase it. Never go to recover a phone yourself if the map shows it at a stranger's address; give the location to the police.
      • TipApple: don't remove the device from Find My even after erasing it, because that removes Activation Lock; if you have AppleCare+ Theft and Loss, don't remove it until the claim is approved. Google: after a factory reset the device no longer shows in Find Hub, and anyone setting it up needs your Google Account password. Remote Lock needs Theft protection set up beforehand (screen lock, verified number, Find Hub on).

      // WHEN IT GOES WRONG 03

      SIM swaps: when someone steals your number

      A criminal talks your carrier into moving your number to their SIM. Your calls and texts, including sign-in codes, now go to them.

      The signsYour phone suddenly has no service, no texts and no calls. Or your carrier tells you your SIM was activated on a new device.
      The numbersThe FBI's crime centre received 971 SIM-swap complaints in 2025, with $17.4 million reported lost. Those are only the cases people reported.

      Protect your number

      1. Add a PIN or passcode to your phone account.
      2. Turn on your carrier's number lock (stops moving the number) and SIM lock (stops SIM changes), if it offers them. They're often separate switches.
      3. Move important accounts off text-message codes: use an authenticator app, a passkey or a security key.
      4. If your service drops for no reason, call the carrier from another phone straight away.

      Sources: FTC: SIM swap scams · FBI IC3 2025 report · Passwords and Accounts talk

      ~3 min

      • SayThis one is nasty because nothing happens to your phone: it simply stops working, and you assume a network fault. Meanwhile, every "we sent you a code" goes to someone else. The SEC example from the start was exactly this.
      • AskDoes anyone know whether their carrier account has a PIN? Most people don't, and it takes five minutes in the carrier's app.
      • TipUS carrier names, from carrier help pages and press coverage (check the carrier's own page): Verizon has Number Lock and SIM Protection; AT&T has Wireless Account Lock; T-Mobile has SIM Protection and Account Takeover Protection. The FCC adopted rules in November 2023 requiring carriers to verify customers before SIM changes and port-outs and to notify them; compliance dates were delayed, so don't promise they're in force everywhere. Elsewhere, ask your carrier for "port-out protection" or a SIM-change lock.
      • TipIC3 figures: 971 complaints and $17,366,758 in 2025, down from 982 and about $26.0 million in 2024 and 2,026 complaints in 2022. IC3 defines a SIM swap as social engineering against mobile carriers to move a victim's service to a criminal's device.

      // WHEN IT GOES WRONG 04

      Stalkerware and unwanted trackers

      Sometimes the threat isn't a stranger. Monitoring apps and hidden trackers are used by partners, exes and family members. It is never the fault of the person being watched.

      • Signs: they know where you've been or what you've said without being told. Fast battery drain or odd data use can be clues, but have many innocent causes.
      • Trackers: iPhone (iOS 17.5+) and Android (6.0+) alert you when an unknown Bluetooth tracker travels with you, and can make it play a sound.
      • iPhone Safety Check reviews who you share location and accounts with, and has an emergency reset.
      Safety firstRemoving an app or a tracker can alert the person who placed it, and some people escalate. If you suspect this, talk to an advocate before changing anything, from a phone or computer they can't see.
      Free, confidential helpUS: National Domestic Violence Hotline, 1-800-799-7233, or text START to 88788. UK: National Domestic Abuse Helpline, 0808 2000 247 (24 hours). In danger now: call your emergency number.

      NNEDV Safety Net · FTC: stalkerware · Coalition Against Stalkerware · thehotline.org · Apple and Google tracker alerts

      ~3 min

      • SayKeep your tone calm and matter-of-fact. Someone in the room may be living this. The message is: it's not your fault, help exists, and the safest next step is a conversation with an advocate, not a factory reset.
      • TipThis slide has no demo or jokes on purpose. NNEDV: "Some people may escalate their abusive behavior when stalkerware is removed", and restoring from a backup can bring stalkerware back. The FTC says to contact advocates from a device that isn't monitored (a friend's phone, a library computer, or a prepaid phone). The US hotline site has a quick-exit button (Escape twice). Refuge runs the UK helpline and refugetechsafety.org. Swap in local services for other countries.
      • TipTracker alerts follow the joint Apple/Google "Detecting Unwanted Location Trackers" specification (May 2024). Safety Check is in Settings › Privacy & Security on iOS 16 and later.

      1 · A random passcodeSix digits or more, no patterns or years. Let your face or finger do the typing, and shield it in public.

      6 · Protect your numberA carrier PIN or lock, and sign-in codes from an app or passkey, not texts.
