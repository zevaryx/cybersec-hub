// COLLECT LESS 01

"We removed the names" is not anonymisation

RE-IDENTIFICATION · 200087%Sweeney's estimate, from 1990 US census data, that ZIP code + gender + full birth date is unique. A later re-count (Golle, 2006) found 61 to 63%. Either way: most people.
RE-IDENTIFICATION · 2008Netflix Prize ratingsAbout 500,000 subscribers' "anonymous" ratings linked back to named people using a little outside knowledge, such as public IMDb reviews (Narayanan and Shmatikov).
PURPOSE · 2022$150 millionThe FTC's penalty against Twitter for using phone numbers and emails collected for account security to target ads. More than 140 million users' details.
  • Personal data is anything about an identifiable person: names, emails, device IDs, IP addresses, and combinations that point to one person. Sensitive categories (health, beliefs, biometrics, children's data) carry stricter rules and higher harm.
  • Pseudonymised data is still personal data. Anonymous data is data nobody can reasonably link back to a person, using "all the means reasonably likely to be used". That's a high bar and it moves over time.

~3 min

  • SayThree stories, three lessons. The first two are about combining harmless-looking fields. Sweeney's 87% came from 1990 census data and was later re-estimated at 61 to 63%: still a majority, and uniqueness is not the same as being identified, but it shows the shape of the risk. The Netflix paper showed the same on sparse data: a few ratings plus public reviews were enough. The Twitter case is the third lesson: data collected for one purpose (securing an account) was used for another (ad targeting). That's a design failure as much as a legal one.
  • AskWhich of your own datasets have had names removed and then been treated as "safe to share"? Answer: there usually are some: analytics exports, test data copied from production, data shared with vendors.
  • TipSources: Sweeney, "Simple Demographics Often Identify People Uniquely" (2000); Golle, "Revisiting the Uniqueness of Simple Demographics in the US Population" (2006: 61% on 1990 data, 63% on 2000); Narayanan and Shmatikov, "Robust De-anonymization of Large Sparse Datasets" (IEEE S&P 2008); FTC press release, 25 May 2022 (a proposed order, $150 million, more than 140 million users, data provided 2014 to 2019 after Twitter began asking in 2013, violating the 2011 order; re-read 11 November 2026). The GDPR test for anonymity is Recital 26, "all the means reasonably likely to be used"; it also says pseudonymised data is personal data.
  • CheckGolle's 61 to 63% and the later critiques are secondary summaries for the "unique is not the same as identified" point. Not legal advice: your lawyer decides what is "anonymous" for your law.

// COLLECT LESS 02

Re-identify the "anonymous" release

The attacker's stepThe register of 60 invented people is public (name, ZIP, birth date, gender). Search it against the "anonymous" medical release.

Names look like: Avery Ashdown, Blake Brookfield, Casey Calloway …

~8 min

  • SayThis is a fully synthetic hospital release of 60 people: ZIP, birth date, gender and a diagnosis, with the names removed. Start with the release as is: every record is unique, because ZIP, date and gender together are a fingerprint. Look up any name from the public register and the diagnosis is right there. Now generalise. Dropping the day, then the month, helps very little. Only when ZIP is cut to two digits, birth date to a decade and gender is dropped do you reach k of 6: every record looks like at least five others. That's k-anonymity: each combination of quasi-identifiers appears at least k times. Then ask what you've lost: the data is now nearly useless for analysis, which is the real trade-off.
  • Askk-anonymity has a known weakness: if everyone in a group has the same diagnosis, the attacker learns it without finding the person. Where can you see that here? Answer: the "homogeneous" count in the stats, groups where all records share a diagnosis. That's why l-diversity and other measures exist, and why "just make k bigger" is not the whole answer.
  • Tipk-anonymity is from Sweeney, 2002. It only protects against the kinds of attack it models: more columns, outside datasets or repeated releases make it weaker. For many uses, the safer choices are not releasing record-level data at all, or adding noise to aggregates (see the privacy-noise slide). Names come from 10 first names times 6 surnames; any resemblance to real people is coincidence.
  • CheckThe dataset is invented and tiny, so the exact numbers are an illustration, not a statistic about real populations.

// COLLECT LESS 03

Minimise: every field needs a reason and an owner

A delivery-app sign-up form: tick what you collect

The ruleA field earns its place with a purpose, an owner who'll defend it, and a way to delete it. No owner, no field. GDPR Art. 25(2) calls this protection "by default": only what's necessary, for the time it's necessary, seen by those who need it.

~5 min

  • SayA real sign-up form grows by accretion: marketing wanted gender, someone ran a growth experiment that uploaded the phone contacts, nobody remembers why we have a date of birth. Tick and untick fields. Every field has a purpose and an owner; three of them have no owner at all. Each field also has a rough "harm if it leaks" score, so the risk total falls as you collect less. The "only what delivery needs" preset keeps three fields and still delivers parcels. The phone number is optional: ask for it when it's needed, at the order, not at sign-up.
  • AskIf you unticked a field and the product broke, what does that tell you? Answer: that the field is needed and its purpose is real. The test isn't "can we do without it" in the abstract: it's "can we say what it's for, who owns it, and when it goes away".
  • TipMinimisation also covers how much you derive and keep (precise location versus city, exact age versus over-18), how long you keep it, and who inside the company can see it. GDPR Art. 5(1)(c) says data must be "adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes"; Art. 25(2) is the "by default" rule. Many privacy bugs are just fields nobody removed.
  • CheckThe fields, owners and risk scores are invented. Not legal advice: the "needed" label depends on your product and your law.

// COLLECT LESS 04

Purpose and consent belong in code, not just in the policy

A service asks for data. For which purpose?

    1. Tag each field with the purposes it was collected for, in the schema.
    2. Make services declare the purpose when they read it, and log that.
    3. Treat consent as data: stored per purpose, checked at read time, revocable.
    4. New purpose? That's a design review, and maybe a new notice or a new consent.
    What goes wrongIn the 2022 Twitter case, details given to secure accounts were used for ad targeting. The fix isn't a better policy: it's that the ad system should not be able to read the "security" phone numbers at all.

    ~5 min

    • SayPurpose limitation (GDPR Art. 5(1)(b)) says data is collected for specified, explicit and legitimate purposes and not used in ways incompatible with them. In code that means the data store knows the purposes. Pick "delivery": the address is allowed. Pick "analytics": the address is blocked, and the order history only works if the user consented. Pick "marketing" with no consent: even the email is refused. A policy page can say all this and the system can still do the opposite. The check has to live where the data is read.
    • AskWhere would this check live in your system: in the database layer, an API gateway, each service? Answer: closer to the data is safer. A shared access layer or a view per purpose beats trusting every service to remember.
    • TipConsent is not the only legal basis (GDPR lists several: contract, legal obligation, legitimate interests and others), and the right one depends on your case; that's for your privacy lead. The engineering job is to make purpose and consent state machine-readable and enforced. The FTC's 25 May 2022 announcement is the source for the Twitter case: phone numbers and emails given for account security, used to target ads, 2014 to 2019, over 140 million users, a $150 million penalty for violating a 2011 order.
    • CheckThe purposes, fields and consent rules in the demo are invented and simplified. Real regimes differ on what needs consent.

    Draw the data map, including the places nobody drew

    Pick a kind of personal data

      A data map answersWhich systems hold it? Who owns each? How long does it stay? Which vendors are processors? Start from the user journey and follow the field into logs, caches, exports and tools.

      ~3 min

      • SayPick "Email" and count the places: the database, its replica, the cache, the logs, the error tracker, the support desk, the warehouse, the backups. Nine stores in this invented system, and the app only "has" one. Many of the rest have no owner, or no expiry. A data map is the first privacy control because everything later (encryption, redaction, deletion) needs to know where the data is. The ones that hurt are the side effects: logs, analytics, error reports and support tools, because they're added by developers for convenience and rarely reviewed.
      • AskWhich store on this list would surprise your legal team? Answer: usually the logs or the error tracker.
      • TipKeep the map as code or a table in the repo, next to the schema, so a pull request that adds a new store or field also updates it. Vendors that process data for you are "processors": each one needs a contract and a deletion path.

      // PROTECT WHAT YOU KEEP 02

      One key per person: encryption that makes deletion real

      Envelope encryptionA master key (the KEK, held in a key service or HSM) wraps a data key (DEK) for each customer. The field is encrypted with the DEK (AES-GCM, with the user ID bound in). Rotate the master key by re-wrapping keys, not re-encrypting every row.
      Crypto-shreddingDelete the customer's wrapped key and every copy of their ciphertext, in the database, replicas and backups, becomes unreadable. The catch: the key must not live in the backup too.

      ~7 min

      • SayThis runs real AES-GCM in your browser using the Web Crypto API. Each customer's email is encrypted with its own data key, and the data key is wrapped by a master key the "database" doesn't hold in the clear. Take a backup with ciphertext only. Now delete Dana's key. Dana's row is unreadable everywhere, including the backup. That's crypto-shredding: a deletion you can do in seconds across copies you can't easily reach. Now the mistake. Take a backup that includes the wrapped keys, delete Dana's key, and restore the backup: the keys come back, and so does Dana. A key backup needs its own retention, and deleting a user must delete their key from it too, or the key store should be the one thing that is deliberately not backed up with the data.
      • AskWhat does crypto-shredding not fix? Answer: plaintext copies. Anything decrypted into a cache, a log, an export or a vendor's system is outside the key. And it only protects the encrypted fields, so the data map still matters.
      • TipSee the Secrets management talk for envelope encryption and key services in depth. The master key here is a non-extractable Web Crypto key in this page: a real system uses a KMS or HSM, with access policies and logging. Field-level encryption also limits which services and which staff can read a field at all.
      • CheckIt's a teaching model of the key hierarchy, not a production design. Regulators' views on whether crypto-shredding counts as erasure vary, so ask your privacy lead before relying on it as your only deletion.
      • Next"Sometimes you need to join data without storing identities. That's pseudonyms."

      // PROTECT WHAT YOU KEEP 03

      Pseudonyms: a hashed email is not anonymous

      The rulesPlain SHA-256 of an email can be reversed by hashing guesses. HMAC with a secret key can't, until the key leaks. Normalise first (trim, lowercase), keep the key apart from the data, and remember: pseudonymised data is still personal data.

      ~5 min

      • SayWe want to count or join users in analytics without carrying their emails around. The tempting answer is SHA-256 of the email. The attacker already has a list of likely emails; they hash each and compare. The table shows three of four rows given away, because the input space is small and guessable. With HMAC-SHA-256 and a secret key, the same guesses match nothing. Leak the key and it's back to a lookup. So the key lives in a separate system with its own access control and rotation plan. Pseudonymisation lowers risk, and GDPR encourages it, but Recital 26 is clear that it's still personal data: you haven't left scope.
      • AskCheckpoint question for later: why is a plain SHA-256 of an email not anonymous? Answer: because the space of plausible emails is small and guessable; anyone with a candidate list can hash and compare.
      • TipUses the page's own SHA-256 and HMAC-SHA-256 (from the shared Crypto 101 lab code). For lookups ("find this user's pseudonym"), HMAC is also a deterministic index: the same email always gives the same value. If you need stronger separation, use tokenisation: a vault that hands out random tokens. GDPR Art. 4(5) defines pseudonymisation: data that can't be attributed to a person without additional information kept separately and protected. The EDPB adopted Guidelines 01/2025 on pseudonymisation on 16 January 2025.
      • CheckOn 11 November 2026 I still couldn't confirm a final version of the EDPB's guidelines after the public consultation (the EDPB's page lists comments due 14 March 2025; some summaries say 28 February): check the EDPB site. The "candidate list" is invented and tiny.
      • Next"What if you want to publish numbers, not records?"

      // PROTECT WHAT YOU KEEP 04 · SKIPPABLE

      Publishing numbers safely: add noise

      "How many people visited the clinic page?" The true answer is 42.

      
      
      The ideaAdd random noise sized to how much one person can change the answer (1 for a count) divided by ε. Small ε: strong privacy, noisy answers. And every question spends budget: ask the same thing again and again and the noise averages away.
      
    
      
  
      
  
      ~5 min
      • SayDifferential privacy is the principled alternative to "remove names and hope". The answer is the true count plus Laplace noise whose scale is 1/ε, because adding or removing one person changes a count by at most one. At ε of 1 the typical error is about 1: useful, and no single person's presence is detectable. Drag ε down and the answers become mostly noise. Push it up and you're publishing the true number with extra steps. Then ask 200 times: the average lands on 42. That's why the budget is spent per question, and why a real system tracks the total.
      • AskWhy can't you answer the same query a thousand times, each with fresh noise? Answer: the average converges on the true value. Repeated releases add up their ε costs (composition).
      • TipThe US Census Bureau used differential privacy for the 2020 Census; its Data Stewardship Executive Policy Committee set the total privacy-loss budget for the redistricting data at ε = 19.61 (Census Bureau, 9 June 2021), which critics called too high to give much protection: a good example that choosing ε is a policy decision, not just maths. Local DP (noise added on the user's device) and libraries from Google, OpenDP and others exist; use a vetted library, not this demo.
      • CheckThis demo draws Laplace noise with Math.random in your browser: fine for a lesson, not for real use (real systems use secure randomness and floating-point-safe mechanisms). The 19.61 figure is from the Census press release; another paper cites a different value for a different product.

      • SayLook at the raw lines: an email, a bearer token, a card number, a password inside a JSON body, a phone number, a cloud key, a postal address. Debug logging and error reporters are how personal data and secrets end up in places with no access control and a long retention. Meta was fined €91 million by Ireland's Data Protection Commission in September 2024 after passwords were stored unprotected on internal systems: the lesson isn't one company, it's that the log pipeline is part of your data store.           Retention that code enforces
          
  
          
    
          
      
          
      
          
    
          
    
          
      
          
      
          
      
          
      
          Make it a job, not a promiseRetention periods are written next to the schema, enforced by a scheduled job, and tested. A legal hold pauses deletion for the records in a dispute, and nothing else.
          
    
          
  
          
  
          ~4 min
          • SayFive classes of records, ten or so of each, aged from new to old. Press "run the purge job": anything older than its retention is deleted, and the table shows how many of each class remain. Tick "legal hold" on invoices and they stay, as they would under a tax rule, which varies by country. Edit a period and re-run. Without the job, the numbers show records kept long past their purpose. Retention is a design decision with three parts: how long you need it, how long the law makes you keep it (accounting and tax rules often require years), and how you'll prove it was deleted.           • SayThe demo starts in the state of a typical system: some stores delete quickly, the warehouse has no deletion path at all, and the vendors and the backups clear on their own schedules. The verdict says there's a copy that nothing will ever remove. Change the choices: join the warehouse to the delete job, call the vendors' deletion APIs, redact logs at write so there's nothing to delete, and crypto-shred the backups. A regulator isn't asking for instant magic: the ICO's guidance says that for backups, where data can't be overwritten immediately, you put it "beyond use" and let it age out on a known schedule, and are clear with the person about it.           • AskCheckpoint question: a user asks to be deleted. List every place their data might still live. Answer: primary DB, replicas, caches, search indexes, the warehouse and exports, logs, error tracker, analytics, support desk, email provider, backups, and any vendors' copies.

Build deletion as an event that every store subscribes to, with a "deleted" receipt you can check.

          ~6 min
          • SayLINDDUN is to privacy what STRIDE is to security: a set of seven threat types you walk through for each part of a data-flow diagram. Linking, Identifying, Non-repudiation, Detecting, Data disclosure, Unawareness and unintervenability, and Non-compliance. Pick a feature. For each letter there's a worked threat for that feature. For each, choose: not considered, accept with an owner and a review date, or reduce it in the design. You cover all seven and, as with security, the "accept" box needs a name and a date. Do it alongside your security threat model: same diagram, extra questions about people.
          • AskWhich of the seven does STRIDE miss entirely? Answer: mostly Detecting, Unawareness and Non-compliance. Security asks "can an attacker break it"; privacy also asks "should we be doing this, and do people know".
          • TipThe names are as on linddun.org's threat-types page (read 11 November 2026), which shows no version number I could find; it writes the sixth one "Unawareness & unintervenability" in its detailed section (its home page shortens it to "Unawareness"). The Threat modeling in practice talk covers STRIDE, data-flow diagrams and the workshop format. Threats here are examples written for this deck, not taken from the LINDDUN catalogue.
          • CheckThe three features and their threats are invented.

          Talk with a neighbour for a couple of minutes, then we share answers.
          
    
            
      
          1. A user asks to be deleted. List every place their data might still live.
            Hint
            Think beyond the main database.
            Answer
            Primary database, replicas, caches, search index, warehouse and exports, logs, error tracker, analytics and support vendors, email provider, backups. The data map is how you find them.
            2. 
      
          2. Your retention policy says 90 days, but nothing deletes old rows. What's missing?
            Hint
            A policy is not a mechanism.
            Answer
            A scheduled job with an owner, a test, an alert on failure, and a "delete after" date on each record. Plus an exception path for legal holds.
            3. 
      
          3. Which LINDDUN letter would catch "support staff can open any customer's live camera view"?
            Hint
            Think about who sees what.
            Answer
            Data disclosure (more data to more people than the purpose needs), with access control and audit as the fixes. It may also raise Non-compliance if the notice says otherwise.
