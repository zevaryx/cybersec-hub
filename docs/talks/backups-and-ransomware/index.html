// WHY THIS TALK

Backups are the quiet hero

Ransomware locks your files and asks for money. Dead disks, spilled coffee and accidental deletes do the same damage for free. One habit covers all of them: a backup you've actually tested.

  • The big cases you read about happen to companies, but the doors and the fixes are the same at home or in a small team.
  • Backups fix locked files. They can't fix stolen ones, which is why the doors matter too.
One old login, 2021Colonial Pipeline's chief executive told the US Senate the attacker used a legacy VPN profile that wasn't meant to be in use. He also called paying the ransom one of the toughest decisions of his life.
A portal without a second step, 2024UnitedHealth's chief executive told Congress criminals used compromised credentials on a Change Healthcare remote-access portal with no multi-factor authentication. Ransomware followed nine days later.
Backups nobody tested, 2017GitLab deleted production data by mistake, then found its nightly backup had failed silently ("The S3 bucket was empty"). It restored from a six-hour-old snapshot. Not ransomware, same lesson.

Sources: Blount testimony, June 2021 · Witty testimony, May 2024 · GitLab postmortem, Feb 2017

~3 min

  • SayThree short stories, one idea each. A single old login. A remote door with no second step. And a company that did have backups, on paper. None of them needed a genius, and none was about the fancy part of ransomware. The encryption is the last step, and the one backups answer.
  • TipColonial: Joseph Blount's written statement to the Senate Homeland Security committee (8 June 2021) says "we believe the attacker exploited a legacy virtual private network (VPN) profile that was not intended to be in use" and that the company was still working out how the credentials were obtained; on payment he wrote "It was one of the toughest decisions I have had to make in my life." The "single-factor authentication" detail comes from the hearing's question-and-answer, which I read only through the committee's summary and press reports, so it isn't on the slide. Change Healthcare: Andrew Witty's written testimony (1 May 2024) says that on 12 February criminals "used compromised credentials to remotely access a Change Healthcare Citrix portal... The portal did not have multi-factor authentication", and "Ransomware was deployed nine days later"; he also wrote "the decision to pay a ransom was mine. This was one of the hardest decisions I've ever had to make." The dollar figure reported in the press isn't in his written statement, so it's left out. GitLab: the postmortem of 31 January 2017 (published 10 February) says the nightly dump "failed silently" because it used PostgreSQL 9.2 tools on a 9.6 database, "The S3 bucket was empty, and there was no recent backup to be found anywhere", and the team restored a staging snapshot from about six hours before; it estimated losing "at least 5000 projects, 5000 comments, and roughly 700 users".

// HOW RANSOMWARE WORKS 01

Four ways in, and all of them are ordinary

1 · A convincing messageA link, an attachment or a fake call that gets someone to run something or hand over a login. The Phishing talk is the defence.
2 · Remote access left openRemote desktop, a VPN or a web portal with a password and no second step. The first two stories on the last slide started here.
3 · Software nobody updatedAcross all the breaches Verizon studied (November 2024 to October 2025), 31% began with an exploited software flaw, now the most common way in, ahead of stolen passwords.
4 · Passwords and other people's mistakesA password reused from an old breach, or a supplier or IT provider that gets hit first. CISA's guide lists both.

AT HOME the same doors look like a fake delivery text, a camera or storage box reachable from the internet, a router or NAS that never got updates, and a password used twice. In 2022 QNAP warned that DeadBolt was encrypting NAS drives directly connected to the internet, and told owners to turn off UPnP and port forwarding. See the Phishing, Passwords and Home Network talks.

Sources: CISA #StopRansomware Guide · Verizon DBIR 2026 · QNAP: DeadBolt statement, Sept 2022

~3 min

  • SayThis is not a list of exotic tricks. Every door here is a normal part of life: email, remote access, software, passwords. If you've seen the Phishing, Passwords and Home Network talks, this is the same advice with a bigger reason to care.
  • AskWhich of the four could happen in your home or office this month? Usually all of them; the one people forget is the exposed remote access, such as a camera, NAS or remote desktop reachable from the internet.
  • TipCISA's #StopRansomware Guide (September 2023) groups its prevention advice under: internet-facing vulnerabilities and misconfigurations, compromised credentials, phishing, precursor malware infection, advanced social engineering, and third parties and managed service providers. It doesn't rank them. I couldn't read the report's exact percentages for credential abuse or phishing, so they're not quoted. // HOW RANSOMWARE WORKS 02

What it does once it's in

A typical attack, in order
Gets inLooks aroundSteals a copyto threaten you withDeletes backups it can reachLocks your filesthen asks for money
  • Locking is ordinary encryption. The same maths that protects your bank, used against you, with the key kept by the criminals. The Crypto 101 talk explains the two kinds of keys.
  • Stealing first is common now. In Coveware's second-quarter 2026 cases, 76% involved data being taken. A backup can't un-steal it.
  • Backups are a target. Attackers delete or lock "online" backups they can reach: a drive left plugged in, a network folder, a synced cloud folder.
Locked filesA tested, separate backup fixes this. It's what the rest of the talk is about.
Stolen dataA backup doesn't help. Strong sign-ins, updates, and a plan for telling the people affected do.

Coveware notes that groups have pivoted from encrypting in place, which is processor-intensive and "can trigger alerts", and that "companies with a backup program can simply restore".

Sources: MITRE ATT&CK T1490 · MITRE T1486 · No More Ransom Q&A · Coveware Q2 2026

~3 min

  • SayIt's patient. The lock-up at the end is the loud part; before it, attackers usually look around, copy what they can, and go hunting for backups so you can't simply restore. That's why a backup needs to be somewhere an intruder on your network can't reach.
  • TipMITRE ATT&CK T1490 (version 1.6, 4 November 2026): adversaries "may delete or remove built-in data and turn off services designed to aid in the recovery" and may "delete 'online' backups that are connected to their network"; it names volume shadow copy deletion as a common method. T1486 describes "encrypting files or data on local and remote drives and withholding access to a decryption key". No More Ransom's Q&A says later families "use two separate keys... a public one to encrypt files, and a private one, which is needed for decryption", held by the criminals. Coveware's Q2 2026 report (published through Veeam): data exfiltration in 76% of cases; mid-market companies were 75% of cases; the quote about backups is from the report's own text. These are Coveware's incident-response cases, mostly organisations, so treat the numbers as a picture of reported cases and not of everyone.

// HOW RANSOMWARE WORKS 03

Why "just crack it" doesn't work

Key size
Guessing

  • The key is the only way in. Modern keys have 128 to 256 bits. Trying them all is not slow, it's out of the question.
  • Decryptors exist, but not from cracking. They come from mistakes: a flaw in the ransomware, a seized server, leaked keys.
  • Check before you do anything else: nomoreransom.org lists free decryptors, and says "not every type of ransomware has a solution".
  • Plan as if none exists. A backup is the plan that doesn't depend on luck.

Sources: No More Ransom · Crypto 101: AES

~2 min

  • ShowStart on 128 bits with a million computers: hundreds of times the age of the universe. Switch to a billion computers, an absurd rate: still about ten billion years to try every key. Click 56 bits, the old DES size: seconds or hours. That's the point of key length. Then 256 bits: the number has no meaning in human terms.
  • SayPeople imagine a clever expert breaking the lock. Real attacks on ransomware happen at the edges: the criminals made a mistake, or police seized their servers and got the keys. That's real and worth a free check, but it's luck, not a plan.
  • TipThe demo tries every key, which takes about twice as long as the average search, so a real attacker would on average need half as long: still not feasible. The rates are illustrative and absurdly generous: 1012 keys a second for one powerful computer, a million or a billion of them together. The age of the universe is about 13.8 billion years. Ransomware families differ: some use 128- or 256-bit symmetric ciphers for files with a public-key step protecting the file key; I describe that as "typically". No More Ransom's home page (checked 9 November 2026) lists decryptors for families including LockBit 3.0, Akira, Rhysida and Phobos / 8base, and says "At the moment, not every type of ransomware has a solution" and "The general advice is not to pay the ransom." Law enforcement has sometimes released keys: the FBI has said publicly that it holds many LockBit keys, but I could only read that second-hand, so it's not on the slide.

// HOW RANSOMWARE WORKS 04

Paying is a gamble, and a hard call

What the authorities say

  • FBI: "does not support paying a ransom". Payment doesn't ensure recovery and "encourages perpetrators to target more victims".
  • UK law enforcement (NCSC): they "do not encourage, endorse, nor condone" payment. It gives no guarantee, funds crime and can make you a repeat target.
  • US Treasury (OFAC): "strongly discourages" it, and says payments can risk breaking sanctions rules.
  • FTC, for small businesses: it's "up to you to determine whether the risks and costs of paying are worth the possibility of getting your files back".

Why it's still hard

  • Two chief executives told Congress that paying was one of the hardest decisions they'd ever made. Hospitals, pipelines and shops can't wait.
  • Paying may not even help: after LockBit's takedown, investigators found data "deleted" after payment hadn't been (Coveware).
19%of Coveware's Q2 2026 cases paid: a record low
$820M+paid on-chain in 2025 (Chainalysis; may be revised up)
3,611ransomware complaints to the FBI in 2025

Sources: FBI IC3 · NCSC · OFAC advisory, Sept 2021 · FTC · Chainalysis · IC3 2025 report

~3 min

  • SayBe even-handed. The official advice is not to pay, and the reasons are real: no guarantee, it feeds the next attack, and stolen data may be used anyway. But nobody in the room has ever faced the choice with a hospital or a business on the line, and the people who did call it one of the hardest decisions of their lives. The way out of the dilemma is to not need to pay.
  • TipFBI IC3 ransomware page: "The FBI does not support paying a ransom in response to a ransomware attack", payment doesn't ensure data recovery, and it "encourages perpetrators to target more victims". NCSC's "Mitigating malware and ransomware attacks" (published 2020, reviewed 2021, version 3.0): law enforcement "do not encourage, endorse, nor condone the payment of ransom demands"; paying doesn't guarantee access, leaves the computer infected, funds criminal groups, and makes you more likely to be targeted again. OFAC's updated advisory (21 September 2021): "The U.S. government strongly discourages all private companies and citizens from paying ransom or extortion demands"; facilitating payments "may risk violating OFAC regulations", liability can be strict, and maintaining offline backups is named as a mitigating factor. FTC: "Law enforcement doesn't recommend paying ransom, but it's up to you to determine whether the risks and costs of paying are worth the possibility of getting your files back." Numbers: Coveware's Q2 2026 report charts a 19% payment rate for its cases, which it calls a record low in a series that starts in Q1 2024; Chainalysis's 2026 report (26 February 2026) says more than $820 million was paid on-chain in 2025, 8% below a revised $892 million for 2024, adds that the total may rise as more is attributed, and puts the share of victims paying at a possible record-low 28%; the FBI's 2025 Internet Crime Report lists 3,611 ransomware complaints (3,156 in 2024) with $32.3 million in reported losses, which it says is an undercount because many victims report no loss amount. The three sources count different populations, so don't compare them as if they were one series.

// CHECKPOINT 1 · 5 MIN

Pause and discuss

Talk with a neighbour for a couple of minutes, then we share answers.

  1. Your files sync to the cloud. Ransomware locks your laptop. What happens to the cloud copy?

    Hint

    What does a sync service do when a file changes?

    Answer

    It follows the laptop. The locked files usually upload as the newest versions, often within moments. The NCSC warns that cloud services "often automatically synchronise immediately after your files have been replaced with encrypted copies". Version history may let you roll back, but only for a limited time, and only if you notice and the account is intact. So don't count a sync folder as your backup.

  2. Why can't an expert just crack the encryption and give your files back?

    Hint

    How many keys are there, and who has the right one?

    Answer

    Modern keys have 128 to 256 bits: trying every one is out of the question. The criminals hold the key. Free decryptors exist for some families, but they come from mistakes and seized keys, not cracking. Check nomoreransom.org, and plan on having none.

  3. A friend's small business is hit and they say "we'll just pay". What would you tell them?

    Hint

    Be kind: what could go wrong, and what can they do first?

    Answer

    That it's their call and an awful position to be in, but authorities advise against it: no guarantee, it funds more attacks, stolen data may still leak. Before deciding, check for a free decryptor, report it, ask what a restore would take, and get help. Next time: tested backups, so there's no choice to make.

5 min

  • ShowPairs talk for 2 minutes, then share. The timer starts by itself.

// BACKUPS THAT SURVIVE 01

Sync is not backup

  • Sync keeps the same files on every device, right now. Delete or lock one and the others follow.
  • Backup keeps older copies you can go back to. The best kind has at least one you can't reach from your laptop.
  • Sync services often keep a short history (this demo uses 30 days). That helps, and it ends.
  • An unplugged copy is only as new as the last time you plugged it in (here, two weeks).

The NCSC says cloud services "often automatically synchronise immediately after your files have been replaced with encrypted copies".

Sources: NCSC · Microsoft: protect your PC from ransomware

~3 min

  • ShowPress "Delete one file": it vanishes from the laptop and from the sync folder at once, but the versioned backup and the unplugged copy still have it. Start again, then "Ransomware locks the laptop": the locked files sync, and the versioned backup now holds locked files as its latest versions, with older good ones behind them. The unplugged copy is untouched. Now slide the "I notice after" bar and press "Try to restore": at 3 days everything but the laptop can recover; at 45 days the sync history has expired; at 120 days only the unplugged copy still has anything.
  • SaySync is brilliant for getting the same file on your phone and laptop. It's not a safety net, because it's designed to copy every change, including a bad one. A backup is the opposite: it holds on to the old state. The question is always how long until you notice.
  • TipAll four stores are illustrations, not products. The 30-day sync window matches what the big services say about deleted files and older versions (next slides); the versioned backup's 90 days and the unplugged copy's two-week age are made-up values for the demo. // BACKUPS THAT SURVIVE 02

3-2-1, and the extra 1

3copies of what matters: the original and two backups
2kinds of storage, so one failure doesn't take both
1copy off-site, away from a fire or a burglary
1copy offline or locked, so ransomware can't reach it
0errors: you've restored from it and it worked

The first three are the classic 3-2-1 rule. The last two are the modern extension (often written 3-2-1-1-0).

What counts as offline or locked?

YesA drive you unplug and keep somewhere else. Tape. Cloud storage with "locked" or immutable copies that nobody can change or delete, even with your login.
NoA drive that stays plugged in, a NAS that's always on, a sync folder, or a cloud account you're always signed in to. They can still help with old versions, but they aren't out of reach.
  • The NCSC's warning: two copies on one removable drive, or several copies in one cloud service, still fail together.
  • The FBI's advice for organisations: backups that are off-site or offline, encrypted, and immutable ("cannot be altered or deleted").

Sources: US-CERT: Data Backup Options (2012) · Veeam: 3-2-1-1-0 · NCSC · FBI IC3 2025

~2 min

  • SayThree copies, two kinds, one away. Then two upgrades for the ransomware era: one copy that can't be reached from your computer, and a zero for "I've tested it". Each number answers a different disaster: a dead disk, a fire, a locked laptop, and a backup that never worked.
  • TipThe 3-2-1 rule as US-CERT wrote it (Paul Ruggiero and Matthew Heckathorn, Carnegie Mellon, 2012): "3 – Keep 3 copies of any important file: 1 primary and 2 backups. 2 – Keep the files on 2 different media types to protect against different types of hazards. 1 – Store 1 copy offsite." It's often credited to the photographer Peter Krogh's writing on digital asset management; secondary sources disagree on the date, so I only cite the 2012 paper. The extra "1" and "0" are Veeam's 3-2-1-1-0 ("Make One Copy Offline, Air-gapped, or Immutable"; "zero errors" after recovery verification); it's a vendor-coined label, but CISA, the NCSC and the FBI all recommend offline or immutable copies. The NCSC says not to rely on "two copies on one removable drive or multiple copies in one cloud service", and not to keep backup devices permanently connected. FBI IC3 2025 report: "ensure all backup data is encrypted, immutable (i.e., cannot be altered or deleted)". The Yes/No box is my summary, not a quote.

// BACKUPS THAT SURVIVE 05

Encrypt your backups, and know where the key lives

Where would you keep the backup password?

    • Why encrypt: a lost or stolen backup drive is otherwise readable by anyone. CISA's advice is offline, encrypted backups.
    • The catch: lose the password and the backup is gone. Apple says "You can't restore an encrypted backup without its password", and a new password doesn't open the old ones.
    • So the key needs its own backup, in a different place from what it protects.

      Sources: CISA guide · Apple: forgotten encrypted-backup password · Apple: Time Machine password

      ~2 min

      • ShowMark each place Good or Bad, then check. The rule of thumb: the key shouldn't depend on the thing it protects, and it shouldn't be where a thief of the backup would find it. Two safe places beat one.
      • SayEncrypting backups protects you from a stolen drive and costs you if you lose the password. So treat the password like the backup: store it in at least two places, away from the laptop.
      • TipCISA's guide: "Maintain offline, encrypted backups of critical data". Apple (page published 14 September 2026): "You can't restore an encrypted backup without its password"; on iOS 11 or later you can make a new encrypted backup by resetting the password, but "Creating a new password doesn't allow you to access previous encrypted backups". For Time Machine, Apple says "To restore files from encrypted Time Machine backups, you must provide your backup password" and advises choosing a hint that helps you remember it; I found no Apple statement on recovering a forgotten one. Password managers have their own recovery steps: set those up too.

      Pause and discuss

      Talk with a neighbour for a couple of minutes, then we share answers.

      1. Which of your backups would survive a fire at home?

        Hint

        Where does each copy physically live, and does any of it live elsewhere?

        Answer

      2. A backup drive stays plugged into your computer all the time. Is it an offline copy, and what would you change?

        Hint

        Could ransomware on your computer reach it?

        Answer

        No: anything the computer can reach, ransomware can usually reach. Keep a second drive unplugged and plug it in only to update it, ideally stored away from the computer. The same goes for an always-on NAS.

      3. Your backup is encrypted and you've forgotten the password. What now?

        Hint

        Does anyone hold a spare key?

        Answer

        Usually nothing: Apple says an encrypted backup can't be restored without its password, and a new password doesn't open old backups. Check your password manager, a printed copy or a trusted person. Next time, store the key in two places before you rely on the backup.

      5 min

      • ShowPairs talk for 2 minutes, then share. The timer starts by itself.
      • TipIf someone asks about RAID or mirrored disks: they keep working when one disk dies, and they faithfully mirror a deleted or locked file. Useful, but not a backup.

        The first hour

        1. 1 · DisconnectUnplug the network cable or turn off Wi-Fi on the affected devices. Don't power them off if you can avoid it: it can destroy evidence.
        2. 2 · Don't wipe, and don't pay yetKeep the ransom note (photograph it with another device). Don't reinstall yet: evidence matters, and a decryptor may exist.
        3. 3 · Protect the backupsUnplug backup drives, and never plug them into an infected machine. Check other devices and accounts.
        4. 4 · Report itIn the US: the FBI's ic3.gov and CISA's cisa.gov/report. Elsewhere: your national cyber agency or police. You only need to report once.
        5. 5 · Look for a free decryptornomoreransom.org can identify the ransomware and may have a tool. Check it before deciding anything.
        6. 6 · Rebuild from cleanRestore from a known-good point onto a clean system, scan the backup first, and change passwords from a clean device.

        Tell the people whose data may have been taken. For a business, that's customers and staff, and sometimes a regulator.

        • SayWhen it happens, you'll be stressed and everyone will have an opinion. Six steps, in this order. The first two are about not making it worse. The rest are about getting help and getting back.
        • TipFTC's small-business guidance: "Immediately disconnect infected devices from your network without powering them down"; "Certain information useful for investigation may be lost if you power down devices"; "Report the attack to your local FBI office". CISA's guide says to isolate affected systems, and treats powering down as a last resort "only if it is not possible to temporarily shut down the network or disconnect affected hosts"; it advises taking "a system image and memory capture of a sample of affected devices" and recommends coordinating out of band, "such as phone calls", because attackers may be reading your messages. CISA's page says one report is enough: "Victims only need to report once, because the agencies share the report with each other", through its reporting tool or the FBI's IC3. The NCSC says to scan backups for malware and connect them only to known clean devices before recovery, and mentions No More Ransom. "Don't wipe yet" is my summary of the evidence-preservation advice, not a quoted rule: CISA's guide has no explicit "do not wipe" line, and it does tell you to rebuild affected systems once they're isolated and evidence is captured. Elsewhere: the UK's NCSC pages say organisations hit by ransomware should report to Action Fraud (now called Report Fraud), the NCSC and, where personal data is involved, the ICO; other countries have equivalents; look up yours before you need it. I couldn't verify every country's current channel, so this slide names only the US ones.

        // THE BAD DAY 03

        For small teams: who to call, and practise once a year

        A one-page contact sheet, printed

        • Your IT person or provider, with a phone number that isn't an email address.
        • Your insurer's incident line, if you have cyber cover.
        • A lawyer, the local FBI office or police, and your bank.
        • Where the offline backup lives, who can restore from it, and where the backup password is kept.
        • Keep a paper copy. During an incident the email and shared drive may be what's down, and attackers may be watching them.

        The FTC's small-business advice: have a plan, "put this plan in writing and share it with everyone who needs to know".

        // THE BAD DAY 04

        Six habits to take home

        1 · 3-2-1, plus one you can't reachThree copies, two kinds, one off-site, and one offline or locked.
        2 · Know sync from backupSync copies mistakes. A backup remembers old versions.
        3 · Ask how far backLook up your provider's history window, and cover the gap.
        4 · Encrypt, and keep the key twiceA manager and a paper copy, never next to the backup.
        5 · Restore a file, twice a yearA backup you haven't restored is a hope.
        6 · Close the doorsUpdates on, a second step on every sign-in, no remote access left open.
