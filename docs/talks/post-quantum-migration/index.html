// WHY NOW

The standards exist, and the deployment has started

27 Oct 2026NIST IR 8547 is final: quantum-vulnerable public-key crypto deprecated after 2030, disallowed after 2035
Aug 2026RFC 10024 defines three hybrid key-exchange groups for TLS 1.3, including X25519MLKEM768
~70%of browser traffic to Cloudflare uses hybrid ML-KEM; about 15% of origins do (29 Sep 2026)
2029Google and Cloudflare each say they aim to be fully post-quantum by then (company targets)
  • Two threats, two clocks. Recorded traffic can be read later; a signature can only be forged once the computer exists. They have different urgency and different fixes.
  • The estimates keep falling. Factoring RSA-2048: 20 million noisy qubits in 2019, under 1 million in 2025, and newer designs claim far fewer on different hardware assumptions.
  • Nobody has a date. Plan around a range and your own lead time.
  • Key exchange is moving. Browsers, CDNs, Apple's OS 26, OpenSSL and OpenSSH already offer hybrid key exchange (slide 16). Your job there is to find what doesn't.
  • Signatures and the long tail aren't. Certificates, firmware, hardware, vendors and archives take years. That's where a plan matters.
  • The plan is mostly inventory. You can't migrate what you can't find.

SOURCES NIST IR 8547 (final) · RFC 10024 · Cloudflare, 29 Sep 2026 · Google Cloud · Cloudflare roadmap · Gidney 2025

// THE THREAT, PRECISELY 01

What a quantum computer breaks, and what it doesn't

Pick a primitive

  • Shor's algorithm (1994) solves factoring and discrete logarithms in polynomial time. That is RSA, finite-field Diffie-Hellman, elliptic curves, and every signature built on them.
  • Grover's algorithm (1996) speeds up searching by a square root. That halves the exponent in the best case, so a 128-bit symmetric key keeps a respectable margin and a 256-bit one a large one.
  • So the target is public-key crypto. Symmetric crypto and hashes need sizes checked, not replacing.
CategoryEquivalent attack
1Key search on AES-128
2Collision search on SHA-256
3Key search on AES-192
4Collision search on SHA3-384
5Key search on AES-256

SOURCES NIST IR 8547, Table 1 and section 2.1.3

~4 min

  • SayBe precise, because both kinds of hype are costly. "Quantum breaks encryption" is wrong: it breaks one family of encryption, the public-key family. And "AES is fine, so we're fine" is also wrong: the public-key part is how you agreed on the AES key.
  • ShowClick through the chips. Every public-key primitive lands in the same bucket. The five NIST categories on the right are the yardstick the post-quantum standards use: they're defined by the cost of breaking AES or SHA, which is why AES-128 is the floor.
  • AskSomeone says "we use AES-256, so we're covered." What's the weak link? Answer: how the key got there. If it was agreed with ECDH or wrapped with RSA, a recording of that exchange gives the key later.
  • CheckThe statement that approved symmetric primitives at 128 bits or more are believed to meet at least Category 1 is from IR 8547 (October 2026), section 2.1.3, and the category table is its Table 1. ChaCha20 isn't a NIST-approved primitive; the slide says so. Shor 1994 and Grover 1996 are the standard references.

// THE THREAT, PRECISELY 02

Harvest now, decrypt later: and forge later

  • Confidentiality gives the attacker a head start: record now, decrypt later. The damage is fixed now.
  • Authenticity has none: a forgery needs the computer at forging time, and only works while people still trust the key.
  • So key exchange first and signatures second, except for signatures that must last decades or sit in hardware.

    // CHECKPOINT 1 · 5 MIN

    Pause and discuss

    Talk with a neighbour for a couple of minutes, then we share answers.

    1. Why is encrypted traffic recorded today at risk, while a signature made today mostly isn't?

      Hint

      When does the attacker need the quantum computer in each case?

      Answer

      A recording can be made now and decrypted later, so the damage is fixed today. A forgery needs the computer at the time of forging, and only works if the key is still trusted, so migrating keys and verifiers before then avoids it. The exceptions are signatures that must last decades and hardware you can't update.

    2. Your health records must stay secret for 30 years and a migration would take 4. The earliest plausible computer is 25 years away. What is your verdict, and which lever do you have?

      Hint

      Add the first two numbers and compare with the third.

      Answer

      30 + 4 = 34 years against 25: exposed by 9 years. The levers are a shorter migration (start with the key exchange that protects the records in transit and the keys wrapping them at rest), a shorter retention period (delete what you don't need), and protecting the data with more than one layer meanwhile.

    3. Which of AES-128, SHA-256, RSA-3072, ECDSA P-256 and ChaCha20 are on the "quantum-vulnerable" list?

      Hint

      Which ones rest on factoring or discrete logarithms?

      Answer

      RSA-3072 and ECDSA P-256. The others are symmetric or hash functions, where the question is key and output size, not replacement.

    // THE NEW ALGORITHMS 01

    Lattices in one slide: a puzzle that needs noise

    • The puzzle: publish many equations b = A·s + e (mod q), where s is the secret and e is small random noise. Without the noise it's school algebra. With it, finding s is the learning-with-errors problem, believed hard even for quantum computers.
    • Encrypt a bit by adding up a random subset of the equations. Decrypt by subtracting what the secret predicts: what's left is small for a 0 and about half of q for a 1.
    • ML-KEM uses a structured version (Module-LWE) with n = 256, q = 3329 and 2, 3 or 4 modules. This toy uses q = 97 and 6 unknowns: far too small to be safe.

    Teaching toy. It is not ML-KEM and not secure; it shows why the noise is there.

    ~5 min
    • SayYou don't need to follow the maths: you need two ideas. First, the security is a puzzle that is easy to state and believed hard to solve, even for a quantum computer. Second, it only works because of the noise: too little and it's school algebra, too much and honest users can't decrypt either.
    • ShowSet E to 0 and press "Try to solve": 16 equations in 6 unknowns, no noise, and the solver recovers the secret instantly (the egg). Set E to 1 and try again: the equations now contradict each other, so there is no exact solution to find. Then slide E up to 10 and watch the failure count climb: honest decryption starts to fail. At 30 it fails a lot. Real schemes pick parameters so that failure is astronomically unlikely and the attack is hopelessly hard; this toy can't have both.
    • AskWhy can't we just make the noise huge for safety? Answer: then the legitimate decryption can't tell a 0 from a 1.
    • CheckFIPS 203 (13 August 2024), Table 2: n = 256, q = 3329, k = 2, 3, 4 for ML-KEM-512, -768 and -1024. The failure counts are measured live over 300 random bits and keys.

    ML-KEM: key encapsulation, and what it costs
    
  
    
    
    
      
      
        
    • A KEM has three steps: KeyGen makes a key pair; Encaps(public key) gives a shared secret and a ciphertext; Decaps(secret key, ciphertext) gives the same secret back.
      • 
        
    • It replaces Diffie-Hellman's "both sides contribute" with "one side encapsulates to the other". Same result: a shared key.

    • SayA classical TLS handshake carries five signatures and two public keys: the leaf and intermediate certificates' signatures, two certificate-transparency proofs, and the server's own signature, plus the two keys in the chain. Swap each for a post-quantum one and the server's first flight grows from about 2 KB to over 20 KB. That crosses TCP's initial window, so it costs a round trip.     // THE NEW ALGORITHMS 05
    The lesson of SIKE: why hybrids exist
    
  
    
    
      
      
    1. By mid-2022SIKE, built on isogenies between elliptic curves, has advanced to the fourth round of NIST's process.
      2. 
      
    2. July 2022Castryck and Decru publish a key-recovery attack on SIDH, the basis of SIKE, and break the level-1 parameters.
      3. 
      
    3. On whatAn ordinary computer: about an hour on a single core in the first version of the paper, about ten minutes in the final one.
      4. 
      
    4. AfterThe attack was published at EUROCRYPT 2023. The schemes NIST went on to standardise rest on lattices and hashes, not isogenies.
      5. 
    
    
    
    
      
      
        
    • Nothing quantum was needed. A classical break of a post-quantum candidate, found by mathematicians after it had survived years of attention.
      • 
        
    • A new hardness assumption is only as good as the analysis it has had. Standardisation is a milestone, not a proof.
      • 
      
    
      
      
        
    • Hybrids are the hedge. Combine the new algorithm with a classical one: the key is safe if either holds. You pay some bytes and some complexity.
      • 
        
    • NIST says hybrids are allowed, add complexity, and are "typically expected to be temporary measures that lead to a second transition".
      • 
      
    
    
    
    
    SOURCES Castryck and Decru, ePrint 2022/975 · NIST IR 8547, section 3.2
    
  
    
  
    ~3 min
    • SayThis is the most useful story in the talk for planners. A candidate that had passed the screening was broken in an afternoon by an ordinary laptop. It wasn't a quantum attack and it didn't touch the lattice schemes, but it's why nobody serious wants to rely on a single new assumption yet. The hybrid costs about a kilobyte, and it buys you insurance against a lattice surprise.
    • AskIf hybrids are "temporary", what does that imply for your plans? Answer: a second migration later. So the agility you build now is worth more than any single algorithm choice.
    • CheckThe ePrint abstract (final version, last revision 15 May 2023; received 30 July 2022) says the attack breaks SIKEp434 "in about ten minutes on a single core", and calls SIKE the instantiation that "recently advanced to the fourth round of NIST's standardization effort".     Talk with a neighbour for a couple of minutes, then we share answers.
    
    
      
      
    1. Why combine a classical and a post-quantum algorithm instead of switching outright?
      Hint
      Think of SIKE.
      Answer
      Because the new assumptions haven't had decades of analysis. If either half holds, the session key stays safe: a break of the new algorithm doesn't expose today's traffic, and a quantum computer doesn't either. The cost is some bytes and more complexity, and NIST expects hybrids to be a temporary stage.
      2. 
      
    2. A server's first flight grows from about 2 KB to about 23 KB when you move every signature in the chain to ML-DSA-65. What breaks or slows, and what is the main fix being worked on?
      Hint
      The initial TCP window is 10 segments, about 14.6 KB.
      Answer
      The flight no longer fits in the first window, so it needs an extra round trip; QUIC's anti-amplification limit and constrained links hurt too. The work is on shrinking what's sent: Merkle Tree Certificates replace most signatures with a short inclusion proof.
      3. 
      
    3. Which post-quantum algorithms are final standards today, and which are still being written?
      Hint
      Three FIPS were published in August 2024.
      Answer
      Final: ML-KEM (FIPS 203), ML-DSA (FIPS 204) and SLH-DSA (FIPS 205), plus the stateful LMS and XMSS in SP 800-208. Still under development as of October 2026 per IR 8547: a FIPS for FN-DSA (Falcon) and one for HQC.

    Hybrid key exchange in TLS 1.3, live
    
  
    
    
    
      
      
        
    • X25519MLKEM768 (RFC 10024, code point 0x11EC): the client sends an ML-KEM-768 encapsulation key and an X25519 share (1,216 bytes); the server replies with a ciphertext and its X25519 share (1,120 bytes). The two secrets are joined, ML-KEM first (64 bytes), and go into the normal TLS key schedule.
      • 
        
    • The key is safe if either half holds.
      •

    // HYBRID TODAY 03
    Certificates: why the PKI is harder to move
    
  
    
    
    
      
    
        
        
        
      
    
      
    
        
    –signatures
    
        
    –public keys
    
        
    –bytes in the certificate chain
    
      
    
      
    
      
      
        
    • Many parties must move together: browsers' root programmes, CAs, logs, servers, clients, and every client that is never updated.
      • 
        
    • A bigger signature appears five times per handshake. Replacing each one is a bandwidth problem, not only a maths one.
      • 
      
    
    
    
    
    
      
    Merkle Tree Certificates proposalA CA batches certificates into an append-only Merkle tree and signs only the tree head. A client checks a short inclusion proof against it. Issuance and logging are coupled. In Cloudflare's design, a handshake carries one public key, one signature and a proof under 1 KB.
    
      
      
        
    • Status: an IETF working-group draft; a Chrome experiment ran with Cloudflare in 2026 (wound down in August); Cloudflare targets early 2027 for Chrome's quantum-resistant root store, under a draft policy. Google Cloud lists Merkle Tree Certificates for its own CA in 2028.
      • 
        
    • Until then: ML-DSA for authentication to origins and for internal PKI, and hybrid key exchange for confidentiality.
      • 
      
    
      
    SOURCES Cloudflare, 29 Sep 2026 · Cloudflare, 9 Jul 2026 · Google Cloud, 11 Aug 2026
    
    
    
  
    
  
    ~6 min
    • SayKey exchange is a two-party matter inside one handshake. Certificates involve the whole web. Every client has to trust a post-quantum root before a server can use one, and browsers decide that. The size problem is real too: a chain carries five signatures. That's why the design that's getting attention changes the structure rather than just the algorithm.
    • ShowPick ML-DSA-44 and then ML-DSA-65 for the CA signatures: the certificate chain goes from about 1.7 KB to about 13.6 KB and then about 18.4 KB. Then SLH-DSA-128s: about 33 KB. Remember this is a model with an assumed per-certificate overhead; the signature and key sizes are the real ones.
    • CheckMTC details are from Cloudflare's post of 29 September 2026: a CA signs only the tree head; the handshake in the landmark-relative form sends one public key, one signature and an inclusion proof of under 1 kB; Chrome's draft policy requires at least two cosignatures; a Chrome Beta 146 experiment served 50% of traffic and wound down in August 2026; Cloudflare "targets early 2027" for Chrome's Quantum-resistant Root Store, which is governed by a draft policy, and its CA isn't trusted for MTC issuance yet. The post links two different IETF draft names (plants and transcert), so the slide says only "a working-group draft". Google Cloud's roadmap lists "Google Trust Service: Merkle Tree Certificates" under 2028 and says the certificate transition will follow IETF standardisation. Nothing here is final, so treat MTCs as a proposal. The sizes in the simulator are real; the overhead is assumed.

    • A forged signature needs the quantum computer at the time of forging, but a device in the field can't change its verifier. If the check is in ROM, today's choice has to outlast the product.
