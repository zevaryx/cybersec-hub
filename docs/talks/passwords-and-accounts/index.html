// BREAK-INS 01

The attacker's shopping list

Almost nobody "hacks" a password by cleverness. They reuse, trick, guess or reset.

Reused passwordsA site you used once leaks. Attackers try the same email and password everywhere else.ALSO CALLEDCredential stuffing
PhishingA convincing fake login page, email or text gets you to type it in yourself.ALSO CALLEDFake login pages
GuessingThe same few thousand common passwords, tried against millions of accounts.ALSO CALLEDPassword spraying
ResettingTaking over your email or phone number, then using "forgot password".ALSO CALLEDSIM swaps, recovery abuse

THE GOOD NEWS One habit each stops all four: unique passwords, a password manager, two-factor or passkeys, and protected recovery.

~3 min

  • SayFour ways in. None needs a genius: they're cheap and automated.
  • AskWhich of these do you think is most common? Answer: reused and stolen passwords are behind a huge share of account takeovers.
  • SayThe rest of the talk is one fix per card.
  • Next"Let's watch reuse go wrong."

// BREAK-INS 03
What a website should store

  

    

    

    
RED FLAG If a site can email you your current password, it's storing it in a way that can be reversed. Use a unique password there, and expect it to leak.

  

  
~3 min
  • SayThree ways a site can store your password, from awful to good.
  • ShowPlain text: a leak is a list of passwords. A fast hash: common passwords are cracked instantly from a pre-computed table. A salted, slow hash (Argon2id): every guess costs real time, for each user separately.
  • SayYou can't see which one a site uses, so assume the worst and never reuse. Crypto 101 has the full story.

// BREAK-INS 04
Checking for leaks without giving your password away

  

    

      

      

      
Simulated: nothing is sent anywhere, and the sample response is made up. The real service is the "Pwned Passwords" check at haveibeenpwned.com.

    

    

      
How it works

      
    
        
  1. Your device hashes the password (SHA-1).
    2. 
        
  2. It sends only the first 5 characters of the hash.
    3. 
        
  3. The service replies with every leaked hash that starts the same way: hundreds of them.
    4. 
        
  4. Your device looks for the rest of its hash in that list, privately.
    5. 
      

      
Why this is safeFive characters match hundreds of different leaked passwords, so the service never learns which one is yours. Password managers and browsers run this check for you.

    

  

  
~3 min
  • ShowType "password123": the first 5 characters of its hash are all that's sent, and the rest is found in the sample reply. Try something random: not found.
  • SayThis trick is called k-anonymity: you hide in a crowd of hundreds of matches.
  • TipRecommend checking your email address at haveibeenpwned.com tonight; it's on the final checklist.
  • Next"Time to discuss."

    Hint

    Answer

    Hint
    How many leaked passwords share those 5 characters?
    Answer

// PASSWORDS 01
Length beats complexity

  

    

      

        
A random password

        

          
        

        

          
          
          
          
        

      

      

        
A random passphrase

        

          
        

        
Words picked at random from a 7,776-word list (dice-based "Diceware"), for example cactus orbit ladle frozen tiptoe.

      

    

    

    
CURRENT GUIDANCE US guidance (NIST SP 800-63B) favours long passwords, drops forced symbol rules and regular forced changes, and checks new passwords against leak lists.

  

  
~4 min
  • Show8 characters of lowercase and digits: a fast hash falls in minutes. Add symbols: better, but drag the length up instead and watch it climb much faster.
  • ShowFive random words beat a typical "complex" password, and they're easier to type.
  • SayThe big caveat: these numbers assume truly random choices. "Summer2026!" isn't random; attackers try those patterns first.
  • Next"So let the computer do the picking."






  
// PASSWORDS 02
Let a computer pick

  

    

      

        
        
        
      

      

      

    

    

      
Why random wins

      
    
        
  • People pick patterns: names, years, a capital at the start, "!" at the end. Attackers' guess lists are built from those patterns.
    • 
        
  • A secure random generator has no pattern to guess (Crypto 101's randomness slide).
    • 
        
  • You don't need to remember it: that's the password manager's job.
    • 
        
  • The only passwords worth memorising: your password manager's, your device's, and your email's.
    • 
      

    

  

  
~3 min
  • ShowPress Generate a few times. 20 random characters: far beyond anything an attacker can try.
  • SayNobody remembers these, and nobody should have to. That's what a password manager is for.
  • Next"So what is a password manager, really?"






  
// PASSWORDS 03
Password managers

  

    

      

        
RemembersA different random password for every site.

        
Fills inOnly on the exact website it was saved for.

        
SyncsAcross your phone and computers, encrypted.

        
WarnsAbout reused, weak or leaked passwords.

      

      
Built into your browser or phone, or a separate app. Any reputable one beats reusing passwords.

    

    

      
Autofill as a phishing alarm

      

      

    

  

  
~4 min
  • SayWhat it does: remembers, fills in, syncs, warns.
  • ShowSwitch the site. On the real bank.example it fills in. On the look-alikes it refuses, because it matches the real address, not how the page looks.
  • SayIf your manager doesn't fill in where it normally would, stop and check the address before typing anything.
  • Ask"Isn't it risky to put all your passwords in one place?" Answer: next slide. It's far less risky than reusing one password everywhere.






  
// PASSWORDS 04
How the vault is locked

  

    

      

        
        
      

      
Press "Lock the vault".

      

        
        
      

      

    

    

      
master passwordslow hash (Argon2id)keyAES encrypts the vault

      
    
        
  • The key is made on your device from your master password. The company never sees it.
    • 
        
  • The company stores only the encrypted vault. If it's breached, attackers get a locked box.
    • 
        
  • The slow hash makes every guess at your master password expensive.
    • 
        
  • So the master password must be long and used nowhere else.
    • 
      

    

  

  
~3 min
  • ShowLock the vault: the master password goes through a slow hash to make a key, and the vault becomes ciphertext. That ciphertext is all the company stores.
  • ShowUnlock with a wrong master password: the tag check fails. Note the time each guess costs.
  • SayThat's why password manager breaches are bad but survivable: if your master password is strong, the stolen vault stays locked.
  • TipDetails vary by product; this is the common design.






  
// PASSWORDS 05
Your master password, and the three you remember

  

    

      
Make it a passphraseFive or six random words. Long, and easy to type on a phone.

      
Use it nowhere elseNot for email, not anywhere. It protects everything else.

      
Protect the manager tooTurn on two-factor or a passkey for the password manager account itself.

    

    

      
Write it down? It's OKA paper copy kept safe at home beats forgetting it. Burglars rarely target sticky notes; online attackers can't reach paper.

      
Plan for emergenciesMost managers offer emergency access or a printable recovery kit for family.

      
The other two to memoriseYour device unlock code and your main email password. Everything else lives in the manager.

    

  

  
~2 min
  • SayThree passwords to remember: the manager's master password, your device's unlock code, and your email. Email matters because it resets everything else.
  • SayWriting the master password on paper at home is fine for most people; the threat is online, not burglars.
  • Next"Even a perfect password can be stolen by phishing. That's where two-factor comes in."







  
// TWO-FACTOR 01
Something you know, have, or are

  

    

      
KnowA password or PIN.WEAKNESSCan be leaked, guessed or phished

      
HaveYour phone, an authenticator app, a security key.WEAKNESSCan be lost; some kinds can be phished

      
AreFingerprint or face, usually to unlock the "have".WEAKNESSCan't be changed, so it stays on your device

    

    
Two-factor means needing two different kinds, so one stolen thing isn't enough.

    
Two passwords is still one factor. A password plus a code from your phone is two.

  

  
~2 min
  • SayKnow, have, are. Two-factor combines two different kinds.
  • SayYour fingerprint usually doesn't go to the website at all: it unlocks something on your phone, which does the real work.
  • Next"Let's make a real two-factor code, right now."

Scan with any authenticator app (Google Authenticator, Microsoft Authenticator, Aegis, 2FAS, your password manager…). It shows up as "Cybersec Hub: Demo"; delete it afterwards.

    

    

      
Shared secret (both sides have it)

------

NO NETWORK NEEDED Your phone and the website each work out the code from the same secret and the current time. Nothing is sent between them.

    

  

  
~4 min
  • ShowAsk everyone to scan the QR code with an authenticator app. Their phones show the same 6 digits as the slide, changing every 30 seconds.
  • SayThe phone and the site agreed on a secret once (that's the QR code). Since then, both compute the code from the secret and the clock. No signal, no SMS, no internet needed.

// TWO-FACTOR 03
How the 6 digits are made

  

    
secret+ time stepHMAC-SHA1pick 4 byteslast 6 digits

    

    
This is TOTP (RFC 6238), used by almost every authenticator app. The code changes every 30 seconds and can't be worked backwards to the secret.

  

  
~3 min
  • SayStep 1: the time step is the number of 30-second periods since 1970. Step 2: mix it with the secret using HMAC, a keyed hash (Crypto 101 Part 2). Step 3: the last byte says where to cut out 4 bytes. Step 4: keep the last 6 digits.
  • ShowWait for the step to tick over: everything changes at once.
  • SayWeakness: if a fake site asks for the code, you can type it in, and an attacker can use it within those 30 seconds. Codes beat passwords alone, but they can still be phished.
  • Next"So which kind of two-factor stops which attack?"






  
// TWO-FACTOR 04
Which method stops which attack?

RULE Any two-factor is far better than none. SMS is the weakest, but still worth it if it's the only option. Prefer an app, and passkeys where offered.

  

  
~4 min
  • ShowClick "Password leaked": every two-factor method stops it. That alone blocks most account takeovers.
  • ShowClick "Look-alike site": codes can be typed into the fake site and relayed live. Only passkeys and security keys are tied to the real address.
  • SaySIM swap: someone convinces your carrier to move your number to their SIM, then receives your SMS codes. "Approval spam": flooding your phone with login prompts until you tap yes; number matching makes that much harder.

// CHECKPOINT 2 · 5 MIN
Pause and discuss

  

    
Talk with a neighbour for a couple of minutes, then we share answers. Guesses are welcome.

    
    
      
  1. Why is a code from an authenticator app safer than one sent by SMS?
    Hint
    Where does each code come from, and who else could receive it?
    Answer
    The app makes the code on your phone from a stored secret. An SMS travels through your phone number, which an attacker can take over with a SIM swap.
    2. 
      
  2. A friend says: "I don't need a password manager, I have a system." What would you ask them?
    Hint
    What happens to a "system" once one of its passwords leaks?
    Answer
    Whether one leaked password reveals the pattern for the rest, and whether every password is unique and random. A pattern like "Site name + 2026!" falls the moment one leaks.
    3. 
      
  3. You lose your phone with your authenticator app on it. What should you have set up beforehand?
    Hint
    How else could you prove it's you?
    Answer
    Recovery codes stored somewhere safe, a second factor (a backup key or a second device), or an authenticator that backs up securely. Part 4 covers this.
    4. 
    

  

  
5 min
  • ShowPairs talk for 2 minutes, then share.
  • SayQ3 leads into the last part: recovery.
  • TipTiming: Part 3 should finish around minute 58.







  
// RECOVERY 01
Don't lock yourself out

  

    

      
Recovery codes

      
When you turn on two-factor, most sites offer one-time backup codes like these. Each works once.

      

      
    

    

      
    
        
  • Save recovery codes in your password manager and on paper at home.
    • 
        
  • Add a second factor: a backup security key, or a passkey on a second device.
    • 
        
  • Keep recovery details current: backup email and phone number.
    • 
        
  • Protect your phone number: ask your carrier for a PIN or port-out protection.
    • 
        
  • Set up a trusted contact or legacy contact where offered.
    • 
      

      
RECOVERY IS A WAY IN Attackers use it too. Weak security questions or an unprotected phone number can undo strong passwords.

    

  

  
~3 min
  • SayTwo-factor's biggest risk is locking yourself out. Recovery codes are the fix: one-time codes for when your phone is gone.
  • SayBut every way back in is also a way in for attackers. Treat recovery options like passwords: few, and protected.
  • TipSecurity questions: if a site forces them, give random answers and store them in the password manager.
