// WHY THIS TALK

The server carries everything, so it must see nothing

An app's servers handle every message you send. End-to-end protocols are why they can't read them, and why a leaked key doesn't have to unlock your whole history.

2004: stop keeping one key for everythingBorisov, Goldberg and Brewer's "Off-the-Record Communication, or, Why Not To Use PGP" argued for short-lived Diffie-Hellman keys that are thrown away, and for message authentication codes instead of signatures, so a transcript can't prove who said what.
5 April 2016: a billion peopleSignal announced that its protocol was fully integrated into WhatsApp: chats, group chats, attachments, voice notes and calls, for "over a billion monthly active users".
2023 to 2026: it keeps movingPost-quantum handshakes (Signal, 2023), a post-quantum ratchet (Signal, 2025; Apple's iMessage, 2024), a standard for big groups (MLS, 2023) and automatic key checks (Signal, August 2026).

Sources: Borisov, Goldberg, Brewer (2004) · Signal: WhatsApp integration complete · PQXDH · SPQR · Apple PQ3 · RFC 9420 · Signal: automatic key verification

~3 min

  • SayThree dates, one idea each. In 2004 researchers pointed out that an encryption key you keep for years is a single point of failure: leak it once and every old message opens. In 2016 the ideas had grown into a protocol that a billion people used without knowing it. And the work isn't finished: the same protocols are still being extended to survive quantum computers and to check that the server hasn't handed you the wrong key.
  • AskWhich apps on your phone do you think are end-to-end encrypted? Keep the answers; we come back to them on the last slide. (Answers vary; the point is that "encrypted" can mean very different things.)
  • CheckThe OTR claims are from the paper's own abstract and summaries (the PDF is the 2004 workshop paper; an earlier draft dates from 2002). The WhatsApp figures are from Signal's post of 5 April 2016, not from today's WhatsApp. Say "named examples, not recommendations" if asked about brands.

// STARTING A CONVERSATION 01

Recap: two people, one secret, in public

AliceAnyone listeningBob

  • Each person makes a secret number (the private key) and a matching public key, and sends only the public one.
  • Combine your private key with their public key and you both get the same shared secret. Someone who only sees the public keys can't compute it.
  • A shared secret isn't yet a message key: it goes through a key-derivation function (HKDF) first.
  • Two gaps remain: nothing says who is on the other end (slide 7), and both people had to be online (next slide).

REAL MATHS, TEACHING CODE X25519 as in RFC 7748 (the curve Signal's protocols use), HKDF-SHA-256 and AES-128-GCM, all running in this page. Real apps use audited libraries.

Sources: RFC 7748: X25519 · Double Ratchet spec (primitives) · Crypto 101: the key-swap idea

~5 min

  • ShowPress "Pick secrets": each side makes a private and public key. Press "Swap public keys": the middle column, "anyone listening", sees only public keys, but both ends compute the same shared secret (the matching row lights up). Press "Send a message": a message key comes out of the shared secret through HKDF, the message is encrypted with AES-GCM, and the listener sees only ciphertext. Press "Start over" and pick again: new keys, a different secret.
  • SayThis is the key-swap trick from Crypto 101, on a modern curve. The only thing that crosses the network is public. If you saw the earlier talk, this is a two-minute recap; if you didn't, the one-line version is that combining your private number with their public number gives you both the same secret, and getting a private number back from a public one is the hard problem.
  • AskIf the listener sees both public keys, why can't they compute the secret? Answer: they'd need one of the private keys, and recovering a private key from a public key is the discrete-logarithm problem.
  • CheckX25519 is verified against the RFC 7748 test vectors in this talk's own tests. The Double Ratchet specification recommends AES-256-based encryption; this deck uses AES-128-GCM because that's what the shared teaching code implements.

// STARTING A CONVERSATION 02

Bob is offline: prekeys, and X3DH


      

    

    

      
MixAlice computesBob computes later

      
    
        
  • The server only stores public keys: Bob's identity key, a signed prekey, and some one-time prekeys. It hands one out (and deletes it) for each new chat.
    • 
        
  • Two mixes prove who is who, two give forward secrecy. If the one-time prekeys run out, three mixes are used and a replayed first message could be accepted; the ratchet that follows limits the damage.
    • 
        
  • The signature on the signed prekey stops the server swapping it for its own.

Source: Signal: the X3DH key agreement protocol (revision 1, 2016)

    

  

  
~6 min
  • ShowPress 1: Bob's phone uploads a bundle (identity key, a signed prekey with its signature, three one-time prekeys) and goes offline. Press 2: Alice fetches the bundle, checks the signature, makes a throwaway ephemeral key, mixes four Diffie-Hellman results into a secret, and sends a first message that only Bob can open. Press 3: Bob comes back, repeats the same four mixes from his side, gets the same secret, and reads the message. The right-hand table shows both sides agreeing on every row. Then start over, untick "one-time prekeys left": only three mixes, and a note about replay. Start over again, tick "a dishonest server swaps in its own prekey": the signature check fails and Alice's app stops.
  • SayBob never had to be online. Everything Alice needed was public and waiting. The four mixes have jobs: two tie the secret to both identity keys (authentication), two bring in fresh keys (forward secrecy). The signature matters because the server is exactly the party who could cheat.
  • AskWhat could a dishonest server still do that the signature doesn't stop? Answer: give Alice a completely different identity key for Bob, signed by itself. That is why safety numbers (slide 7) exist.
  • CheckThe mixes follow the specification: DH1 = DH(IK_A, SPK_B), DH2 = DH(EK_A, IK_B), DH3 = DH(EK_A, SPK_B), DH4 = DH(EK_A, OPK_B), then a key-derivation step. The spec attributes mutual authentication to DH1 and DH2 and forward secrecy to DH3 and DH4, and lists replay without a one-time prekey as a known caveat. The signature scheme here is a teaching shortcut (see the callout).

// STARTING A CONVERSATION 03
Harvest now, decrypt later: add a second secret

  

    

      
Handshake

      

        
        
      

      

      

    

    

      
    
        
  • A recorded handshake can wait years in an archive. If a large quantum computer can one day undo the curve maths, today's recordings could be opened. That's "harvest now, decrypt later".
    • 
        
  • The fix: add one more secret from a post-quantum key-encapsulation mechanism (Kyber, standardised by NIST as ML-KEM) next to the curve secrets. An attacker has to break both. Signal added this to its handshake as PQXDH in September 2023.
    • 
        
  • Why not replace the curve? The new maths is younger. Keeping both means a flaw in either one alone isn't enough.
    • 
        
  • The limits, in Signal's own specification: authentication in PQXDH isn't quantum-secure, and it isn't designed to stop an attacker with a quantum computer today. It protects recorded traffic.
    • 
      

      
NOT A REAL KEM There is no Kyber or ML-KEM in this page. A random secret stands in for what a KEM would deliver, so you can see what the hybrid changes. The "quantum" step simply hands the attacker every private key behind the recorded public keys.

      
Sources: Signal: quantum resistance and the Signal Protocol · PQXDH specification (revision 3) · NIST FIPS 203 (ML-KEM)

    

  

  
~5 min
  • ShowLeave "Curve only" selected and press 1, then 2: the attacker rebuilds the secret from the recording and reads the message (real AES-GCM opens it). Now pick "Curve + post-quantum", press 1 and 2 again: the attacker gets every curve secret but is missing the extra one, so the tag check fails and nothing is read.
  • SayThe attack needs patience, not a quantum computer today: record everything now, wait. The defence has to be in place before the recording happens, which is why messengers added it to the handshake first. Notice what the demo does not claim: this protects recordings, not a live quantum attacker, and it doesn't fix who-is-on-the-other-end.
  • AskWhich data would you not want opened in 15 years? (Messages about health, legal matters, relationships, sources.)
  • CheckFrom the PQXDH specification (revision 3, 2023-05-24, updated 2024-01-23): it computes the key from the curve secrets plus the KEM secret; "Authentication in PQXDH is not quantum-secure" and "PQXDH is not designed to provide protection against active quantum attackers". The specification's example KEM is Crystals-Kyber-1024. NIST's FIPS 203 (ML-KEM) was published on 13 August 2024. The September 2023 date is from Signal's announcement post.

// STARTING A CONVERSATION 04
Safety numbers: check the keys, not the server

  

    

      
What's going on

      

      

      

    

    

      
    
        
  • The risk: a dishonest or hacked server could give Alice its own key in place of Bob's.
    • 
        
  • The check: each phone shows a fingerprint of both identity keys: 60 digits, in 12 groups of five. If both hold the right keys, both show the same number. Compare in person, on a call, or scan the QR code.
    • 
        
  • A changed number most often means a new phone or a reinstall. It's a prompt to check, not proof of an attack.
    • 
        
  • Newer: Signal's automatic key verification (August 2026) checks a public, audited key log, so you can catch a swapped key without meeting. It needs your contact's phone number and doesn't prove who controls an account. Apple's iMessage Contact Key Verification (announced October 2023) is a similar idea.
    • 
      

      
SIMPLIFIED Signal's real number comes from an iterated hash and account identifiers. This one uses SHA-256 and the same layout: two 30-digit halves, sorted so both phones agree.

      
Sources: Signal: safety number updates (2016) · Signal: automatic key verification (11 Aug 2026) · Apple: Contact Key Verification

    

  

  
~4 min

"Mallory swaps the keys": Mallory sits in the server's place and gives each side her own key; each phone now fingerprints a different pair of keys, the numbers differ (the differing groups turn red), and the verdict is red.   • SayEncryption is only as good as the key you encrypted to. A safety number checks, outside the server's reach, that you hold the keys the other person holds. Most people never compare, and that is okay for most chats; it's for the conversation that matters. A changed number isn't an alarm by itself: phones get replaced. What you do next depends on what's at stake.
  • AskWhy compare the numbers over a call or in person rather than in the same chat? Answer: a man in the middle could alter the chat too.
  • CheckSignal's 2016 post describes the number as a sorted concatenation of two 30-digit fingerprints, shown as 12 groups of five digits. // RATCHETS 01
Forward secrecy: hash forward, delete the key

  

    

      
Design

      

        
        
        
        
      

      
#Message keyAfter the theft

      

    

    

      
    
        
  • Forward secrecy: if today's keys leak, yesterday's messages stay unreadable.
    • 
        
  • How: a chain key makes each message key, and the next chain key: message key = HMAC(chain key, 0x01), next chain key = HMAC(chain key, 0x02). Then the old keys are deleted.
    • 
        
  • HMAC only runs one way: from today's chain key you can compute tomorrow's, never yesterday's.
    • 
        
  • But not enough alone: steal the chain key and you can follow the chain forward for ever. The next slide fixes that.
    • 
      

      
REAL HMAC, REAL AES-GCM The constants 0x01 and 0x02 are the ones in the Double Ratchet specification. "After the theft" is an actual attempt to decrypt with only the stolen chain key.

      
Source: Signal: the Double Ratchet algorithm (revision 4, 4 Nov 2025)

    

  

  
~7 min
  • ShowStart on "Hash chain". Three messages are already sent; press Send a couple more: each has its own key, and the key is struck out as "deleted" once used. Now press "Steal the keys now": the thief copies the current chain key. The three old messages say "can't read: key deleted". Send three more: each one is now read by the thief, because the chain just keeps hashing forward. Switch to "One key forever" and repeat: the thief reads everything, past and future.
  • SayThe ratchet is a one-way staircase. Each message key is used once and forgotten, so a leak only exposes what hasn't been deleted yet. That's forward secrecy: the past is safe. But look at the other half: with only a hash chain, once the thief has the key they stay in for ever. Good news and bad news: we need something that lets the conversation recover.
  • AskWhy can't the thief run the chain backwards? Answer: HMAC is a one-way function: you can't compute its input from its output.
  • CheckThe specification (revision 4, 2025-11-04) says to use HMAC-SHA-256 or SHA-512 with the chain key as the key, the single byte 0x01 to produce the message key and 0x02 to produce the next chain key. It defines "forward security" (output keys from the past appear random to an adversary who learns the KDF key) and "break-in recovery" for the other direction. This deck's message encryption (AES-128-GCM with a key and nonce derived by HKDF) is a simplification of the specification's recommended AES-256 constructions.

// RATCHETS 02
Healing: a fresh Diffie-Hellman with every reply

  

    

      

        
        
        
        
        
      

      

      
#FromRatchet keynThief

      

    

    

      
    
        
  • Each reply can carry a fresh public key. Mixing a new Diffie-Hellman result into the root key starts a new chain of message keys.
    • 
        
  • A thief who copied the keys falls behind: the next step uses a private key generated after the copy, which they never saw.
    • 
        
  • The thief gets a short extra: they can still read Alice's current chain, and Bob's next reply (they hold the key it's mixed with). After Alice replies on a new key, they're locked out.
    • 
        
  • The specification calls this "break-in recovery". Papers and Apple's PQ3 post call it post-compromise security.
    • 
      

      
REAL RATCHET The root key, chain keys and Diffie-Hellman steps follow the specification; the thief is a genuine attempt to decrypt using only the copied keys and what crosses the wire.

      
Source: Signal: the Double Ratchet algorithm · Apple: iMessage PQ3

    

  

  
~7 min
  • ShowAlice's first message is already sent. Press "Bob sends", then "Alice sends", a few turns each way: the "Ratchet key" column gets a new badge (A1, B1, A2…) whenever the sender's key changes, and the state cards show new keys appearing. Now press "Copy Alice's keys now". Send a message from Alice on her current key: the thief reads it. Press "Bob sends": the thief reads that too. Now press "Alice sends": her reply uses a brand-new key and the thief can't read it. Keep going: from now on every message is locked to them. The comment line says when the conversation healed.
  • SayThe trick is that every reply brings a new secret in: a Diffie-Hellman result the thief has no way to compute. It doesn't need the other person to do anything special; it falls out of replying. That's why the conversation heals itself, usually within a round trip or so of replies. The catch for security: if one side never replies, the healing doesn't happen, so a conversation that goes one way for a long time stays exposed longer.
  • AskWhat would the thief need to do to keep reading? Answer: copy the keys again after the next exchange, that is, stay compromised.
  • CheckThe specification's own term is "break-in recovery" (future output keys appear random to an adversary who learned the KDF key, as long as future inputs supply enough entropy); it doesn't define "post-compromise security" as a separate term. Apple's post uses "post-compromise security". The thief model here is deliberately simple: a copy of Alice's complete state, plus everything sent on the wire afterwards. Messages sent before the copy are not attempted because the keys were already deleted.

      // GROUPS, DEVICES, METADATA 01
      Group chats: pairwise, sender keys, or a tree
      
  
      
    
      
      
      Group size16
      
      
      DesignPer messageWhen someone leavesIn practice
      
      
      
      
      Per message = encryptions; when someone leaves = key messages to send. Counts are operation counts for this simple model (signatures, welcome messages and server work ignored), not benchmarks. Sources: Signal: private groups (2014) · libsignal: group_cipher.rs · Matrix: Megolm · RFC 9420: MLS · Rösler, Mainka, Schwenk · Balbás, Collins, Gajland (sender keys)
      
    
      
    
      
      
      Click a member: the path to the root is what changes (yellow). Blue nodes are who the new keys are encrypted to.
      
      
        
        
      • Pairwise: encrypt each message separately for every member. Simple, and cost grows with the group.
        • 
        
      • Sender keys: each member has one key for their own messages and hands it to everyone. One encryption per message, but handing out keys after a change is costly and healing is weak.
        •

      ~7 min
      • ShowSlide the group size from 2 to 1000. Pairwise: the cost of every message climbs with the group (n − 1 encryptions). Sender keys: one encryption per message, but the cost when someone leaves explodes (about n²). The tree: one encryption per message and about 10 steps to change a thousand-member group. Then click a member in the tree: the yellow path is the keys that change; the blue nodes are the siblings the new keys are encrypted to. Click another: a different path.
      • SayA one-to-one chat has one secret to look after; a group has many people sharing one conversation, members who come and go, and devices that go offline. Signal's original answer (2014) was simply to send a separate encrypted copy to each member. Sender keys, used by WhatsApp and also present in libsignal, make each message cheap but changing the group expensive. MLS, the IETF standard, uses a tree so that updates cost about log n: it's designed for groups "from two to thousands", with forward secrecy and post-compromise security.
      • AskSomeone leaves a group of 1,000. Why does the design matter? Answer: with sender keys everyone must replace their key and tell everyone else; with a tree, one update does it.
      • CheckThe pairwise-copy design is from Signal's 2014 post (it says "we simply deliver a pairwise encrypted message to each member of the group"). libsignal's group_cipher.rs contains a sender-key cipher (a chain key, a signing key, distribution messages); I did not find a Signal document describing which path today's apps use for groups. The sender-keys description (each member's sender key is sent to everyone over existing two-party channels; when someone leaves, everyone deletes their keys, generates fresh ones and starts again, which costs O(n²) communication; it "does not aim for strong PCS in groups") is from Balbás, Collins and Gajland's analysis (RECSI 2022 slides), which extracted the protocol from WhatsApp's whitepaper plus Signal's code; WhatsApp's own current PDF blocked automated reading, so confirm against it before quoting what WhatsApp does today. Matrix's Megolm specification itself says it gives "partial" forward secrecy, no backward secrecy on its own, and shares session keys over a secure channel such as Olm. The RFC 9420 abstract says MLS gives forward secrecy and post-compromise security for groups "from two to thousands" and that update costs scale as the log of the group size. The 2017 analysis (Rösler, Mainka, Schwenk; EuroS&P 2018) found that Signal's future secrecy didn't hold for groups and that message integrity and group management weren't protected end to end in the apps it studied: that was seven years ago and the apps have changed.
      • Next"And what about your laptop and your tablet?"
      

      


      
  
      // GROUPS, DEVICES, METADATA 02
      Several devices: one copy per device
      
  
      
    
      
      
      
        
        
        
        
        
      
      
      
      
      
      Encrypted forWhat goes out (real AES-GCM)
      
      
      
    
      
    
      
      
        
        
      • Encryption happens per device. A message "to Bob" is encrypted separately for each of Bob's devices, and Alice's app also sends a copy to her own other devices.
        • 
        
      • A new device starts empty: old messages were encrypted to the keys of the devices that existed then, so a newly linked device can't read them.
        • 
        
      • History comes from a transfer, not from the server: Signal's optional link-and-sync is end-to-end encrypted, and a linked device is another place your messages can be read.
        • 
        
      • Designs differ: the Sesame specification describes both one identity key per user and one per device.
        • 
      
      
      
      ILLUSTRATION Each device here has its own random session key; the copies are real AES-GCM ciphertexts, so they really differ. Real apps run a full Double Ratchet session per device pair.
      
      
      Sources: Signal: the Sesame algorithm (revision 2, 2017) · Signal: more linked devices (4 Aug 2026)
      
    
      
  
      
  
      ~5 min
      • ShowPress "Alice sends": the table lists one ciphertext per device (Bob's phone, plus Alice's own laptop). Press "Bob links a device": now one more copy. Press "Alice links a device": and another. Notice every copy is different, though the message is the same. Then watch the comment line: a freshly linked device has no history of earlier messages.
      • SayEnd-to-end encryption is between devices, not between people. That's why linking a laptop or tablet matters for security: each is another endpoint that holds keys and can read your chats. It's also why a new device doesn't see your history: nothing was ever encrypted to it. Apps offer an optional, encrypted transfer for that.
      • AskWhen did you last look at the list of devices linked to your messenger? (A good habit: unlink what you don't use.)
      • CheckThe Sesame specification (revision 2, 2017-04-14) says messages are encrypted separately for each recipient device, a device sends a copy of each outbound message to the user's other devices, and it describes both per-user and per-device identity keys as options. Signal's 4 August 2026 post says history transfer is optional (full history plus the last 45 days of saved media) and "fully end-to-end encrypted". Limits on the number of linked devices appear in Signal's support pages with inconsistent wording, so none is stated here.
      • Next"The one thing the encryption doesn't hide: who's talking to whom."
      

      


      
  
      // GROUPS, DEVICES, METADATA 03
      Metadata: who, when, how much
      
  
      
    
      
      
      The envelope
      
      
      The service seesSeen?
      
      
      
    
      
    
      
      
        
        
      • End-to-end hides the contents, not the delivery: the service still has to know where to send a message.
        • 
        
      • Sealed sender (Signal, 2018) puts the sender's identity inside the encrypted envelope, so the service doesn't see who it came from.
        • 
        
      • What's still visible: the recipient, the timing and the network address. Signal itself called this "an incremental step" and said timing and IP-address correlation were ongoing work.
        • 
        
      • You can shrink metadata, by choosing apps that keep less, but you can't remove it from a service that delivers your messages. The earlier talk, Privacy Tools Explained, has the full envelope demo.
        • 
      
      
      
      Sources: Signal: sealed sender (29 Oct 2018) · Signal: government requests
      
    
      
  
      
  
      ~3 min
      • ShowStart on "End-to-end": the contents are hidden, but the sender, the recipient, the time and the size are visible to the service. Switch to "sealed sender": the sender row flips to hidden, and the rest is unchanged.
      • SayMetadata is the data about the message. It can be revealing on its own: who you talk to, how often, at what hours. Sealed sender is one clever step to reduce it, but a service that delivers your messages always sees something. In 2016 a US grand jury subpoena asked Signal for information about two users; Signal could provide only when each account was created and when it last connected. That is what a service that keeps little looks like.
      • CheckThe 2018 post is a "technology preview"; the sealed-sender behaviour today may differ, so describe it as a design rather than a promise about any current app. The post says the service "doesn't authenticate" the envelope's sender, uses a short-lived sender certificate inside the encrypted envelope, and lists "traffic correlation via timing attacks and IP addresses" as ongoing development. The 2016 subpoena is from Signal's own government-requests page.

      // CHECKPOINT 3 · 5 MIN
      Pause and discuss
      
  
      
    
      Talk with a neighbour for a couple of minutes, then we share answers.
      
    
        
      
      1. Why are group chats harder to secure than one-to-one chats?
        Hint
        Think about who holds which key, and what happens when someone leaves.
        Answer
        Many people share one conversation, members come and go, and every device's keys must stay in step. Pairwise copies get costly for big groups, sender keys make a departure expensive and heal slowly, and a key tree such as MLS aims to keep each change to about log n steps.
        2. 
      
      2. You link a new device to your account. Can it read your old chats?
        Hint
        Who were the old messages encrypted to?
        Answer
        Not by default: they were encrypted to the keys of your other devices. Some apps offer an optional, encrypted history transfer between your devices.
        3. 
      
      3. Sealed sender hides who sent a message from the server. What can the server still see?
        Hint
        What does it need to know to deliver the message?
        Answer
        Who it's for, when it arrived, roughly how big it is, and the network address it came from. Signal itself described timing and IP-address correlation as ongoing work.
        4.

      // WRAP-UP 01
      What end-to-end encryption doesn't protect
      
  
      
    
      
      
      
      
      
      
      
    
      
    
      
      
        
        
      • It protects the middle of the journey: the network and the company's servers.
        • 
        
      • The ends are up to you: an unlocked phone, a backup, a screenshot, a linked laptop, a notification on a lock screen.
        • 
        
      • And the person you're talking to: they hold the plaintext and always can copy it. "Disappearing" is good manners, not enforcement.
        • 
      
      
      
      Sources: CISA: mobile communications best practices · Signal: disappearing messages · Signal: secure backups, a year in review
      
    
      
  
      
  
      ~3 min
      • ShowClick each place in turn. For each, the card says what encryption does there, what it doesn't, and one thing to do about it.
      • SayEverything in this talk protects a message while it travels. Once it arrives it's text on a screen, in an app, perhaps in a backup, with a person who can show it to anyone. The most common ways private chats actually leak are at the ends, not in the middle. That isn't a flaw in the protocols; it's the edge of what protocols can do.
      • AskWhich of these places is the weakest link in your own setup? (Often: backups, lock-screen previews, or an old linked device.)
      • CheckThe Signal post on disappearing messages (11 October 2016) says the timer starts once each recipient has read a message, that it's for conversations where "all participants want to automate minimalist data hygiene, not for situations where your contact is your adversary", and that a recipient "can always use another camera to take a photo of the screen". CISA's December 2024 guidance names end-to-end encrypted messaging for highly targeted people. Signal's September 2026 post describes Secure Backups as end-to-end encrypted with a recovery key you hold. Other apps' backup behaviour varies: see the earlier talk on privacy tools.
