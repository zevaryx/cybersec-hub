// WHY THIS TALK

Accounts fail in ordinary ways

None of these needed a clever attack: a password written where it shouldn't be, logins with no second factor, reused passwords tried at scale.

Twitter, 2018 · passwords in a logA bug wrote passwords to an internal log before hashing finished. Twitter saw no sign anyone outside could read it, and advised all users to change theirs.
Facebook, 2019 · readable passwordsSome passwords sat in a readable format in internal systems. Facebook said none were visible outside the company and found no evidence of abuse; hundreds of millions of Lite users were expected to be notified.
23andMe, 2023 · credential stuffingPasswords reused from other breaches, April to September 2023. The UK regulator fined the company £2.31m (155,592 UK residents) and cited no mandatory MFA, among other gaps.
Snowflake customers, 2024 · no second factorAbout 165 organisations were told they might be exposed. Mandiant: stolen logins, mostly from infostealer malware, on accounts without MFA, some unchanged for up to four years.

Sources: Meta, 21 Mar 2019 · The Register, 3 May 2018 · UK ICO · Mandiant

~4 min

  • SayFour stories, four lessons, none of them exotic. Passwords found in places that were never the database. Logins that needed only a password. Accounts opened with passwords from other people's breaches. Everything else today is about making those stories boring.
  • TipTwitter: The Register (3 May 2018) quotes the CTO, Parag Agrawal, saying a bug logged passwords "before completing the hashing process", to about 330 million users, with no sign anyone outside the company could view the file; I read it through press coverage because Twitter's own post returned a 403 to my fetch. Facebook: the 21 March 2019 post said "hundreds of millions of Facebook Lite users, tens of millions of other Facebook users, and tens of thousands of Instagram users", and an update of 18 April 2019 raised Instagram to millions. 23andMe: the ICO's notice describes credential stuffing from April to September 2023, 155,592 UK residents, and says the company lacked mandatory MFA, strong password requirements and unpredictable usernames. // SHOULD YOU BUILD IT? 01

Buy, borrow or build

OptionGood when
Hosted identity providere.g. Auth0, Amazon Cognito, Firebase Authentication, Microsoft Entra External IDLogin isn't what makes your product different, and you want MFA, passkeys and recovery without writing them
Self-hosted identity servere.g. Keycloak, OryYou need control of the data, or can't send logins to a third party, and you can run and patch it
Your framework's auth librarye.g. Django, Rails, ASP.NET Core Identity, Spring SecurityOne app, a standard login, and a mature library that already hashes, rotates sessions and resets passwords
From scratchRarely. Only when login is the product, and then with specialists and a review

RULE OF THUMB Don't invent a login protocol or a password scheme. Use OpenID Connect or OAuth 2.0 and your library's hash function.

You still own, whatever you pick

  • Authorization: who may do what, on every request.
  • Configuration: MFA on, weak options off, sensible session lifetimes.
  • Recovery policy: what happens when someone loses their device.
  • Logging: failed logins, resets and MFA changes, watched by someone.
  • Deletion and data: what you keep about people, and for how long.

Product names are examples, not endorsements. See OWASP Authentication.

~5 min

  • SayThe safest login code is the code a team with a security budget already wrote and keeps patching. A provider or a mature framework library gets you hashing, sessions, resets, MFA and passkeys, and a team that responds to advisories. It doesn't get you authorization or a sensible policy: those stay yours. Build from scratch only if login is your product.
  • AskWho has written their own sign-up form, hand up. Who has regretted it later? Answer: usually in the same sentence, around password reset.
  • TipThis is a teaching table, not a purchasing guide: price, data location, lock-in and outage behaviour all matter, and I haven't compared the vendors.

// SHOULD YOU BUILD IT? 02

Seven parts, and the attacker picks the weakest

1 · Sign-upCan I register as someone else, or learn who is already registered?
2 · LoginCan I guess, stuff or phish my way in?
3 · SessionsCan I steal, fix or keep a session alive for ever?
4 · Second factorsCan I relay, reuse or skip the code?
5 · RecoveryCan I take the account by "forgetting" the password?
6 · ChangesCan I swap the email or phone, then reset?
7 · DeletionCan I be sure it's gone, and that nobody else's data went with it?
The weakest linkA strong password and a passkey don't help if recovery is "answer a security question".

Think of each part as a door. Good locks on one of them don't matter if another opens with a stranger's word.

~4 min

  • SayMost teams spend their time on the login form. Attackers spend theirs on whichever door is cheapest, and that's very often recovery or a change flow. For each part, I'll show the question an attacker asks of it. We'll visit every one in the next hour.
  • AskWhich of the seven does your own project handle worst today? Answer: there isn't a right one, but "deletion" and "changes" are the commonest honest answers.

// PASSWORDS AND SESSIONS 01

Storing passwords: a slow, salted hash, through a library

AlgorithmOWASP's minimum settings
Argon2id first choice19 MiB of memory, 2 iterations, 1 thread (or an equivalent trade: 46 MiB and 1 iteration)
scryptN = 217 (128 MiB), r = 8, p = 1
bcrypt older systemswork factor 10 or more; only the first 72 bytes count
PBKDF2 when FIPS is required600,000 iterations with HMAC-SHA-256
 
stored in one column:
$argon2id$v=19$m=19456,t=2,p=1$c29tZXNhbHQ$aGFzaGhlcmU…
algorithm · settings · random salt · the hash
  • Call the library's hash and verify. It makes the salt and writes the settings into the string.
  • Because the settings travel with the hash, you can raise them later and spot the old ones.
  • Never a fast hash (SHA-256, MD5), never your own scheme: speed helps the guesser.
  • A pepper kept in a vault or HSM adds depth, not a substitute.
  • Mind the 72-byte limit if you must use bcrypt.

Why slow? See Crypto 101 and Passwords and Accounts.

Sources: OWASP Password Storage · NIST SP 800-63B-4

~4 min

  • SayA stolen password table should cost the thief years, not hours. That's what a slow, memory-hungry, salted hash buys. The settings are a floor from OWASP's cheat sheet: tune them on your own servers so a login takes a noticeable fraction of a second, and revisit them every couple of years.
  • ShowThe stored string: algorithm, settings, salt and hash all in one value. That's the PHC format, and it's why upgrades are easy: the record says how it was made.
  • TipFacts from OWASP's Password Storage cheat sheet as I read it on 11 November 2026: Argon2id minimum m=19456 KiB, t=2, p=1 (equivalent: 46 MiB with t=1, 12 MiB with t=3, 9 MiB with t=4, 7 MiB with t=5); scrypt N=2^17, r=8, p=1; bcrypt cost at least 10 and a 72-byte limit (OWASP calls pre-hashing risky); PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256 600,000. NIST SP 800-63B-4 asks for a salt of at least 32 bits and suggests a keyed hash step, with the key in a hardware-protected area.

Rehash on login: upgrade hashes as people sign in

UserStored hashStatus
 

    

    

      
    
        
  • On a correct login you briefly hold the plaintext: check the record, and if it's out of date, store a fresh Argon2id hash.

    •

~6 min
  • ShowFive accounts: two with fast unsalted hashes, one with Argon2id at old, weak settings, one with current settings, and Eve, who hasn't been back in years. The passwords are filled in for you (they're demo passwords). Log Alice in with a wrong password first: nothing changes. Then the right one: the row turns into a current Argon2id hash. Dave needs nothing. "Everyone logs in" runs them all. Then "Expire what's left" deals with Eve.
  • SayYou can't convert a hash into a stronger hash without the password, and you only have the password at the moment someone types it. So do the upgrade right there. The same trick raises Argon2id settings later. For the people who never come back, you have two honest options: expire the hash and force a reset, or wrap it (for example bcrypt of the old hash) and replace it on next login. Wrapping is quicker but the result is easier to crack than a direct hash.
  • TipOWASP's cheat sheet describes exactly these two options for legacy hashes (expire and delete, or wrap), and recommends storing the algorithm and work factor with each hash. The "known user takes the same time as an unknown one" detail in the login function comes back in the enumeration slide.

    // PASSWORDS AND SESSIONS 05
    Rotate, expire, revoke
    
  
    
    
    
      
    
        
        
      
    
      
    1 · Session fixation
    
      
    
        
        
        
      
    
      
    2 · Lifetimes and revocation
    
      
    
        
        
        
        
        
        
      
    
      
    
      
    
    
    
    
    
      
      
        
    • Issue a new ID at login and after any privilege change. Kill the old one.
      • 
        
    • Enforce idle and absolute timeouts on the server: a client timer can be skipped.
      • 
        
    • Sign-out must end the session on the server; deleting the cookie isn't enough.
      •

      • 
        
    • Store a hash of the ID, not the ID: a leaked table can't be used as cookies.
      •

    ~6 min
    • ShowWith "New session ID at login" on: plant an ID, let the victim log in, then try the attacker's ID: it's dead. Untick the box and repeat: the attacker now holds a logged-in session. Then the lifetimes: sign in on both devices, move the clock 31 minutes (idle expiry), then 9 hours (absolute expiry). Sign in again and press "Change password": the other device is signed out, and so is the old session on this one. "Sign out everywhere" ends the rest.
    • SayFixation is old and still found in custom code: the attacker gets the victim to arrive with an ID the attacker already knows, then waits for the victim to log in. One line of code stops it: a fresh ID when the privilege level changes. And a session that can't be revoked is a liability: build "sign out everywhere" and make password change use it.
    • TipOWASP: regenerate the ID after login and any privilege change and destroy the old one; idle timeouts commonly 2–5 minutes for high-value apps and 15–30 minutes for low-risk ones; absolute timeouts such as 4–8 hours for a full-day office application. Storing only a hash of the ID is common practice and my recommendation, not something I found in OWASP's text.

    • ShowPress "App refreshes": each refresh gives a new token and retires the old one.     TOTP: the server's side
    
  
    
    
    
      
    
        
        
      
    
      
    Your authenticator app shows------
    
      
    
        
        
      
    
      
    Press Verify.
    
      
    
    
    
    
    
      
      
        
    • A code comes from a shared secret and the time (30-second steps).
      • 
        
    • Accept one step either side: phone clocks drift. Wider windows help guessers.
      •

      •

    • ShowThe phone shows the code for its own clock. Press "Type it in", then Verify: accepted. Press Verify again with the same code: rejected as already used.     • SayThis is the verifier from the Passwords and Accounts talk, now seen from the server. Three decisions are yours: how much drift to tolerate (RFC 6238 recommends allowing at most one step for network delay), how to stop a code being used twice (RFC 6238: the verifier must not accept a second attempt of the same code), and how to keep the secret. The secret is a key, not a password: you need the original to compute codes, so you can't hash it. Encrypt it, keep the key out of the database, and apply the same care as for any key.
    • AskAn attacker watches you type your code and types it in a second later. Does one-use stop them? Answer: only if you got there first. A real-time phishing site relays the code immediately, so one-use doesn't save you from that: it's why passkeys are better.
    • TipRFC 6238 §5.2: "at most one time step is allowed as the network delay", 30 seconds is the recommended time step, and "the verifier MUST NOT accept the second attempt of the OTP". The RFC's resynchronisation example allows drift of a couple of steps; the demo's ±1 matches the network-delay advice. The demo checks every slot rather than stopping at the first match, and compares byte by byte. The demo's TOTP maths matches RFC 6238's SHA-1 test vectors (the Node tests check them).
    • CheckSecret length: RFC 4226 asks for at least 128 bits and recommends 160; I did not re-read RFC 4226 and the page I read for RFC 6238 only says keys should be as long as the HMAC output. The demo secret is 160 bits.

    Passkeys: what the server sends, receives and keeps
    
  
    
    
    
      
    
        
        
        
        
        
        
      
    
      
    
    
    
    
    
      
      
        
    • Server stores a credential ID, a public key, a counter and flags. Nothing secret.
      • 
        
    • Login = sign a fresh challenge; server checks origin, RP ID, flags, signature.
      • 
        
    • The RP ID (your domain) is bound in: a look-alike site gets nothing signed.
      • 
        
    • Use a maintained WebAuthn library for the checks.
      • 
      
    
      
    This is a simulation with real P-256 signatures. Try a real passkey on the Crypto 101 page.
    
      
    Source: W3C Web Authentication Level 3 (sections 7.1, 7.2)
    
    
    
  
    
  
    ~8 min
    • ShowPress 1: the authenticator makes a key pair, and the server keeps only the public half, a credential ID, a counter and the hash of your RP ID. Press 2: the server sends a random challenge, the device signs it together with the origin and RP ID, and the server runs its checklist, line by line. "Replay a captured login" sends the same response again: the challenge is already spent. "Look-alike site asks": the browser won't ask the authenticator for your site's key on another site, so nothing is signed. "A copied key signs": its counter isn't above the stored one, which is a signal.
    • SayA passkey is a public-key signature in the same shape as Crypto 101's signatures. The server never holds anything that could log in as the user, so a database leak is nearly useless to attackers. And because the browser fills in the site's name, a convincing fake can't get a signature for the real one: that's what "phishing-resistant" means. NIST SP 800-63B-4 requires verifiers at AAL2 to offer a phishing-resistant option.
    • TipWebAuthn §7.1 (registration) and §7.2 (assertion) steps used here: type, challenge match, origin match, rpIdHash equals the SHA-256 of the RP ID, user-present flag, user-verified flag if required, signature over authData plus the SHA-256 of clientDataJSON, signCount stored and compared. RP ID: by default equal to the origin's effective domain, or a registrable domain suffix of it. The spec's signature-counter text says a counter that doesn't increase means "a cloned authenticator may exist, or the authenticator may be malfunctioning", and many synced passkeys always report 0, so don't treat 0 as a clone. The demo is simplified: no attestation, no attested credential data, no COSE encoding, and a P-256 key in a page (not a hardware authenticator).

    // SECOND FACTORS AND PASSKEYS 03
    Offering passkeys alongside passwords
    
  
    
    
    
      
      
        
    • Offer, don't force: add passkeys next to passwords, and let people choose.
      • 
        
    • Prompt after a successful login: "Create a passkey for faster sign-in?"
      • 
        
    • Allow several per account, with names ("work laptop"), so one lost device isn't a lockout.
      • 
        
    • Offer passkey autofill in the username field.
      • 
        
    • Synced or device-bound? The backup flags tell you which.
      • 
        
    • Keep a recovery path as strong as the thing it replaces.
      • 
      
    
    
    
    
    
      
    Why botherPasskeys can't be phished, can't be reused across sites, and leave nothing in your database worth stealing.
    
      
    What the numbers sayMicrosoft reported in May 2025 that sign-in with a passkey succeeded about 98% of the time against 32% for a password, and was about eight times faster than a password plus a second factor.
    
      
    The trapAdding a passkey but leaving "forgot password by email" as the only recovery: the attacker just goes round the front door.
    
      
    Sources: Microsoft, 1 May 2025 · NIST SP 800-63B-4 · WebAuthn L3
    
    
    
  
    
  
    ~4 min
    • SayYou don't have to choose between passwords and passkeys on day one. Add passkeys as an option, nudge people to create one after they've signed in, and let them keep more than one. The one trap is recovery: your account is as strong as its easiest way back in.
    • TipMicrosoft's figures (nearly a million passkeys registered a day, 98% vs 32% success, eight times faster than password plus MFA) are Microsoft's own, from its 1 May 2025 post, and describe its own users; they aren't an industry average. WebAuthn L3 defines the backup-eligible (BE) and backup-state (BS) flags: a multi-device credential is synced; a device-bound one isn't. NIST SP 800-63B-4: syncable authenticators can't be used at AAL3 because their keys are exportable.
    • CheckPasskey autofill ("conditional UI") and the autocomplete token for it: browser and library support varies by stack; check your platform's current docs. I did not verify library names or versions, so none are named on the slide: choose a maintained one for your language and read its release notes.
    • NextThen checkpoint 2.
    

    


    
  
    // CHECKPOINT 2 · 5 MIN
    Pause and discuss
    
  
    
    
    Talk with a neighbour for a couple of minutes, then we share answers.
    
    
      
      
    1. You store TOTP secrets in your user table. Why can't you hash them like passwords, and what do you do instead?
      Hint
      What does the server need to compute a code?
      Answer
      The server must compute the same code the app does, so it needs the original secret. Encrypt the secrets with a key held outside the database (a key service or secrets manager), and treat that key with care.
      2. 
      
    2. Why can't a passkey be phished the way a password or a TOTP code can?
      Hint
      Who decides which website the signature is for?
      Answer
      The browser binds the site's name (RP ID and origin) into what the device signs, and the authenticator only offers keys registered for that site. A look-alike site can't get a signature that the real site will accept, and nothing reusable is typed.
      3. 
      
    3. Your server sees a passkey login whose signature counter is lower than the stored one. What do you do?
      Hint
      Is it always an attack?
      Answer
      It may mean a cloned key or a fault, so treat it as a risk signal: log it, maybe ask for another factor. Many synced passkeys always report 0, so a counter of 0 on both sides is normal. Decide the policy for your own risk.
      4. 
    
    
  
    
  
    5 min
    • ShowPairs talk for 2 minutes, then share. The timer starts by itself.
    

    



    
  
    // RECOVERY AND ABUSE 01
    Reset links: random, hashed, single-use, short-lived
    
  
    
    
    
      
    
        
        
        
        
        
      
    
      
    
        
    Email sent to the user
    (nothing yet)
    
        
    What the database keeps
    (nothing yet)
    
      
    
      
    Press "Request a reset".
    
      
    
    
    
    
    
      
      
        
    • Long and random from a CSPRNG, tied to one account.
      • 
        
    • Store only a hash: a leaked table can't be used as links.
      • 
        
    • Single use and short-lived; a newer request replaces the old.
      • 
        
    • Same response whether or not the account exists. Don't build the link from the Host header.
      • 
        
    • On use: set the password, end other sessions, email the user, don't sign them in automatically.
      • 
        
    • Rate-limit per account, but don't lock the account because of reset requests.
      • 
        
    • Set Referrer-Policy: no-referrer on the reset page.
      • 
      
    
      
    Source: OWASP Forgot Password. The 30-minute lifetime and 3-an-hour limit are this demo's choices.
    
    
    
  
    
  
    ~6 min
    • ShowRequest a reset: the email contains the real link token, the database keeps only its SHA-256. Click the link: it works once. Click again: rejected. Request again, move the clock 31 minutes, click: expired. Request a few more times and watch the log: the user always sees the same message, but the server quietly stops sending after the third in an hour.
    • SayA reset link is a password that's valid for a short time and sent over email. Treat it like one: random, stored hashed, single use, with a short life. The question on the checkpoint is what an attacker can do with a link that never expires: use it whenever they get hold of an old email, a forwarded message, or a browser history entry.
    • TipOWASP's Forgot Password cheat sheet as I read it: identical responses and timing for existing and non-existing accounts; tokens from a CSPRNG, "long enough to protect against brute-force attacks", single use, expiring "after an appropriate period"; per-account rate limiting; no-referrer on the reset page; build reset URLs from a trusted value, not the Host header; don't log the user in automatically; send a notification; let the user end other sessions, or end them automatically; don't lock accounts in response to forgotten-password activity. It gives no token length, no lifetime and, in the page I read, no explicit "hash the token" rule: hashing is my recommendation, in the same spirit as the password storage cheat sheet.
    • CheckPick your own lifetime: 30 minutes is a demo value, not a standard.
    • Next"Reset links need email. What if email is the problem?"
    

    


    
  
    // RECOVERY AND ABUSE 02
    Recovery codes, and recovery as the weakest link
    
  
    
    
    
      
    
        
        
        
      
    
      
    Press "Make 10 codes".
    
      
    
    
    
    
    
      
      
        
    • Show the codes once; the server keeps hashes, each single-use.
      • 
        
    • Make them long and random (this demo: 16 characters), so a fast hash is fine.
      • 
        
    • Using one should alert the user, and offer to make new ones.
      • 
        
    • Recovery must be as strong as login. An easier way back in is the real login.
      • 
        
    • Avoid security questions and a help desk that "just resets it" on a phone call.
      • 
      
    
      
    RULE Every way back into an account is a way into it. List them all and make the weakest one stronger.
    
    
    
  
    
  
    ~5 min
    • ShowMake ten codes: they're random, shown once. Use one: it works, and the count drops. Use it again: rejected. The database holds only hashes. Try a made-up code.
    • SayWhen a user loses their device, you need a way back that you'd trust as much as the login. Recovery codes are one: printed or stored in a manager when the second factor is set up. The wider point is to list every recovery route (email, SMS, codes, support) and notice that the account is exactly as strong as the weakest.
    • TipNIST SP 800-63B-4 says verifiers must not prompt for knowledge-based authentication or security questions when choosing passwords, and sends account recovery to its section 4.2, which I did not read. OWASP's Authentication cheat sheet says not to use security questions for re-authentication. The 16-character length and the "fast hash is fine for long random codes" claim are my own reasoning (80 random bits can't be guessed through a rate-limited login, and a thief with the table would need to search 2^80 values), not a figure from either source.
    • CheckSection 4.2 of NIST SP 800-63B-4 (account recovery): not read for this talk. Read it before telling people what the standard requires.

    Changing an email or phone: ask, notify, confirm
    
  
    
    
    The safe order of events
      
    Signed in?valid sessionRe-authenticatepassword or passkeySave as pendingold address stays activeNotify the old address"someone asked to change it"Confirm the new addresstime-limited linkSwitchand rotate sessions
    
    
    
    
    
      
    The attack this blocksSomeone with a stolen session changes the email to their own, then uses "forgot password" to take the account for good. Every step above slows or reveals that.
    
      
    Same for everything sensitivePassword change, phone number, MFA removal, new passkeys: ask for a fresh credential first, tell the user, and rotate sessions afterwards.
    
    
    
    
    Source: OWASP Authentication, "Changing a registered email address"
    
  
    
  
    ~3 min
    • SayThis is OWASP's recommended flow: a valid session, then identity verification (MFA if enabled, otherwise the current password), the new address stored as a pending change, a notification-only message to the current address, and a confirmation message to the proposed address before it takes effect, each with time-limited, single-purpose links. The old address getting a heads-up is what lets a real owner react.
    • AskWhat other settings turn an account over to someone else? Answer: phone number for SMS codes, recovery email, adding a passkey or an MFA device, removing MFA, API keys.
    • Next"Now the attacker who isn't targeting one person at all."
    

    


    
  
    // RECOVERY AND ABUSE 04
    Credential stuffing: layers, and what each costs
    
  
    
    
    
      
    
        
        
      
    
      
    
        
        
        
      
    
      
    
      
    
      
    
    
    
    
    
      
      
        
    • MFA is the strongest single control. It's also the one attackers can't route around with more addresses.
      • 
        
    • Check passwords against breaches at sign-up and at login.
      • 
        
    • Per-address limits fail against many addresses; per-account limits fail against one try each.
      • 
        
    • Prefer throttling and step-up to lockout, which lets an attacker lock others out.
      • 
        
    • Use unpredictable usernames where you can: lists of bare emails are easy to build.
      • 
        
    • Log and alert on the failure rate. Defences work in layers.
      • 
      
    
      
    Sources: OWASP Credential Stuffing Prevention · OWASP Authentication. The model's numbers are invented.
    
    
    
  
    
  
    ~7 min
    • ShowAn attacker holds 2,000 stolen email and password pairs and tries each once, spread over some number of addresses. Start with no defences and 500 addresses: every valid pair gets in. Turn on the per-address limit: nothing changes, because each address only tries a few times. Drop the attacker to one address: the limit works. Now turn on breached-password blocking, then MFA at 90%, then step-up on new devices, and watch the takeovers fall, and the friction for honest users rise.
    • SayThis is a toy model, so the numbers are invented: 20% of the stolen pairs are still valid, 85% of those are in a breach list you can check, honest users are interrupted at made-up rates. What it shows is the shape: the defences that look obvious (blocking addresses, locking accounts) lose to spreading the attack out, while checking breached passwords and requiring MFA go straight to the cause, which is password reuse.
    • TipOWASP's Credential Stuffing Prevention cheat sheet as I read it: MFA is "by far the best defense against the majority of password-related attacks"; CAPTCHAs aren't perfect; device fingerprints are client-supplied and spoofable; blocking IPs alone is weak against proxy networks, so use a graduated response; check new passwords against breached-password datasets; require unpredictable usernames; each defence should emit metrics. The Authentication cheat sheet adds: count failures per account, not per IP, and take care that lockout can't be used for denial of service. That page also quotes Microsoft's analysis that MFA "would have stopped 99.9% of account compromises": I didn't trace that figure, so it isn't on the slide.
    • CheckThe model is mine, not from any source: say so out loud.
    • Next"One last leak, in the words your login page uses."
    

    


    
  
    // RECOVERY AND ABUSE 05
    Account enumeration: one answer for every failure
    
  
    
    
    
      
    
        
        
      
    
      
    
        
        
        
        
      
    
      
    
      
    
    
    
    
    
      
      
        
    • Say "Invalid email or password" for every failure: no account, wrong password, locked, disabled.
      • 
        
    • Do the same work: hash a dummy password when the account is missing, or response time gives it away.
      • 
        
    • Keep status codes and page shapes the same too.
      • 
        
    • Reset: "If that account exists, we've emailed it." Sign-up: don't say "already registered": send that person an email instead.
      • 
      
    
      
    Some products must say if a name is taken. Then rate-limit it hard and accept that it's a trade.
    
      
    Source: OWASP Authentication. Timings are real, from Argon2id in your browser.
    
    
    
  
    
  
    ~5 min
    • ShowStart on "Helpful messages". Send with nobody@example.test: "No account with that email", and it's fast, because the server never hashes anything. Change it to alice@example.test: "Incorrect password", slower. Probe six names and the list reveals which exist. Switch to "One answer, same work" and probe again: every reply is the same, and the times line up.
    • AskWhich of the two messages leaks? Answer: both help the attacker: the first tells them the account doesn't exist, the second that it does. Also the time: skipping the hash for unknown names makes the unknown ones faster.
    • SayEnumeration turns "try everything on everyone" into "try only on real accounts", and it leaks who uses your service, which can be private by itself. The fix is boring: one message, one status code, the same amount of work. The same idea applies to reset and sign-up, where the leak hides in a different sentence.
    • TipOWASP's Authentication cheat sheet: respond with a generic message whatever the cause, equalise processing time (run the hash lookup on every attempt, even for a missing user), keep status codes consistent, and treat registration and reset the same way. The timing numbers on the slide come from your own browser, so they vary; the gap is the point, not the figure.

    // CHECKPOINT 3 · 5 MIN
    Pause and discuss
    
  
    
    
    Talk with a neighbour for a couple of minutes, then we share answers.
    
    
      
      
    1. Why should "wrong password" and "no such user" look the same?
      Hint
      What can an attacker do with a list of real accounts?
      Answer
      Different answers let an attacker find which accounts exist, so they only attack real ones, and learn who uses your service. Use one message, one status code and the same work (including time), for login, reset and sign-up.
      2. 
      
    2. What can an attacker do with a password-reset link that never expires?
      Hint
      Think about old emails and forwarded messages.
      Answer
      Use it any time they get hold of it: an old inbox, a forward, a browser history entry or a log. Make links single-use and short-lived, store only their hash, and replace the old one when a new one is sent.
      3. 
      
    3. You lock an account after five wrong passwords. How could an attacker use that against you?
      Hint
      They don't need the right password to cause trouble.
      Answer
      By failing on purpose they can lock real users out. Prefer throttling and step-up checks, count failures per account, give locked users a way back, and don't lock accounts over reset requests.
